    0Unit map, notation & maths warm-up

    The unit has three blocks.

    1. Describing two RVs together. We start from the joint CDF. From it come the joint PDF/PMF, the marginals (the distribution of each RV on its own), independence, and conditional densities.
    2. Summarising how they are related, using a few numbers. These are correlation, covariance and the correlation coefficient. Then comes the special case where everything is Gaussian, in which those few numbers tell you everything.
    3. Making new RVs from old ones. First $Y=g(X)$, then $Z=g(X,Y)$. The block ends with the most important transformation of all: the sum of many RVs. That leads to the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem.

    Maths warm-up

    Almost every calculation in this unit uses one of the six tools below. If double integrals feel shaky, work through this part carefully. Every later section depends on it.

    (a) Double integrals over a region

    $\displaystyle\iint_R g(\alpha,\beta)\,d\alpha\,d\beta$ means "add up $g$ over every point of the region $R$". If $g$ is a density, this sum is the volume under the surface $g$ above $R$. You never compute it in one go. You do it as two ordinary integrals, one inside the other:

    • the inner integral adds up along one thin strip of the region;
    • the outer integral adds up all the strips.

    The only hard part is the limits, and you get those from a picture. Here is the triangle $R=\{0\le\beta\le\alpha\le1\}$ cut into strips two different ways:

    Vertical strips (left). Freeze α at some value between 0 and 1. The vertical line at that α enters the triangle at the bottom edge $\beta=0$. It leaves at the slanted edge $\beta=\alpha$. So the inner integral runs over β from 0 to α. Then α itself sweeps from 0 to 1:

    $$\iint_R g\;=\;\int_{\alpha=0}^{1}\left[\int_{\beta=0}^{\alpha} g(\alpha,\beta)\,d\beta\right]d\alpha .$$

    Horizontal strips (right). Freeze β instead. The horizontal line at height β enters the triangle at the slanted edge, where $\alpha=\beta$, and leaves at the right edge $\alpha=1$. So α runs from β to 1, and then β sweeps from 0 to 1:

    $$\iint_R g\;=\;\int_{\beta=0}^{1}\left[\int_{\alpha=\beta}^{1} g(\alpha,\beta)\,d\alpha\right]d\beta .$$

    Both give the same number. Pick whichever makes the inner integral easier.

    EasyWarm-up example: both orders give the same answer

    Compute $\displaystyle\iint_R 6\alpha\,d\alpha\,d\beta$ over the triangle above, both ways.

    Solution

    Vertical strips. The inner variable is β, so treat α as a constant:

    $$\begin{aligned} \int_0^1\left[\int_0^{\alpha}6\alpha\,d\beta\right]d\alpha &=\int_0^1 6\alpha\Big[\beta\Big]_0^{\alpha}\,d\alpha &&\why{α is a constant inside}\\ &=\int_0^1 6\alpha^2\,d\alpha\\ &=\Big[2\alpha^3\Big]_0^1\\ &=2 . \end{aligned}$$

    Horizontal strips. The inner variable is α:

    $$\begin{aligned} \int_0^1\left[\int_\beta^{1}6\alpha\,d\alpha\right]d\beta &=\int_0^1\Big[3\alpha^2\Big]_\beta^{1}\,d\beta\\ &=\int_0^1\big(3-3\beta^2\big)\,d\beta\\ &=\Big[3\beta-\beta^3\Big]_0^1\\ &=2 . \end{aligned}$$

    Same answer, as promised. Notice how the limits differ: in the second version the inner limit is $\beta$ to $1$, not $0$ to $\alpha$. Getting this right is 80% of every joint-PDF question.

    Recipe: limits of a double integral
    1. Sketch the region. In probability questions this means: the support of the PDF (where it is non-zero) intersected with the event you want.
    2. Choose the outer variable. Its limits are plain numbers: the full extent of the region in that direction.
    3. Freeze the outer variable at a typical value. Draw the strip across the region. Read off where the strip enters and leaves: those curves are the inner limits.
    4. Inner limits may depend on the outer variable. Outer limits must never depend on anything.
    5. If the strip's entry or exit curve changes partway across the region, split the region into pieces and add.

    1. Freeze the outer variable at a typical value, e.g. $x=0.6$, and draw that vertical line.
    2. Walk up the line from the bottom. Where do you enter the shaded region? That curve (written as $y=\dots$) is the lower limit. Where do you leave it? That is the upper limit.
    3. Slide the frozen line left and right. As long as you enter and leave through the same two curves, one integral is enough. If the entry or exit curve changes at some $x$ (region ③ with θ > 1 changes at $x=\theta-1$), split the outer integral there.

    (b) Polar coordinates

    Whenever the region is a circle, or the function contains $\alpha^2+\beta^2$, switch to polar coordinates:

    $$\begin{aligned} \alpha&=r\cos\phi, \qquad \beta=r\sin\phi,\\ \alpha^2+\beta^2&=r^2,\\ d\alpha\,d\beta&=r\,dr\,d\phi . \end{aligned}$$

    Why the extra $r$? A small polar "rectangle" with sides $dr$ (radial) and $d\phi$ (angular) is not a square. Its angular side is an arc of length $r\,d\phi$, which gets longer the further you are from the centre. So its area is $dr\times r\,d\phi$. This factor $r$ is what makes Gaussian integrals solvable: $\int re^{-r^2/2}dr=-e^{-r^2/2}$, whereas $\int e^{-r^2/2}dr$ has no formula.

    (c) Differentiating an integral whose limit moves (Leibniz rule)

    We constantly find a CDF first and then differentiate it to get a PDF. The CDF is usually an integral with a variable limit. The rule is:

    $$\frac{d}{d\theta}\int_{-\infty}^{h(\theta)} g(\alpha)\,d\alpha \;=\; g\big(h(\theta)\big)\cdot h'(\theta).$$

    In words: put the upper limit into the integrand, then multiply by the derivative of the upper limit (the chain rule). For a lower limit you get a minus sign. Example:

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{d\beta}F_X(\sqrt\beta) &=f_X(\sqrt\beta)\cdot\frac{d}{d\beta}\sqrt\beta\\ &=f_X(\sqrt\beta)\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt\beta}. \end{aligned}$$

    (d) Convolution

    $$(f*g)(\theta)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(\alpha)\,g(\theta-\alpha)\,d\alpha .$$

    Read it as a procedure:

    1. Flip $g$ left-to-right, so $g(\alpha)$ becomes $g(-\alpha)$.
    2. Slide the flipped copy so it sits at position θ: $g(\theta-\alpha)$.
    3. Multiply it by $f(\alpha)$, point by point.
    4. The area under that product is the value of the convolution at θ.
    5. Repeat for every θ.

    We will prove that the PDF of a sum of independent RVs is the convolution of their PDFs (§10). With rectangles, the answer changes form as the sliding copy moves, so you work in cases: "no overlap", "partial overlap", "full overlap".

    (e) Matrices: just enough for the covariance matrix

    • Transpose of a product reverses the order: $(AB)^T=B^TA^T$.
    • For a $2\times2$ matrix: $\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\dfrac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix}$.
    • Multiplying: row of the first × column of the second. For example $$\begin{bmatrix}2&3\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2(1)+3(4)\\-1(1)+2(4)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}14\\7\end{bmatrix}.$$
    • We will use: if $\mathbf Y=A\mathbf X$, then $E[\mathbf Y]=AE[\mathbf X]$ and $\mathbf K_Y=A\mathbf K_XA^T$ (proved in §7).

    (f) Integrals you will reuse

    $$\begin{aligned} \int_0^\infty x^ne^{-ax}\,dx&=\frac{n!}{a^{n+1}}, \\[4pt] \int_0^\infty re^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}\,dr&=\sigma^2, \\[4pt] \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2/2}\,dx&=\sqrt{2\pi}. \end{aligned}$$

    1Joint cumulative distribution function

    Why we need a joint description

    Suppose you know the distribution of the transmitted bit $X$, and separately the distribution of the received bit $Y$. Can you work out the probability of an error, $P(X\ne Y)$?

    No. Here is a concrete reason. Let both $X$ and $Y$ be 0 or 1 with probability ½ each. A perfect channel ($Y=X$ always) fits this description, and so does a channel that flips every bit ($Y=1-X$ always). The first has error probability 0, the second has error probability 1. Yet the individual distributions of $X$ and of $Y$ are identical in both cases.

    So the separate distributions (the marginals) say nothing about how $X$ and $Y$ move together. We need one object that describes the pair. As in Unit 2, the most general such object is a CDF, now with two arguments.

    Definition (lecturer)

    For two RVs $X,Y$ defined on the same experiment, the joint CDF is

    $$F_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)=P\big(X\le\alpha,\ Y\le\beta\big)=P\big(\{X\le\alpha\}\cap\{Y\le\beta\}\big).$$

    The comma means "and": both conditions must hold at once.

    Picture it. Think of each run of the experiment as throwing a dart that lands at the point $(X,Y)$ in the plane. Then $F_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)$ is the probability that the dart lands in the infinite quadrant to the south-west of the point $(\alpha,\beta)$, i.e. to the left of $\alpha$ and below $\beta$. The lecturer calls such a region a "contour in the 2-D plane".

    Properties of the joint CDF (lecturer; asked in MS 2024)

    Each property follows from the quadrant picture.

    1. $F_{XY}(-\infty,\beta)=0$ and $F_{XY}(\alpha,-\infty)=0$.
      Pushing the corner infinitely far left (or down) makes the quadrant empty. The probability of landing in an empty region is 0.
    2. Marginal CDFs: $F_{XY}(\infty,\beta)=F_Y(\beta)$ and $F_{XY}(\alpha,\infty)=F_X(\alpha)$.
      Pushing α to +∞ removes the condition on $X$ ("$X\le\infty$" is always true). What remains is $P(Y\le\beta)$, the CDF of $Y$ alone.
    3. $F_{XY}(\infty,\infty)=1$, and always $0\le F_{XY}\le1$.
      The quadrant becomes the whole plane, and the dart always lands somewhere.
    4. Non-decreasing in each argument: if $\alpha_1\ge\alpha_2$ and $\beta_1\ge\beta_2$, then $F_{XY}(\alpha_1,\beta_1)\ge F_{XY}(\alpha_2,\beta_2)$.
      Moving the corner up or right makes the quadrant bigger, and a bigger region cannot have a smaller probability.
    5. Right-continuous in each argument, as in Unit 2.
    6. Rectangle rule (next): every rectangle gets probability $\ge0$.

    Probability of a rectangle

    The lecturer asks: if $\alpha_1\gt\alpha_2$ and $\beta_1\gt\beta_2$, what is the probability of region ④, the rectangle $\{\alpha_2\lt X\le\alpha_1,\ \beta_2\lt Y\le\beta_1\}$? The CDF only gives us quadrants, so we must build the rectangle out of quadrants.

    Step 1: name the pieces. The four regions ①, ②, ③, ④ do not overlap. Write $P_k$ for the probability of region $k$. Each quadrant is a union of some of the regions:

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{quadrant at }(\alpha_1,\beta_1)&=①\cup②\cup③\cup④ &&\Rightarrow\ F(\alpha_1,\beta_1)=P_1+P_2+P_3+P_4\\ \text{quadrant at }(\alpha_2,\beta_1)&=①\cup② &&\Rightarrow\ F(\alpha_2,\beta_1)=P_1+P_2\\ \text{quadrant at }(\alpha_1,\beta_2)&=①\cup③ &&\Rightarrow\ F(\alpha_1,\beta_2)=P_1+P_3\\ \text{quadrant at }(\alpha_2,\beta_2)&=① &&\Rightarrow\ F(\alpha_2,\beta_2)=P_1 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2: solve for $P_4$. Start from the big quadrant and remove the two side quadrants. Region ① lies in both side quadrants, so it gets removed twice; add it back once:

    $$\begin{aligned} &F(\alpha_1,\beta_1)-F(\alpha_2,\beta_1)-F(\alpha_1,\beta_2)+F(\alpha_2,\beta_2)\\ &=(P_1+P_2+P_3+P_4)-(P_1+P_2)-(P_1+P_3)+P_1\\ &=P_4 . \end{aligned}$$
    Rectangle formula $$\begin{aligned} &P(\alpha_2\lt X\le\alpha_1,\ \beta_2\lt Y\le\beta_1)\\ &\quad=F(\alpha_1,\beta_1)-F(\alpha_2,\beta_1)-F(\alpha_1,\beta_2)+F(\alpha_2,\beta_2). \end{aligned}$$

    Memory aid: top-right − top-left − bottom-right + bottom-left. It is inclusion–exclusion from Unit 1 in disguise.

    Why it matters for validity: a function of two variables can be 0 at −∞, 1 at +∞ and increasing in each variable, and still fail to be a joint CDF. It is a valid joint CDF only if this rectangle expression is ≥ 0 for every rectangle.

    Other probabilities from the joint CDF

    Using the same "cut into pieces" idea (write $F=F_{XY}$):

    $$\begin{aligned} P(X\le a,\ Y\gt b)&=F_X(a)-F(a,b)&&\why{strip left of a, minus its lower part}\\ P(X\gt a,\ Y\le b)&=F_Y(b)-F(a,b)\\ P(X\gt a,\ Y\gt b)&=1-F_X(a)-F_Y(b)+F(a,b)&&\why{complement of "X ≤ a or Y ≤ b"} \end{aligned}$$

    with $F_X(a)=F(a,\infty)$ and $F_Y(b)=F(\infty,b)$. The last line is used in min-of-RVs questions (§10).

    EasyExample 1.1: product-form CDF

    $F_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)=(1-e^{-\alpha})(1-e^{-2\beta})$ for $\alpha,\beta\ge0$, and 0 otherwise. Find the marginal CDFs and $P(1\lt X\le2,\ 0\lt Y\le1)$.

    Solution

    Marginals. Let the other argument go to ∞. Since $e^{-\infty}=0$, that bracket becomes 1:

    $$\begin{aligned} F_X(\alpha)&=F_{XY}(\alpha,\infty)\\ &=(1-e^{-\alpha})(1-0)\\ &=1-e^{-\alpha},\quad\alpha\ge0 &&\why{this is Exp(1)}\\[6pt] F_Y(\beta)&=F_{XY}(\infty,\beta)\\ &=1-e^{-2\beta},\quad\beta\ge0 &&\why{this is Exp(2)} \end{aligned}$$

    Rectangle. Here $\alpha_1=2,\ \alpha_2=1,\ \beta_1=1,\ \beta_2=0$:

    $$\begin{aligned} P&=F(2,1)-F(1,1)-F(2,0)+F(1,0)\\ &=F(2,1)-F(1,1)-0+0 &&\why{$1-e^{0}=0$, so $F(\cdot,0)=0$}\\ &=(1-e^{-2})(1-e^{-2})-(1-e^{-1})(1-e^{-2})\\ &=(1-e^{-2})\big[(1-e^{-2})-(1-e^{-1})\big] &&\why{factor out the common bracket}\\ &=(1-e^{-2})(e^{-1}-e^{-2})\\ &=0.8647\times0.2325\\ &=\mathbf{0.201}. \end{aligned}$$

    Observation. The joint CDF is exactly the product $F_X(\alpha)F_Y(\beta)$. We will see in §4 that this means $X$ and $Y$ are independent. Then the answer is simply $P(1\lt X\le2)\cdot P(0\lt Y\le1)$, which gives the same number.

    ExamExample 1.2: building a joint CDF from a joint PDF, zone by zone

    $(X,Y)$ is uniform on the triangle $0\lt y\lt x\lt1$, i.e. $f_{XY}=2$ there (area ½, so height 2). Find $F_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)$ for every $(\alpha,\beta)$, then the marginal CDFs.

    Solution

    Why this is hard, and the trick. $F_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)$ is the probability of the south-west quadrant at $(\alpha,\beta)$. As the corner moves around the plane, the quadrant cuts the triangle in differently shaped pieces, so the formula changes. The trick is to split the plane into zones where the piece has the same shape, and handle each zone separately. Since the density is constant (2), each answer is just $2\times$(area of the piece).

    Zone A: α < 0 or β < 0. The quadrant misses the triangle completely: $F=0$.

    Zone B: $0\le\beta\le\alpha\le1$ (corner inside the triangle). The piece is the part of the triangle with $x\le\alpha$ and $y\le\beta$. Use horizontal strips: for fixed $y$ between 0 and β, $x$ runs from the diagonal ($x=y$) to α:

    $$\begin{aligned} F(\alpha,\beta)&=\int_0^{\beta}\int_{y}^{\alpha}2\,dx\,dy\\ &=\int_0^{\beta}2(\alpha-y)\,dy &&\why{inner: length α − y}\\ &=\Big[2\alpha y-y^2\Big]_0^{\beta}\\ &=2\alpha\beta-\beta^2 . \end{aligned}$$

    Zone C: $0\le\alpha\le1$, β > α (corner above the diagonal). Every dot with $x\le\alpha$ automatically has $y\lt x\le\alpha\lt\beta$, so the condition on $y$ does nothing:

    $$F(\alpha,\beta)=P(X\le\alpha)=2\times\tfrac12\alpha^2=\alpha^2 .$$

    Zone D: α > 1, $0\le\beta\le1$. Now $x\le\alpha$ does nothing (all $x\lt1$). It is zone B with α replaced by 1:

    $$F(\alpha,\beta)=2\beta-\beta^2 .$$

    Zone E: α > 1 and β > 1. The quadrant holds the whole triangle: $F=1$.

    Checks that catch mistakes.

    • Neighbouring zones must agree on their common border. B at β = α: $2\alpha^2-\alpha^2=\alpha^2$ = C ✓. B at α = 1: $2\beta-\beta^2$ = D ✓.
    • Differentiating in zone B: $\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\alpha\,\partial\beta}(2\alpha\beta-\beta^2)=2$ = the PDF ✓.

    Marginals (send the other argument to ∞):

    $$\begin{aligned} F_X(\alpha)&=F(\alpha,\infty)=\alpha^2\ \ (0\le\alpha\le1) &&\why{zone C}\Rightarrow f_X=2\alpha\\ F_Y(\beta)&=F(\infty,\beta)=2\beta-\beta^2\ \ (0\le\beta\le1) &&\why{zone D}\Rightarrow f_Y=2-2\beta \end{aligned}$$

    These match the dot picture in §0: more $x$-values near 1, more $y$-values near 0.

    2Joint PMF: two discrete random variables

    Idea

    When $X$ and $Y$ can each take only a list of values, the pair $(X,Y)$ can only land on a grid of points. All we need is the probability of each grid point. Store those probabilities in a table and you have the whole joint distribution.

    Definition

    If $X$ takes values $x_1,x_2,\dots$ and $Y$ takes $y_1,y_2,\dots$, the joint PMF is

    $$p_{ij}=P_{XY}(x_i,y_j)=P(X=x_i,\ Y=y_j).$$

    Write it as a table: one row per value of $X$, one column per value of $Y$.

    • Validity: every $p_{ij}\ge0$, and all entries add to 1.
    • Marginals = row and column sums. $P_X(x_i)=\sum_jp_{ij}$ and $P_Y(y_j)=\sum_ip_{ij}$. They are traditionally written in the margins of the table, which is where the name "marginal" comes from.
    • Any probability: add the cells that belong to the event. For example $P(X\lt Y)=\sum_{x_i\lt y_j}p_{ij}$.
    • Joint CDF: $F_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{x_i\le\alpha}\ \sum_{y_j\le\beta}p_{ij}$, a two-dimensional staircase.

    Why are the marginals row sums? Take $P(X=x_1)$. Whenever $X=x_1$ happens, $Y$ takes exactly one of its values. So the event $\{X=x_1\}$ splits into the disjoint pieces $\{X=x_1,Y=y_1\}$, $\{X=x_1,Y=y_2\}$, …, and probabilities of disjoint pieces add. That sum is the first row. This is the theorem of total probability from Unit 1.

    3Joint PDF & marginal densities

    Idea: a hill over the plane

    For one continuous RV, the PDF was "probability per unit length". For two, the joint PDF is probability per unit area. Picture a surface $f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)$ standing over the plane, like a hill or a tent. The probability that $(X,Y)$ lands in a region is the volume under the hill above that region. Where the hill is tall, the dart is likely to land.

    The precise meaning of a single value: for a tiny rectangle of sides $d\alpha$ and $d\beta$ at the point $(\alpha,\beta)$,

    $$P\big(\alpha\lt X\le\alpha+d\alpha,\ \ \beta\lt Y\le\beta+d\beta\big)\approx f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)\,d\alpha\,d\beta .$$

    That is: height × base area = volume of a thin column. Adding up all the columns over a region gives the double integral below.

    Definition (lecturer)

    The joint PDF is the mixed second derivative of the joint CDF, and the CDF is the double integral of the PDF:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)&=\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\alpha\,\partial\beta}F_{XY}(\alpha,\beta),\\[4pt] F_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)&=\int_{-\infty}^{\beta}\int_{-\infty}^{\alpha}f_{XY}(\theta,\gamma)\,d\theta\,d\gamma . \end{aligned}$$

    (Inside the integral the variables are renamed $\theta,\gamma$, so that they don't clash with the limits $\alpha,\beta$.) For any region $C$ of the plane:

    $$P\big((X,Y)\in C\big)=\iint_C f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)\,d\alpha\,d\beta .$$
    Properties (lecturer)
    1. Total volume is 1: $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{XY}\,d\alpha\,d\beta=1$. This is just $F_{XY}(\infty,\infty)=1$.
    2. Never negative: $f_{XY}\ge0$. A tiny rectangle has probability $\approx f\,d\alpha\,d\beta$, and probabilities can't be negative.
    3. Like a 1-D PDF, $f_{XY}$ is a density, not a probability. Its values can be larger than 1.
    4. Marginals: theorem below.
    Theorem: marginal PDFs (with the lecturer's derivation) $$\boxed{f_X(\alpha)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)\,d\beta,\qquad f_Y(\beta)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)\,d\alpha}$$

    Proof. Start from property 2 of the joint CDF, $F_X(\alpha)=F_{XY}(\alpha,\infty)$, write the CDF as a double integral, and differentiate with respect to α:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_X(\alpha)&=\frac{d}{d\alpha}F_X(\alpha)\\ &=\frac{d}{d\alpha}F_{XY}(\alpha,\infty) &&\why{marginal CDF property}\\ &=\frac{d}{d\alpha}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\{\int_{-\infty}^{\alpha}f_{XY}(\theta,\gamma)\,d\theta\right\}d\gamma &&\why{CDF as a double integral}\\ &=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d}{d\alpha}\left\{\int_{-\infty}^{\alpha}f_{XY}(\theta,\gamma)\,d\theta\right\}d\gamma &&\why{move d/dα inside the γ-integral} \end{aligned}$$

    Now let $G_{XY}(\theta,\gamma)$ be an antiderivative of $f_{XY}$ in θ, so $\partial G_{XY}/\partial\theta=f_{XY}$. The inner integral is

    $$\int_{-\infty}^{\alpha}f_{XY}(\theta,\gamma)\,d\theta=G_{XY}(\alpha,\gamma)-G_{XY}(-\infty,\gamma).$$

    The second term does not depend on α, so its α-derivative is 0. The derivative of the first term is $f_{XY}(\alpha,\gamma)$. Therefore

    $$f_X(\alpha)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{XY}(\alpha,\gamma)\,d\gamma .\qquad ∎$$

    (This is just the Leibniz rule (c) from the warm-up.)

    What the marginal theorem means. To get the PDF of one variable, integrate the other variable out. In the hill picture, you stand at a fixed α and add up the hill along the whole vertical line at α. That total, as a function of α, is the PDF of $X$. It is like squashing the hill flat against the α-axis. The discrete version is "add up a row of the table".

    Joint → marginals always works; marginals → joint does not

    Given $f_{XY}$ you can always find $f_X$ and $f_Y$. Given only $f_X$ and $f_Y$, you generally cannot rebuild $f_{XY}$. Many different hills squash down to the same two shadows: that was the perfect-vs-flipping channel story at the start of §1. The two exceptions are:

    • independence, where $f_{XY}=f_Xf_Y$ (§4);
    • jointly Gaussian RVs, where the marginals plus one number ρ determine the joint PDF (§7).
    Recipe: any joint-PDF question
    1. Draw the support, the region where $f_{XY}\gt0$. Most wrong answers come from integral limits that ignore it.
    2. Finding a constant $k$: set the double integral over the support equal to 1 and solve.
    3. Marginal $f_X(\alpha)$: fix α and integrate over β along the vertical strip inside the support. Then state the range of α where the formula holds, and write "0 elsewhere".
    4. Probability of an event: draw (event) ∩ (support), choose the strip direction, set the limits from the picture, integrate.
    5. Check: each marginal must integrate to 1, and every probability must lie between 0 and 1.
    EasyExample 3.1 (lecturer): marginals of $e^{-(\alpha+\beta)}$

    $f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)=e^{-(\alpha+\beta)}$ for $\alpha,\beta\ge0$, and 0 otherwise. Find $f_X$ and $f_Y$.

    Solution

    Support: the first quadrant. For a fixed $\alpha\ge0$, the vertical strip runs over all $\beta\ge0$:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_X(\alpha)&=\int_0^\infty e^{-(\alpha+\gamma)}\,d\gamma\\ &=e^{-\alpha}\int_0^\infty e^{-\gamma}\,d\gamma &&\why{$e^{-\alpha}$ is a constant here}\\ &=e^{-\alpha}\Big[-e^{-\gamma}\Big]_0^\infty\\ &=e^{-\alpha}\,\big(0-(-1)\big)\\ &=e^{-\alpha},\qquad\alpha\ge0 . \end{aligned}$$

    The formula is symmetric in α and β, so likewise $f_Y(\beta)=e^{-\beta}$ for $\beta\ge0$. Both are Exp(1). Notice $f_X(\alpha)f_Y(\beta)=e^{-\alpha}e^{-\beta}=f_{XY}$: the joint PDF is the product of the marginals, so (§4) $X$ and $Y$ are independent.

    MediumExample 3.2 (lecturer exercise): $P(X\le1,\ |Y|\le X)$

    Same PDF $e^{-(\alpha+\beta)}$ on $\alpha,\beta\ge0$. Compute $P(X\le1,\ |Y|\le X)$.

    Solution

    Step 1: draw the event.

    • $|Y|\le X$ means $-X\le Y\le X$: the wedge between the lines $\beta=\alpha$ and $\beta=-\alpha$, opening to the right.
    • $X\le1$: everything left of the line $\alpha=1$.
    • The support kills everything with $\beta\lt0$, so the lower half of the wedge contributes nothing.

    What remains is the triangle $0\le\beta\le\alpha\le1$:

    Step 2: choose strips. The lecturer uses horizontal strips. For a fixed $\beta\in[0,1]$, the horizontal line enters the triangle at the diagonal, $\alpha=\beta$, and leaves at $\alpha=1$.

    Step 3: inner integral (over α).

    $$\begin{aligned} \int_\beta^1e^{-\alpha}e^{-\beta}\,d\alpha&=e^{-\beta}\Big[-e^{-\alpha}\Big]_\beta^1\\ &=e^{-\beta}\big(e^{-\beta}-e^{-1}\big)\\ &=e^{-2\beta}-e^{-1-\beta}. \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4: outer integral (over β).

    $$\begin{aligned} P&=\int_0^1\big(e^{-2\beta}-e^{-1-\beta}\big)\,d\beta\\ &=\Big[-\tfrac12e^{-2\beta}+e^{-1-\beta}\Big]_0^1\\ &=\Big(-\tfrac12e^{-2}+e^{-2}\Big)-\Big(-\tfrac12+e^{-1}\Big)\\ &=\frac12-\frac1e+\frac1{2e^2}\\ &=0.5-0.3679+0.0677\\ &=\mathbf{0.1998}. \end{aligned}$$

    Check with vertical strips. For fixed α, β runs from 0 to α:

    $$\begin{aligned} P&=\int_0^1e^{-\alpha}\big(1-e^{-\alpha}\big)\,d\alpha\\ &=(1-e^{-1})-\tfrac12(1-e^{-2})\\ &=0.6321-0.4323\\ &=0.1998 .\ ✓ \end{aligned}$$

    The handwritten notes end with $\frac1{e^2}$ instead of $\frac1{2e^2}$; that is a slip, as the check confirms.

    MediumExample 3.3 (lecturer): probability of a disc under a Gaussian hill

    $f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)=\dfrac1{2\pi}\exp\left\{-\tfrac12(\alpha^2+\beta^2)\right\}$. Find $P(X^2+Y^2\le a^2)$.

    Solution

    Why polar? The region is a disc and the function depends only on $\alpha^2+\beta^2=r^2$. In Cartesian coordinates the limits would be $\pm\sqrt{a^2-\alpha^2}$ and the integral would be impossible. In polar coordinates both are easy: $r$ from 0 to $a$, φ from 0 to $2\pi$.

    $$\begin{aligned} P&=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^a\frac1{2\pi}e^{-r^2/2}\,r\,dr\,d\phi &&\why{$d\alpha\,d\beta=r\,dr\,d\phi$}\\ &=\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot2\pi\int_0^ae^{-r^2/2}\,r\,dr &&\why{nothing depends on φ}\\ &=\int_0^{a^2/2}e^{-t}\,dt &&\why{$t=r^2/2$, $dt=r\,dr$}\\ &=\Big[-e^{-t}\Big]_0^{a^2/2}\\ &=\mathbf{1-e^{-a^2/2}}. \end{aligned}$$

    What this tells us. This is $P(R\le a)$, the CDF of the distance $R=\sqrt{X^2+Y^2}$ of the dart from the centre. Differentiating gives the PDF of $R$:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_R(a)&=\frac{d}{da}\big(1-e^{-a^2/2}\big)\\ &=a\,e^{-a^2/2},\qquad a\ge0, \end{aligned}$$

    the Rayleigh distribution (§10). So the lecturer's exercise is secretly a derivation of the Rayleigh PDF.

    MediumExample 3.4: point uniform on a disc (MS 2021, ES Nov–Dec 2024)

    $(X,Y)$ is uniform on the disc $x^2+y^2\le R^2$: $f_{XY}=k$ inside and 0 outside. Find $k$ and $f_X$.

    Solution

    Finding k. A constant density is a flat-topped "cake". Its volume is height × base area:

    $$k\cdot\pi R^2=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad k=\frac1{\pi R^2}.$$

    (General rule: for any uniform joint PDF, $k=1/(\text{area of the support})$.)

    Finding the marginal. Fix α with $-R\le\alpha\le R$. The vertical line at α crosses the disc along a chord. From $\alpha^2+\beta^2=R^2$, the chord runs from $\beta=-\sqrt{R^2-\alpha^2}$ to $\beta=+\sqrt{R^2-\alpha^2}$:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_X(\alpha)&=\int_{-\sqrt{R^2-\alpha^2}}^{\sqrt{R^2-\alpha^2}}\frac{1}{\pi R^2}\,d\beta\\ &=\frac{1}{\pi R^2}\times\big(\text{length of the chord}\big)\\ &=\frac{2\sqrt{R^2-\alpha^2}}{\pi R^2},\qquad|\alpha|\le R . \end{aligned}$$

    By symmetry $f_Y$ has the same form.

    Two lessons.

    • The marginal is not uniform, even though the joint PDF is. Chords near the centre are long; chords near the edge are short. So $X$ is more likely to be near 0.
    • $X$ and $Y$ are not independent. If you learn that $X$ is close to $R$, then $Y$ is forced to be close to 0. Knowing one changes what you expect of the other.
    MediumRunning example "Drill T", part 1: constant, marginals, a probability

    $f_{XY}(x,y)=k(x+y)$ on the triangle $0\lt y\lt x\lt1$, and 0 elsewhere. (i) Find $k$. (ii) Find $f_X$ and $f_Y$. (iii) Find $P(X+Y\lt1)$.

    Solution

    Step 0: draw the support. $0\lt y\lt x\lt1$ is the triangle below the diagonal (gallery region ①). Vertical strip at $x$: $y$ from 0 to $x$. Horizontal strip at $y$: $x$ from $y$ to 1.

    (i) Total volume = 1. Vertical strips, inner variable $y$ ($x$ is a constant inside):

    $$\begin{aligned} \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{x}k(x+y)\,dy\,dx&=k\int_0^1\Big[xy+\tfrac{y^2}{2}\Big]_{y=0}^{x}dx\\ &=k\int_0^1\Big(x^2+\tfrac{x^2}{2}\Big)dx\\ &=k\int_0^1\tfrac32x^2\,dx=k\cdot\tfrac12 . \end{aligned}$$

    Setting $\frac k2=1$: $\mathbf{k=2}$, so $f_{XY}=2(x+y)$ on the triangle.

    $$f_X(x)=\int_0^{x}2(x+y)\,dy=2\cdot\tfrac32x^2=3x^2,\qquad0\lt x\lt1 .$$

    Marginal of Y. Fix $y\in(0,1)$; the horizontal strip runs over $y\lt x\lt1$. This is where people go wrong: the limits are $y$ to 1, not 0 to 1.

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Y(y)&=\int_y^{1}2(x+y)\,dx\\ &=\Big[x^2+2xy\Big]_{x=y}^{1}\\ &=(1+2y)-(y^2+2y^2)\\ &=1+2y-3y^2,\qquad0\lt y\lt1 . \end{aligned}$$

    $$\begin{aligned} P(X+Y\lt1)&=\int_0^{1/2}\int_{y}^{1-y}2(x+y)\,dx\,dy\\ &=\int_0^{1/2}\Big[x^2+2xy\Big]_{x=y}^{1-y}dy\\ &=\int_0^{1/2}\Big\{\big[(1-y)^2+2y(1-y)\big]-\big[y^2+2y^2\big]\Big\}dy\\ &=\int_0^{1/2}\big\{1-y^2-3y^2\big\}dy &&\why{$(1-y)^2+2y-2y^2=1-y^2$}\\ &=\int_0^{1/2}(1-4y^2)\,dy\\ &=\Big[y-\tfrac43y^3\Big]_0^{1/2}=\tfrac12-\tfrac16=\mathbf{\tfrac13}. \end{aligned}$$

    (Inner integral on the right: $2[x(1-x)+\frac{(1-x)^2}{2}]=(1-x)(2x+1-x)=1-x^2$.) Same answer, more work: choosing the strip direction well saves time.

    4Independent random variables

    Idea

    Two RVs are independent when learning the value of one tells you nothing about the other. In Unit 1, events $A$ and $B$ were independent when $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$. For RVs we demand this for every pair of events of the form $\{X\le\alpha\}$ and $\{Y\le\beta\}$.

    Definition (lecturer)

    $X$ and $Y$ are independent if, for every α and β,

    $$\begin{aligned} F_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)&=P(X\le\alpha)\,P(Y\le\beta)\\ &=F_X(\alpha)\,F_Y(\beta). \end{aligned}$$

    Differentiating both sides with respect to α and β gives the equivalent PDF form. The PMF form is the discrete analogue:

    $$\boxed{f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)=f_X(\alpha)\,f_Y(\beta)}\qquad\qquad P_{XY}(x_i,y_j)=P_X(x_i)\,P_Y(y_j)\ \ \text{for all }i,j .$$

    Consequences: $P(X\in A,\ Y\in B)=P(X\in A)\,P(Y\in B)$ for any sets $A,B$; and any function of $X$ is independent of any function of $Y$.

    Fast independence test (continuous case)

    $X$ and $Y$ are independent if and only if both of these hold:

    1. On its support, $f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)$ factors as (a function of α only) × (a function of β only); and
    2. the support is a rectangle $\{a\lt\alpha\lt b\}\times\{c\lt\beta\lt d\}$ (infinite sides allowed, so a quadrant or the whole plane count).

    Why does the shape of the support matter? Take $f=2$ on the triangle $x+y\le1$. The formula "2" certainly factors. But the triangle itself links the variables: if $X=0.9$, then $Y$ must be below 0.1, while if $X=0$, $Y$ can be anything up to 1. So learning $X$ changes the possible values of $Y$: dependent. A rectangle is exactly the shape where the allowed range of $Y$ is the same whatever $X$ is.

    EasyExample 4.1 (lecturer): $d(\alpha+\beta)$ on the unit square

    $f_{XY}=d(\alpha+\beta)$ for $0\lt\alpha\le1$, $0\lt\beta\le1$. (i) Find $d$. (ii) Are $X$ and $Y$ independent?

    Solution

    (i) Set the total volume to 1. Do the α-integral first, treating β as a constant:

    $$\begin{aligned} 1&=\int_0^1\int_0^1d(\alpha+\beta)\,d\alpha\,d\beta\\ &=d\int_0^1\Big[\tfrac{\alpha^2}{2}+\beta\alpha\Big]_{\alpha=0}^{1}\,d\beta\\ &=d\int_0^1\Big(\tfrac12+\beta\Big)\,d\beta\\ &=d\Big(\tfrac12+\tfrac12\Big)\\ &=d . \end{aligned}$$

    So $d=\mathbf1$.

    (ii) Find both marginals:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_X(\alpha)&=\int_0^1(\alpha+\beta)\,d\beta=\alpha+\tfrac12,\qquad0\lt\alpha\le1,\\ f_Y(\beta)&=\beta+\tfrac12,\qquad0\lt\beta\le1 . \end{aligned}$$

    Their product:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_X(\alpha)f_Y(\beta)&=\big(\alpha+\tfrac12\big)\big(\beta+\tfrac12\big)\\ &=\alpha\beta+\tfrac12\alpha+\tfrac12\beta+\tfrac14\\ &\ne\alpha+\beta . \end{aligned}$$

    Not independent. One specific point is enough to prove it: at $(0,0)$ the joint PDF is 0 but the product of marginals is $\tfrac14$. (The quick test also says so: $\alpha+\beta$ cannot be written as $g(\alpha)h(\beta)$.)

    MediumExample 4.2: meeting problem (a classic, asked in MS 2024)

    Two friends arrive independently, each at a time uniformly distributed between 12:00 and 13:00. What is the probability that whoever arrives first has to wait more than 10 minutes?

    Solution

    Set up. Measure time in minutes after 12:00. Then $X,Y$ are iid $U(0,60)$. Independence lets us multiply the marginals:

    $$f_{XY}=\frac1{60}\cdot\frac1{60}=\frac1{3600}\quad\text{on the square }[0,60]^2 .$$

    The event. The first person waits more than 10 minutes when the arrival times differ by more than 10: $|X-Y|\gt10$. In the square this is two corner triangles, one above the line $y=x+10$ and one below $y=x-10$. Each triangle has two legs of length $60-10=50$.

    Uniform density ⇒ probability = favourable area / total area.

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{favourable area}&=2\times\tfrac12\times50\times50=2500,\\ P(|X-Y|\gt10)&=\frac{2500}{3600}\\ &=\mathbf{\frac{25}{36}}\approx0.694 . \end{aligned}$$
    EasyDrill T, part 2: are X and Y independent?

    For $f_{XY}=2(x+y)$ on $0\lt y\lt x\lt1$ (marginals $f_X=3x^2$, $f_Y=1+2y-3y^2$ from part 1), decide whether $X$ and $Y$ are independent. Give a one-line reason and a proof.

    Solution

    One-line reason (fast test). The support is a triangle, not a rectangle, so they cannot be independent: knowing $X=0.3$ forces $Y\lt0.3$. (Also, $x+y$ doesn't factor as $g(x)h(y)$.)

    Proof: find one point where $f_{XY}\ne f_Xf_Y$. The easiest point is one outside the triangle but inside the "rectangle of marginals", e.g. $(x,y)=(0.2,\ 0.5)$:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_{XY}(0.2,0.5)&=0 &&\why{$y\gt x$: outside the support}\\ f_X(0.2)\,f_Y(0.5)&=3(0.04)\times(1+1-0.75)=0.12\times1.25=0.15\ne0 . \end{aligned}$$

    So $f_{XY}\ne f_Xf_Y$: dependent. (Strictly, densities may differ on a set of zero area without mattering, but here they differ on the whole region $y\gt x$, which has area ½.)

    5Conditional densities & conditional expectation Beyond class notes

    The syllabus lists "conditional densities" and "conditional expectation". The handwritten notes condition only on an event (Unit 2 §14); here we condition on the value of another RV.

    Idea: slice the hill, then rescale the slice

    Suppose you have observed that $X=\alpha$ (for example, you measured the received voltage). What is the distribution of $Y$ now?

    Go back to the hill $f_{XY}$. Knowing $X=\alpha$ means the dart landed somewhere on the vertical line at α. So cut the hill along that line. The cut edge is a curve in β. It has the right shape for the new distribution of $Y$: where it is tall, $Y$ is likely. But its area is not 1. Its area is $\int f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)\,d\beta=f_X(\alpha)$, the marginal. Dividing the slice by its area turns it into a proper PDF. That is the whole definition.

    Conditional PMF / PDF $$\begin{aligned} P(Y=y_j\mid X=x_i)&=\frac{P_{XY}(x_i,y_j)}{P_X(x_i)} &&\why{discrete: a row, divided by its total}\\[6pt] f_{Y|X}(\beta\mid\alpha)&=\frac{f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)}{f_X(\alpha)},\qquad f_X(\alpha)\gt0 &&\why{continuous: a slice, divided by its area} \end{aligned}$$

    For each fixed α, this is a genuine PDF in β: it is ≥ 0 and its area is 1. The discrete version is just the definition of conditional probability from Unit 1, $P(B\mid A)=P(A\cap B)/P(A)$.

    Rearranging the definition gives four tools you will use again and again:

    Consequences
    1. Multiplication rule. $f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)=f_{Y|X}(\beta\mid\alpha)\,f_X(\alpha)$. Joint PDFs are often built this way: "first pick $X$, then pick $Y$ given $X$".
    2. Total probability. Integrate the multiplication rule over α: $$f_Y(\beta)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{Y|X}(\beta\mid\alpha)\,f_X(\alpha)\,d\alpha .$$
    3. Bayes' rule. Use the multiplication rule both ways round: $$f_{X|Y}(\alpha\mid\beta)=\frac{f_{Y|X}(\beta\mid\alpha)\,f_X(\alpha)}{f_Y(\beta)}.$$
    4. Independence ⇔ $f_{Y|X}(\beta\mid\alpha)=f_Y(\beta)$ for every α. Learning $X$ doesn't change the distribution of $Y$. (Substitute $f_{XY}=f_Xf_Y$ into the definition and $f_X$ cancels.)
    Conditional expectation

    The conditional distribution has a mean, like any distribution:

    $$E[Y\mid X=\alpha]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\beta\,f_{Y|X}(\beta\mid\alpha)\,d\beta\qquad\Big(\text{discrete: }\sum_jy_j\,P(Y=y_j\mid X=\alpha)\Big).$$

    This number depends on α, so it is a function of α. If you put the random $X$ in place of α, you get a random variable $E[Y\mid X]$. Think of it as "the best guess of $Y$ once you know $X$". Its key property:

    $$\boxed{E[Y]=E\big[E[Y\mid X]\big]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}E[Y\mid X=\alpha]\;f_X(\alpha)\,d\alpha}\qquad\text{(law of total expectation)}$$

    Why the law of total expectation is true. Write $E[Y]$ with the joint PDF, then use the multiplication rule:

    $$\begin{aligned} E[Y]&=\iint\beta\,f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)\,d\beta\,d\alpha\\ &=\iint\beta\,f_{Y|X}(\beta\mid\alpha)\,f_X(\alpha)\,d\beta\,d\alpha &&\why{multiplication rule}\\ &=\int\Big[\int\beta\,f_{Y|X}(\beta\mid\alpha)\,d\beta\Big]f_X(\alpha)\,d\alpha &&\why{$f_X(\alpha)$ doesn't involve β}\\ &=\int E[Y\mid X=\alpha]\,f_X(\alpha)\,d\alpha . \end{aligned}$$

    In words: average the conditional averages, weighting each one by how likely its condition is. It is the theorem of total probability (Unit 1) applied to means. It is often the easiest way to find a mean in a two-stage experiment. (There is a matching rule for variance: $\Var[Y]=E[\Var(Y\mid X)]+\Var(E[Y\mid X])$.)

    MediumExample 5.1: triangle support

    $f_{XY}=2$ for $0\lt\beta\lt\alpha\lt1$. Find $f_{Y|X}$, $E[Y\mid X=\alpha]$ and $E[Y]$.

    Solution

    Step 1: marginal of X. For fixed α, the vertical strip in the triangle runs from β = 0 up to β = α:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_X(\alpha)&=\int_0^\alpha2\,d\beta\\ &=2\alpha,\qquad0\lt\alpha\lt1 . \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2: conditional PDF.

    $$\begin{aligned} f_{Y|X}(\beta\mid\alpha)&=\frac{f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)}{f_X(\alpha)}\\ &=\frac{2}{2\alpha}\\ &=\frac1\alpha,\qquad0\lt\beta\lt\alpha . \end{aligned}$$

    This is a constant on $(0,\alpha)$. So given $X=\alpha$, $Y$ is uniform on $(0,\alpha)$. That makes sense: the slice of a flat cake is flat, and its length is α.

    Step 3: conditional mean. The mean of $U(0,\alpha)$ is the midpoint:

    $$E[Y\mid X=\alpha]=\frac\alpha2,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad E[Y\mid X]=\frac X2 .$$

    Step 4: overall mean by the law of total expectation:

    $$\begin{aligned} E[Y]&=E\Big[\frac X2\Big]\\ &=\frac12\int_0^1\alpha\cdot2\alpha\,d\alpha\\ &=\frac12\cdot\frac23\\ &=\mathbf{\frac13}. \end{aligned}$$

    Check directly. $f_Y(\beta)=\int_\beta^12\,d\alpha=2(1-\beta)$, so $E[Y]=\int_0^12\beta(1-\beta)\,d\beta=2\big(\tfrac12-\tfrac13\big)=\tfrac13$ ✓.

    ExamExample 5.2: a two-stage experiment

    $X\sim U(0,1)$. Given $X=x$, $Y\sim U(0,x)$. Find $f_{XY}$, $f_Y$ and $E[Y]$.

    Solution

    Joint PDF by the multiplication rule:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_{XY}(x,y)&=f_{Y|X}(y\mid x)\,f_X(x)\\ &=\frac1x\cdot1,\qquad0\lt y\lt x\lt1 . \end{aligned}$$

    Marginal of Y. For fixed $y$, which $x$ are allowed? We need $y\lt x\lt1$:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Y(y)&=\int_y^1\frac{1}{x}\,dx\\ &=\big[\ln x\big]_y^1\\ &=-\ln y,\qquad0\lt y\lt1 . \end{aligned}$$

    Mean. We could integrate $-y\ln y$, but the law of total expectation is quicker:

    $$\begin{aligned} E[Y]&=E\big[E[Y\mid X]\big]\\ &=E\Big[\frac X2\Big] &&\why{mean of U(0, x) is x/2}\\ &=\frac12\cdot\frac12\\ &=\mathbf{\frac14}. \end{aligned}$$

    (The same $-\ln y$ shape turns up again in §10 as the PDF of a product of two uniforms. It is no coincidence: picking $Y$ uniform on $(0,X)$ is the same as $Y=XU$ with $U\sim U(0,1)$ independent of $X$.)

    EasyExample 5.3: back to the binary channel

    In Example 2.1, a 1 is received. What is the probability that a 1 was sent?

    Solution

    Condition on $Y=1$: take the $Y=1$ column of the table and divide by its total.

    $$\begin{aligned} P(X=1\mid Y=1)&=\frac{P_{XY}(1,1)}{P_Y(1)}\\ &=\frac{0.54}{0.62}\\ &=\mathbf{0.871}. \end{aligned}$$
    Recipe: conditional PDF questions
    1. Find the marginal of the variable you condition on (e.g. $f_X$), with its range.
    2. Divide: $f_{Y|X}(y\mid x)=f_{XY}(x,y)/f_X(x)$. Treat $x$ as a fixed number; the answer is a function of $y$.
    3. Write the range of $y$ for that fixed $x$: it is the vertical strip of the support at $x$.
    4. Check it integrates to 1 over that strip.
    5. For $E[Y\mid X=x]$, integrate $y\cdot f_{Y|X}$ over the strip. For a probability given $X=x$, integrate $f_{Y|X}$ over the asked range of $y$.
    MediumDrill T, part 3: conditional PDF and conditional mean

    For $f_{XY}=2(x+y)$ on $0\lt y\lt x\lt1$ with $f_X=3x^2$: find $f_{Y|X}(y\mid x)$, $E[Y\mid X=x]$, $P(Y\lt\frac X2\mid X=x)$, and $E[Y]$ via the law of total expectation.

    Solution

    Conditional PDF. Fix $x\in(0,1)$. The strip is $0\lt y\lt x$:

    $$f_{Y|X}(y\mid x)=\frac{2(x+y)}{3x^2},\qquad0\lt y\lt x .$$

    For example, at $x=0.6$: $f_{Y|X}(y\mid0.6)=\frac{2(0.6+y)}{1.08}$ on $(0,0.6)$, a straight line rising from 1.11 to 2.22. Given $X=0.6$, values of $Y$ near 0.6 are twice as likely as values near 0.

    Check the area is 1:

    $$\int_0^{x}\frac{2(x+y)}{3x^2}\,dy=\frac{2}{3x^2}\Big(x^2+\frac{x^2}{2}\Big)=\frac{2}{3x^2}\cdot\frac{3x^2}{2}=1\ ✓$$

    Conditional mean.

    $$\begin{aligned} E[Y\mid X=x]&=\int_0^{x}y\cdot\frac{2(x+y)}{3x^2}\,dy\\ &=\frac{2}{3x^2}\int_0^{x}(xy+y^2)\,dy\\ &=\frac{2}{3x^2}\Big(\frac{x^3}{2}+\frac{x^3}{3}\Big)\\ &=\frac{2}{3x^2}\cdot\frac{5x^3}{6}=\frac{5x}{9}. \end{aligned}$$

    Meaning: once you know $X=x$, your best guess for $Y$ is $\frac59x$, a bit above the midpoint $\frac x2$ of the strip (because the density leans towards the top).

    A conditional probability.

    It doesn't depend on $x$: the conditional PDFs all have the same shape, just stretched to the strip length.

    Law of total expectation. $E[Y]=E\big[E[Y\mid X]\big]=E[\frac59X]=\frac59E[X]$, and $E[X]=\int_0^1x\cdot3x^2dx=\frac34$:

    $$E[Y]=\frac59\cdot\frac34=\frac5{12}.$$

    6Joint moments: correlation, covariance & correlation coefficient

    Why summarise with numbers?

    A joint PDF is a whole surface, which is a lot of information. Often we only want to know one thing: do $X$ and $Y$ tend to move together, and how strongly? When $X$ is above its average, is $Y$ usually above its average too, or below it, or is there no pattern? Covariance and the correlation coefficient answer this with a single number each. They are also all you need to describe jointly Gaussian RVs (§7). In Unit 4 they become the autocorrelation function of a random signal.

    Joint expectation

    Expectation of a function of two RVs (lecturer) $$E[g(X,Y)]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(\alpha,\beta)\,f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)\,d\alpha\,d\beta\qquad\Big(\text{discrete: }\sum_i\sum_jg(x_i,y_j)\,p_{ij}\Big).$$

    Each possible value $g(\alpha,\beta)$ is weighted by how likely that point is. You do not need to find the PDF of $g(X,Y)$ first.

    Does this agree with the old $E[X]$? Take $g(\alpha,\beta)=\alpha$:

    $$\begin{aligned} E[X]&=\iint\alpha\,f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)\,d\beta\,d\alpha\\ &=\int\alpha\Big[\int f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)\,d\beta\Big]d\alpha &&\why{α is constant in the inner integral}\\ &=\int\alpha\,f_X(\alpha)\,d\alpha &&\why{the bracket is the marginal} \end{aligned}$$

    which is the Unit 2 definition. Good: the joint and marginal routes always agree.

    Linearity of expectation (always true, dependent or not) $$E[aX+bY]=aE[X]+bE[Y],\qquad E\Big[\sum_{i=1}^Na_iX_i\Big]=\sum_{i=1}^Na_iE[X_i].$$

    Proof.

    $$\begin{aligned} E[aX+bY]&=\iint(a\alpha+b\beta)\,f_{XY}\,d\alpha\,d\beta\\ &=a\iint\alpha\,f_{XY}+b\iint\beta\,f_{XY} &&\why{split the integral}\\ &=aE[X]+bE[Y]. \end{aligned}$$

    No independence was used. That is why linearity is so powerful: the mean of a sum is always the sum of the means.

    Second-order moments (lecturer)

    Non-central and central joint moments

    The non-central (raw) moments are $e_{ij}=E[X^iY^j]$. The central moments are $m_{ij}=E[(X-m_X)^i(Y-m_Y)^j]$: the same thing, measured from the means. The order is $i+j$. The order-2 ones are the important ones:

    MomentNameFormula
    $e_{20},\ e_{02}$mean squares$E[X^2]=\sigma_X^2+m_X^2$, $E[Y^2]=\sigma_Y^2+m_Y^2$
    $e_{11}$correlation $R_{XY}$$E[XY]=\iint\alpha\beta\,f_{XY}\,d\alpha\,d\beta$
    $m_{20},\ m_{02}$variances$\sigma_X^2$, $\sigma_Y^2$
    $m_{11}$covariance$\Cov(X,Y)=E[(X-m_X)(Y-m_Y)]$
    $\dfrac{m_{11}}{\sqrt{m_{20}m_{02}}}$correlation coefficient$\rho_{XY}=\dfrac{\Cov(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}$

    Why does covariance measure "moving together"? Look at the product $(X-m_X)(Y-m_Y)$ for one outcome:

    • If $X$ and $Y$ are both above their means, both brackets are positive, so the product is positive.
    • If both are below, both brackets are negative, and the product is again positive.
    • If one is above and the other below, the product is negative.

    The covariance averages this product. If "same side" outcomes dominate, it is positive. If "opposite side" outcomes dominate, it is negative. If they balance, it is zero.

    The shortcut formula for covariance (lecturer's corollary)

    Expand the product and use linearity. Remember $m_X$ and $m_Y$ are constants:

    $$\begin{aligned} \Cov(X,Y)&=E\big[(X-m_X)(Y-m_Y)\big]\\ &=E\big[XY-m_YX-m_XY+m_Xm_Y\big] &&\why{multiply out}\\ &=E[XY]-m_YE[X]-m_XE[Y]+m_Xm_Y &&\why{linearity}\\ &=E[XY]-m_Ym_X-m_Xm_Y+m_Xm_Y\\ &=\boxed{E[XY]-E[X]\,E[Y]} \end{aligned}$$

    Compare with $\Var[X]=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2$. Covariance is the two-variable version, and $\Cov(X,X)=\Var[X]$. In numericals, always compute $E[X]$, $E[Y]$, $E[XY]$ separately, then use this formula.

    Orthogonal and uncorrelated (lecturer)
    • $X,Y$ are orthogonal if $E[XY]=0$ (zero correlation).
    • $X,Y$ are uncorrelated if $\Cov(X,Y)=0$. Equivalently $E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]$. Equivalently $\rho=0$.

    If either mean is 0, the two notions coincide, because then $E[X]E[Y]=0$. Beware the vocabulary trap: zero correlation ($E[XY]=0$) means orthogonal, while uncorrelated means zero covariance. Read exam questions carefully.

    Theorem: independent ⇒ uncorrelated (lecturer)

    Proof. If $X,Y$ are independent then $f_{XY}=f_Xf_Y$, and the double integral splits into a product of two single integrals:

    $$\begin{aligned} \Cov(X,Y)&=\iint(\alpha-m_X)(\beta-m_Y)\,f_X(\alpha)\,f_Y(\beta)\,d\alpha\,d\beta\\ &=\left[\int(\alpha-m_X)\,f_X(\alpha)\,d\alpha\right]\cdot\left[\int(\beta-m_Y)\,f_Y(\beta)\,d\beta\right] &&\why{the integrand separates}\\ &=\big(m_X-m_X\big)\cdot\big(m_Y-m_Y\big)\\ &=0 . \end{aligned}$$

    The same splitting shows that for independent RVs, $E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]$, and more generally $E[g(X)h(Y)]=E[g(X)]\,E[h(Y)]$. ∎

    INDEPENDENT ⟹ UNCORRELATED, but uncorrelated ⟹̸ independent
    (except for jointly Gaussian RVs, §7)

    Why doesn't the converse hold? Independence says nothing about $Y$ changes when you learn $X$. Zero covariance only says there is no straight-line trend. $Y$ can depend on $X$ in a curved or symmetric way that averages out in the covariance. The next example is the standard demonstration.

    MediumExample 6.1 (lecturer): uncorrelated but not independent

    $\Theta\sim U[0,2\pi]$, $Y=\cos\Theta$, $Z=\sin\Theta$. Are $Y,Z$ orthogonal? Uncorrelated? Independent?

    Solution

    Orthogonal? Compute $E[YZ]$ using $\sin\theta\cos\theta=\tfrac12\sin2\theta$:

    $$\begin{aligned} E[YZ]&=E[\sin\Theta\cos\Theta]\\ &=\tfrac12E[\sin2\Theta]\\ &=\frac12\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\sin2\theta\,d\theta &&\why{$f_\Theta=1/2\pi$}\\ &=0 &&\why{two full periods of sine} \end{aligned}$$

    So orthogonal.

    Uncorrelated? $E[Y]=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\theta\,d\theta=0$ and likewise $E[Z]=0$. Then $\Cov(Y,Z)=E[YZ]-E[Y]E[Z]=0-0=0$. So uncorrelated.

    Independent? No. Always $Y^2+Z^2=\cos^2\Theta+\sin^2\Theta=1$. The point $(Y,Z)$ always lies on the unit circle. If you know $Y=1$, then $Z$ must be 0. To prove it formally, find one event that breaks the product rule:

    $$\begin{aligned} P(Y\gt0.9,\ Z\gt0.9)&=0 &&\why{$0.9^2+0.9^2\gt1$: off the circle}\\ P(Y\gt0.9)\,P(Z\gt0.9)&\gt0 . \end{aligned}$$

    Dependent, strongly so: $Z$ is determined by $Y$ up to sign. The relationship is a circle, which has no upward or downward linear trend, so ρ cannot see it.

    EasyExample 6.2 (lecturer): $Y=aX+b$

    Find $m_Y$, $\sigma_Y^2$ and $\rho_{XY}$.

    Solution

    Mean: by linearity, $m_Y=am_X+b$.

    Variance: subtract the means first. The $b$ cancels:

    $$\begin{aligned} Y-m_Y&=(aX+b)-(am_X+b)\\ &=a(X-m_X),\\[4pt] \sigma_Y^2&=E\big[a^2(X-m_X)^2\big]\\ &=a^2\sigma_X^2,\qquad\text{so}\ \ \sigma_Y=|a|\,\sigma_X . \end{aligned}$$

    Covariance and ρ:

    $$\begin{aligned} \Cov(X,Y)&=E\big[(X-m_X)\cdot a(X-m_X)\big]\\ &=a\,\sigma_X^2,\\[4pt] \rho&=\frac{a\,\sigma_X^2}{\sigma_X\cdot|a|\,\sigma_X}\\ &=\frac{a}{|a|}=\begin{cases}+1,&a\gt0\\-1,&a\lt0\end{cases} \end{aligned}$$

    Meaning. An exact straight-line relationship gives $|\rho|=1$. The sign of ρ is the sign of the slope. The size of the slope doesn't matter: $Y=0.001X$ and $Y=1000X$ both give ρ = 1. ρ measures how close the points are to some line, not how steep the line is.

    Theorem: $|\rho_{XY}|\le1$ (lecturer's proof)

    The trick. Build a new RV that we know cannot have a negative mean square. For any real number λ, let

    $$Z=(X-m_X)+\lambda(Y-m_Y).$$

    Since $Z^2\ge0$ always, its mean is ≥ 0 for every λ. Expand it:

    $$\begin{aligned} 0\le E[Z^2]&=E\big[(X-m_X)^2\big]+2\lambda\,E\big[(X-m_X)(Y-m_Y)\big]+\lambda^2E\big[(Y-m_Y)^2\big]\\ &=\sigma_X^2+2\lambda\,\Cov(X,Y)+\lambda^2\sigma_Y^2\\ &=\sigma_X^2+2\lambda\,\rho\,\sigma_X\sigma_Y+\lambda^2\sigma_Y^2 &&\why{$\Cov=\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y$} \end{aligned}$$

    Read it as a quadratic in λ. It is an upward-opening parabola, $A\lambda^2+B\lambda+C$ with $A=\sigma_Y^2$, $B=2\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y$, $C=\sigma_X^2$. It never dips below zero, so it touches the axis at most once. That means its discriminant is ≤ 0:

    $$\begin{aligned} B^2-4AC&\le0\\ 4\rho^2\sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2-4\sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2&\le0\\ \rho^2&\le1\\ |\rho|&\le1 . \end{aligned}$$

    Written in terms of covariance, $\{\Cov(X,Y)\}^2\le\sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2$: this is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.

    When is $|\rho|=1$? Exactly when the discriminant is 0, so the parabola touches zero at some λ. Then $E[Z^2]=0$, so $Z=0$, so $X-m_X=-\lambda(Y-m_Y)$: $X$ and $Y$ lie exactly on a line (the lecturer's "completely correlated case"). ∎

    What ρ means (asked in MS 2025 and ES July 2024)
    • ρ measures the strength of the linear relationship, on a scale from −1 to +1.
    • ρ has no units. It is unchanged if you shift $X$ or $Y$, or rescale either by a positive factor (volts to millivolts, say).
    • $\rho\approx+1$: points cluster tightly around a rising line. $\rho\approx-1$: around a falling line. $\rho=0$: no linear trend, though there may still be a curved relationship (Example 6.1).
    • Covariance has the same sign as ρ, but its size depends on units (volts × amperes…). That makes its size hard to interpret, which is why we divide by $\sigma_X\sigma_Y$.
    Variance of a sum $$\Var[aX+bY]=a^2\sigma_X^2+b^2\sigma_Y^2+2ab\,\Cov(X,Y).$$

    Proof. Let $\tilde X=X-m_X$ and $\tilde Y=Y-m_Y$. By linearity, the deviation of $aX+bY$ from its mean is $a\tilde X+b\tilde Y$. Square it and take expectations:

    $$\begin{aligned} \Var[aX+bY]&=E\big[(a\tilde X+b\tilde Y)^2\big]\\ &=E\big[a^2\tilde X^2+2ab\,\tilde X\tilde Y+b^2\tilde Y^2\big]\\ &=a^2E[\tilde X^2]+2ab\,E[\tilde X\tilde Y]+b^2E[\tilde Y^2] &&\why{linearity}\\ &=a^2\sigma_X^2+2ab\,\Cov(X,Y)+b^2\sigma_Y^2 . \end{aligned}$$

    Important special cases.

    • If $X,Y$ are uncorrelated (in particular, independent), the cross term vanishes: $\Var[X+Y]=\sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2$, and also $\Var[X-Y]=\sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2$. Variances add even for a difference, because $b=-1$ gives $b^2=+1$. Subtracting two noisy measurements makes the result noisier, not cleaner.
    • For $N$ uncorrelated RVs: $\Var\big[\sum X_i\big]=\sum\Var[X_i]$.
    • Covariance behaves like multiplication: $\Cov(aX+b,\ cY+d)=ac\,\Cov(X,Y)$ (constants added don't matter), and $\Cov(X,\ Y+Z)=\Cov(X,Y)+\Cov(X,Z)$.
    MediumExample 6.3: ρ for $f=\alpha+\beta$ on the unit square

    Using Example 4.1, find $\Cov(X,Y)$ and ρ.

    Solution

    Step 1: moments of X from the marginal $f_X(\alpha)=\alpha+\frac12$:

    $$\begin{aligned} E[X]&=\int_0^1\alpha\big(\alpha+\tfrac12\big)\,d\alpha=\frac13+\frac14=\frac7{12},\\ E[X^2]&=\int_0^1\alpha^2\big(\alpha+\tfrac12\big)\,d\alpha=\frac14+\frac16=\frac5{12},\\ \sigma_X^2&=\frac5{12}-\Big(\frac7{12}\Big)^2=\frac{60-49}{144}=\frac{11}{144}. \end{aligned}$$

    By symmetry $Y$ has the same mean and variance.

    Step 2: the correlation $E[XY]$ needs the joint PDF. On a square, each term splits into a product of single integrals:

    $$\begin{aligned} E[XY]&=\int_0^1\int_0^1\alpha\beta(\alpha+\beta)\,d\alpha\,d\beta\\ &=\int_0^1\int_0^1\big(\alpha^2\beta+\alpha\beta^2\big)\,d\alpha\,d\beta\\ &=\frac13\cdot\frac12+\frac12\cdot\frac13\\ &=\frac13 . \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3: covariance and ρ.

    $$\begin{aligned} \Cov(X,Y)&=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]\\ &=\frac13-\frac{49}{144}\\ &=-\frac1{144},\\[4pt] \rho&=\frac{-1/144}{\sqrt{\frac{11}{144}\cdot\frac{11}{144}}}\\ &=\mathbf{-\frac1{11}}\approx-0.091 . \end{aligned}$$

    Meaning. A weak negative relationship. The density is lowest at (0,0) and highest at (1,1), which would suggest a positive trend; but it is also fairly high at (1,0) and (0,1), and those "opposite" corners win slightly.

    MediumExample 6.4: from a joint PMF table

    $P(0,0)=0.2$, $P(0,1)=0.3$, $P(1,0)=0.3$, $P(1,1)=0.2$. Find ρ.

    Solution

    Marginals: each row and each column sums to 0.5. So $X$ and $Y$ are each Bernoulli(0.5), with mean 0.5 and variance $0.5\times0.5=0.25$.

    Correlation: $XY$ is 1 only in the cell (1,1), so $E[XY]=1\times1\times0.2=0.2$.

    $$\begin{aligned} \Cov&=0.2-0.5\times0.5=-0.05,\\ \rho&=\frac{-0.05}{\sqrt{0.25\times0.25}}=\mathbf{-0.2}. \end{aligned}$$

    Meaning. If $X,Y$ were independent, each cell would be $0.5\times0.5=0.25$. Here the "matching" cells (0,0) and (1,1) have less than that, 0.2 each, and the mismatching cells have more. So $X$ and $Y$ tend to differ: negative correlation.

    Recipe: every covariance / ρ question in five numbers

    Make this table first, then nothing else can go wrong:

    NumberFrom
    $E[X]$, $E[X^2]$the marginal $f_X$ (or row sums), single integrals
    $E[Y]$, $E[Y^2]$the marginal $f_Y$ (or column sums)
    $E[XY]$the joint PDF/PMF: $\iint xy\,f_{XY}$ over the support (the only double integral)

    Then: $\sigma_X^2=E[X^2]-E[X]^2$, $\sigma_Y^2=E[Y^2]-E[Y]^2$, $\Cov=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]$, $\rho=\Cov/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y)$. Sanity checks: variances > 0, $|\rho|\le1$, and the sign of ρ should match the tilt of the support/cloud.

    Shortcuts: if $X,Y$ are independent, $\Cov=0$ without any integral. If $Y=aX+b$, $\rho=\pm1$. In a table, $E[XY]$ only needs the cells where both $x\ne0$ and $y\ne0$.

    MediumDrill T, part 4: covariance and correlation coefficient

    For $f_{XY}=2(x+y)$ on $0\lt y\lt x\lt1$, find $\Cov(X,Y)$ and $\rho_{XY}$.

    Solution

    Numbers 1–2, from $f_X=3x^2$:

    $$E[X]=\int_0^13x^3dx=\frac34,\qquad E[X^2]=\int_0^13x^4dx=\frac35,\qquad\sigma_X^2=\frac35-\frac9{16}=\frac3{80}.$$

    Numbers 3–4, from $f_Y=1+2y-3y^2$:

    $$\begin{aligned} E[Y]&=\int_0^1(y+2y^2-3y^3)\,dy=\tfrac12+\tfrac23-\tfrac34=\tfrac5{12},\\ E[Y^2]&=\int_0^1(y^2+2y^3-3y^4)\,dy=\tfrac13+\tfrac12-\tfrac35=\tfrac7{30},\\ \sigma_Y^2&=\tfrac7{30}-\big(\tfrac5{12}\big)^2=\tfrac{168-125}{720}=\tfrac{43}{720}. \end{aligned}$$

    Number 5, the joint one (vertical strips, $y$ from 0 to $x$):

    $$\begin{aligned} E[XY]&=\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{x}xy\cdot2(x+y)\,dy\,dx\\ &=\int_0^12x\Big[\frac{xy^2}{2}+\frac{y^3}{3}\Big]_0^{x}dx &&\why{inner: $\int(xy+y^2)\,dy$, with $x$ fixed}\\ &=\int_0^12x\cdot\frac{5x^3}{6}\,dx=\frac53\int_0^1x^4\,dx=\frac13 . \end{aligned}$$

    Assemble.

    $$\begin{aligned} \Cov(X,Y)&=\frac13-\frac34\cdot\frac5{12}=\frac{16}{48}-\frac{15}{48}=\mathbf{\frac1{48}},\\ \rho&=\frac{1/48}{\sqrt{\frac3{80}\cdot\frac{43}{720}}}=\frac{1/48}{\sqrt{129}/240}=\frac{5}{\sqrt{129}}\approx\mathbf{0.44}. \end{aligned}$$

    Does it make sense? Positive, moderate. The support lies under the diagonal: small $x$ forces small $y$, large $x$ allows large $y$. So big $X$ tends to go with big $Y$, a rising trend, but with plenty of scatter. You can also see it in part 3: $E[Y\mid X=x]=\frac59x$ rises with $x$.

    7Jointly Gaussian random variables

    Why this case is special

    Noise samples in a receiver, measurement errors in two directions, the outputs of a linear filter driven by noise: these are all jointly Gaussian. And in this one family everything is easy:

    • the whole joint PDF is fixed by just five numbers: $m_X,\ m_Y,\ \sigma_X,\ \sigma_Y,\ \rho$;
    • every marginal, every conditional and every linear combination is again Gaussian;
    • uncorrelated means independent, unlike the general case.
    Bivariate Gaussian PDF (lecturer: "the multi-dimensional Gaussian law")

    Write the standardised values as $a=\dfrac{\alpha-\mu_1}{\sigma_1}$ and $b=\dfrac{\beta-\mu_2}{\sigma_2}$ (how many standard deviations each coordinate is from its mean). Then

    $$f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{a^2-2\rho ab+b^2}{2(1-\rho^2)}\right\}.$$

    Written out in full:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}\exp\Bigg\{-\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^2)}\bigg[&\Big(\frac{\alpha-\mu_1}{\sigma_1}\Big)^2\\ &-2\rho\Big(\frac{\alpha-\mu_1}{\sigma_1}\Big)\Big(\frac{\beta-\mu_2}{\sigma_2}\Big)\\ &+\Big(\frac{\beta-\mu_2}{\sigma_2}\Big)^2\bigg]\Bigg\} \end{aligned}$$

    We write $(X,Y)\sim N(\mu_1,\mu_2,\sigma_1^2,\sigma_2^2,\rho)$, with $|\rho|\lt1$. The parameters are exactly what their names suggest: $\mu_1=E[X]$, $\sigma_1^2=\Var X$, $\mu_2,\sigma_2^2$ the same for $Y$, and $\rho=\rho_{XY}$.

    What the hill looks like. The exponent is constant along curves where $a^2-2\rho ab+b^2$ is constant. Those curves are ellipses centred at $(\mu_1,\mu_2)$, so the hill's contour lines are ellipses:

    • $\rho=0$ (and $\sigma_1=\sigma_2$): circles. No preferred direction.
    • $\rho\gt0$: ellipses tilted along the line $\beta=\alpha$. Big $X$ goes with big $Y$.
    • $\rho\lt0$: tilted along $\beta=-\alpha$.
    • The closer $|\rho|$ gets to 1, the thinner the ellipses. In the limit the hill collapses onto a line.
    Theorem: the marginals are Gaussian (lecturer's derivation)

    Goal: show $f_X(\alpha)=\int f_{XY}\,d\beta$ is the $N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)$ PDF.

    Step 1: complete the square in $b$. We want to integrate over β, i.e. over $b$. So rewrite the bracket so that $b$ appears in only one squared term:

    $$\begin{aligned} a^2-2\rho ab+b^2&=\big(b^2-2\rho ab+\rho^2a^2\big)+a^2-\rho^2a^2 &&\why{add and subtract $\rho^2a^2$}\\ &=(b-\rho a)^2+(1-\rho^2)\,a^2 . \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2: split the exponent. Divide by $2(1-\rho^2)$:

    $$-\frac{a^2-2\rho ab+b^2}{2(1-\rho^2)}=-\frac{a^2}{2}-\frac{(b-\rho a)^2}{2(1-\rho^2)} .$$

    The first part does not involve β, so its exponential comes out of the β-integral.

    Step 3: integrate. Split the constant $\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}$ as $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_1}\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(1-\rho^2)}\,\sigma_2}$:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_X(\alpha)&=\frac{e^{-a^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(1-\rho^2)}\,\sigma_2}\exp\Big\{-\frac{(b-\rho a)^2}{2(1-\rho^2)}\Big\}\,d\beta\\ &=\frac{e^{-a^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(1-\rho^2)}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{(b-\rho a)^2}{2(1-\rho^2)}\Big\}}_{\text{PDF of }N(\rho a,\ 1-\rho^2)\text{ in }b}\,db &&\why{$d\beta=\sigma_2\,db$}\\ &=\frac{e^{-a^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_1}\times1 &&\why{a PDF integrates to 1}\\ &=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_1}\exp\Big\{-\frac{(\alpha-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^2}\Big\}. \end{aligned}$$

    So $X\sim N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)$, and likewise $Y\sim N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)$. ∎

    Bonus: the conditional distribution Beyond class notes

    The same algebra gives the conditional PDF for free. $f_{Y|X}=f_{XY}/f_X$ is exactly the leftover Gaussian under the brace above. Converting $b-\rho a$ back to β:

    $$Y\mid X=\alpha\ \sim\ N\Big(\mu_2+\rho\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}(\alpha-\mu_1),\ \ \sigma_2^2(1-\rho^2)\Big).$$

    Meaning.

    • The conditional mean is a straight line in α (the regression line). If $X$ is one standard deviation above its mean, we expect $Y$ to be ρ standard deviations above its own.
    • Observing $X$ shrinks the variance of $Y$ by the factor $1-\rho^2$. With ρ = 0 you learn nothing; with $|\rho|\to1$ you learn $Y$ almost exactly.
    Observations (lecturer)
    1. Given $f_{XY}$ we can always obtain $f_X$ and $f_Y$.
    2. Given $f_X$ and $f_Y$, we may or may not be able to obtain $f_{XY}$.
    3. For jointly Gaussian RVs, the marginals plus ρ give $f_{XY}$.
    4. ρ = 0 ⇒ $f_{XY}=f_Xf_Y$ ⇒ $X,Y$ are independent. To see it, put ρ = 0 into the PDF: $$\begin{aligned} f_{XY}&=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2}\exp\Big\{-\frac{a^2+b^2}{2}\Big\}\\ &=\frac{e^{-a^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_1}\cdot\frac{e^{-b^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_2}\\ &=f_X(\alpha)\,f_Y(\beta). \end{aligned}$$ The cross term $-2\rho ab$ was the only thing linking α and β. Remove it and the PDF factors. So for jointly Gaussian RVs, uncorrelated ⇔ independent.

    Careful: this needs $X,Y$ to be jointly Gaussian. Two RVs can each be Gaussian on their own, be uncorrelated, and still be dependent if their joint PDF is not of this form.

    Random vectors and the covariance matrix

    With more than two RVs, writing out all the cross terms gets unmanageable. Matrices keep it tidy.

    Mean vector and covariance matrix (lecturer)

    For $\mathbf X=[X_1\ X_2\ \cdots\ X_N]^T$, the mean vector is $\boldsymbol\mu_X=[\mu_1\ \cdots\ \mu_N]^T$, and the covariance matrix is

    $$\mathbf K_X=E\big[(\mathbf X-\boldsymbol\mu_X)(\mathbf X-\boldsymbol\mu_X)^T\big].$$

    The column vector times the row vector is an $N\times N$ matrix. Its $(i,j)$ entry is $E[(X_i-\mu_i)(X_j-\mu_j)]=\Cov(X_i,X_j)$. So:

    $$\mathbf K_X=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1^2&\Cov(X_1,X_2)&\cdots&\Cov(X_1,X_N)\\\Cov(X_2,X_1)&\sigma_2^2&\cdots&\Cov(X_2,X_N)\\\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\\Cov(X_N,X_1)&\cdots&&\sigma_N^2\end{bmatrix}$$

    with $\Cov(X_i,X_j)=\rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j$. Variances sit on the diagonal, and the matrix is symmetric because $\Cov(X_i,X_j)=\Cov(X_j,X_i)$.

    $\mathbf X$ is jointly Gaussian, written $\mathbf X\sim N(\boldsymbol\mu_X,\mathbf K_X)$, if

    $$f_{\mathbf X}(\mathbf x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N/2}\,|\mathbf K_X|^{1/2}}\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu_X)^T\mathbf K_X^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu_X)\Big\}.$$

    Check that $N=2$ gives back the bivariate formula.

    $$\begin{aligned} \mathbf K&=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1^2&\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\\\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2&\sigma_2^2\end{bmatrix},\\[4pt] |\mathbf K|&=\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2-\rho^2\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2=\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2(1-\rho^2),\\[4pt] \mathbf K^{-1}&=\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2(1-\rho^2)}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_2^2&-\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\\-\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2&\sigma_1^2\end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned}$$

    So $(2\pi)^{N/2}|\mathbf K|^{1/2}=2\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2\sqrt{1-\rho^2}$, matching the constant. Multiplying out the quadratic form $(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^T\mathbf K^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)$ gives $\frac{a^2-2\rho ab+b^2}{1-\rho^2}$, matching the exponent.

    If all $\rho_{ij}=0$, $\mathbf K_X$ is diagonal, the quadratic form is a plain sum of squares, and $f_{\mathbf X}=f_{X_1}f_{X_2}\cdots f_{X_N}$ (lecturer).

    Theorem: linear transformation of a Gaussian vector (lecturer)

    If $\mathbf X\sim N(\boldsymbol\mu_X,\mathbf K_X)$ and $\mathbf V=A\mathbf X$ for a constant matrix $A$, then $\mathbf V$ is jointly Gaussian with

    $$\boldsymbol\mu_V=A\boldsymbol\mu_X,\qquad\mathbf K_V=A\,\mathbf K_X\,A^T .$$

    Proof of the mean. Each entry of $A\mathbf X$ is a linear combination of the $X_i$, so linearity gives $E[A\mathbf X]=AE[\mathbf X]$.

    Proof of the covariance.

    $$\begin{aligned} \mathbf V-\boldsymbol\mu_V&=A\mathbf X-A\boldsymbol\mu_X=A(\mathbf X-\boldsymbol\mu_X),\\[4pt] \mathbf K_V&=E\big[(\mathbf V-\boldsymbol\mu_V)(\mathbf V-\boldsymbol\mu_V)^T\big]\\ &=E\big[A(\mathbf X-\boldsymbol\mu_X)\,(\mathbf X-\boldsymbol\mu_X)^TA^T\big] &&\why{$(AB)^T=B^TA^T$}\\ &=A\,E\big[(\mathbf X-\boldsymbol\mu_X)(\mathbf X-\boldsymbol\mu_X)^T\big]\,A^T &&\why{constants come out}\\ &=A\,\mathbf K_X\,A^T . \end{aligned}$$

    These two formulas hold for any random vector. What is special to Gaussians is that $\mathbf V$ is again Gaussian, so these numbers describe it completely. (That part is proved with the MGF in §8.)

    Scalar special case: $W=aX+bY$ (lecturer)

    If $X,Y$ are jointly Gaussian, $W=aX+bY$ is Gaussian with

    $$\begin{aligned} \mu_W&=a\mu_X+b\mu_Y,\\ \sigma_W^2&=a^2\sigma_X^2+b^2\sigma_Y^2+2ab\,\rho\,\sigma_X\sigma_Y,\\ f_W(\theta)&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_W}\exp\Big\{-\frac{(\theta-\mu_W)^2}{2\sigma_W^2}\Big\}. \end{aligned}$$

    This is $A=[a\ \ b]$ in the theorem, or simply the variance-of-a-sum formula of §6.

    ExamExample 7.1 (lecturer): a linear transformation

    $(X_1,X_2)\sim N(0,0,1,1,\rho)$ with $\rho=\frac12$. Find the joint PDF of $Y_1=2X_1+3X_2$ and $Y_2=-X_1+2X_2$; in particular, find the correlation coefficient of $Y_1,Y_2$.

    Solution

    Step 1: write everything as matrices.

    $$\boldsymbol\mu_X=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf K_X=\begin{bmatrix}1&\frac12\\\frac12&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad A=\begin{bmatrix}2&3\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}.$$

    $\mathbf K_X$ has variances 1 on the diagonal and $\Cov=\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2=\frac12$ off it. The rows of $A$ are the coefficients of $Y_1$ and $Y_2$.

    Step 2: mean. $\boldsymbol\mu_Y=A\mathbf0=\mathbf0$.

    Step 3: covariance. Multiply $A\mathbf K_X$ first, then by $A^T$:

    $$\begin{aligned} A\mathbf K_X&=\begin{bmatrix}2&3\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&\frac12\\\frac12&1\end{bmatrix}\\ &=\begin{bmatrix}2(1)+3(\frac12)&2(\frac12)+3(1)\\-1(1)+2(\frac12)&-1(\frac12)+2(1)\end{bmatrix}\\ &=\begin{bmatrix}\frac72&4\\0&\frac32\end{bmatrix},\\[8pt] \mathbf K_Y=A\mathbf K_XA^T&=\begin{bmatrix}\frac72&4\\0&\frac32\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\3&2\end{bmatrix}\\ &=\begin{bmatrix}7+12&-\frac72+8\\0+\frac92&0+3\end{bmatrix}\\ &=\begin{bmatrix}19&\frac92\\\frac92&3\end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4: read off the parameters. $\sigma_{Y_1}^2=19$, $\sigma_{Y_2}^2=3$, $\Cov(Y_1,Y_2)=\frac92$. Then

    $$\begin{aligned} \rho_{Y_1Y_2}&=\frac{\Cov(Y_1,Y_2)}{\sigma_{Y_1}\sigma_{Y_2}}\\ &=\frac{9/2}{\sqrt{19}\,\sqrt3}\\ &=\frac{9}{2\sqrt{57}}\approx0.596 . \end{aligned}$$

    So $(Y_1,Y_2)\sim N\big(0,\,0,\,19,\,3,\,\frac{9}{2\sqrt{57}}\big)$. Substitute these into the bivariate formula to write out the PDF.

    Sanity check with the scalar formula for $Y_1$:

    $$\sigma_{Y_1}^2=2^2(1)+3^2(1)+2(2)(3)\big(\tfrac12\big)(1)(1)=4+9+6=19\ ✓$$
    Recipe: jointly Gaussian numericals

    You almost never integrate the bivariate PDF. Instead, turn every question into a question about one Gaussian RV:

    1. Write down the five parameters $\mu_X,\mu_Y,\sigma_X^2,\sigma_Y^2,\rho$ and compute $\Cov=\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y$.
    2. Probability about a combination (e.g. $P(aX+bY\gt c)$): let $W=aX+bY$. It is Gaussian with $\mu_W=a\mu_X+b\mu_Y$, $\sigma_W^2=a^2\sigma_X^2+b^2\sigma_Y^2+2ab\,\Cov$. Standardise: $P(W\gt c)=Q\big(\frac{c-\mu_W}{\sigma_W}\big)$.
    3. Probability given $X=x$: $Y\mid X=x$ is Gaussian with mean $\mu_Y+\rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x-\mu_X)$ and variance $\sigma_Y^2(1-\rho^2)$. Standardise.
    4. "Make them independent": for jointly Gaussian RVs, zero covariance is enough. Set the covariance to 0 and solve.
    5. Several outputs ($\mathbf Y=A\mathbf X$): $\mathbf K_Y=A\mathbf K_XA^T$, then read variances and covariances off $\mathbf K_Y$.
    MediumExample 7.2: three standard questions on one Gaussian pair

    $(X,Y)$ jointly Gaussian with $\mu_X=1$, $\mu_Y=2$, $\sigma_X^2=4$, $\sigma_Y^2=9$, $\rho=0.5$. Find (a) $P(X+Y\gt5)$; (b) $P(Y\gt2\mid X=3)$; (c) the constant $a$ that makes $X$ and $Y-aX$ independent.

    Solution

    Step 1. $\sigma_X=2$, $\sigma_Y=3$, $\Cov(X,Y)=\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y=0.5\times2\times3=3$.

    (a) $W=X+Y$ is Gaussian:

    $$\begin{aligned} \mu_W&=1+2=3,\\ \sigma_W^2&=4+9+2(3)=19 &&\why{$a=b=1$: $\sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2+2\Cov$}\\ P(W\gt5)&=Q\Big(\frac{5-3}{\sqrt{19}}\Big)=Q(0.459)\approx\mathbf{0.323}. \end{aligned}$$

    Note that if $X,Y$ were independent, $\sigma_W^2$ would be 13, not 19: the positive correlation makes the sum more spread out.

    (b) Given $X=3$ (one σ above its mean, since $\frac{3-1}{2}=1$):

    $$\begin{aligned} E[Y\mid X=3]&=2+0.5\cdot\frac32\,(3-1)=2+1.5=3.5,\\ \Var[Y\mid X=3]&=9(1-0.25)=6.75,\quad\text{s.d.}=2.598,\\ P(Y\gt2\mid X=3)&=P\Big(Z\gt\frac{2-3.5}{2.598}\Big)=P(Z\gt-0.577)\\ &=1-Q(0.577)\approx\mathbf{0.718}. \end{aligned}$$

    Without the information $X=3$, $P(Y\gt2)$ would be exactly 0.5 (2 is its mean). Learning that $X$ is high pushes our expectation of $Y$ up (ρ > 0), so the probability rises to 0.72.

    (c) $X$ and $Y-aX$ are jointly Gaussian (a linear transformation of a Gaussian pair), so independence ⇔ zero covariance:

    $$\begin{aligned} \Cov(X,\ Y-aX)&=\Cov(X,Y)-a\,\Var(X)\\ &=3-4a=0\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf{a=\tfrac34}. \end{aligned}$$

    Meaning: $\frac34X$ is the part of $Y$ that can be "explained" by $X$ (the regression slope $\Cov/\sigma_X^2$); what is left, $Y-\frac34X$, has nothing to do with $X$.

    8Joint MGF, characteristic function & sums of RVs

    Idea

    Finding the PDF of a sum directly needs a convolution, which is messy (§10). The MGF turns convolution into multiplication: for independent RVs, the MGF of the sum is the product of the MGFs. And since an MGF identifies its distribution uniquely (Unit 2), recognising the product tells you the distribution of the sum without doing any convolution.

    MGF of a sum of independent RVs (lecturer)

    Recall $M_X(t)=E[e^{tX}]=\int e^{t\alpha}f_X(\alpha)\,d\alpha$ and $\Phi_X(\omega)=E[e^{j\omega X}]$. If $X$ and $Y$ are independent:

    $$\begin{aligned} M_{X+Y}(t)&=E\big[e^{t(X+Y)}\big]\\ &=E\big[e^{tX}\,e^{tY}\big] &&\why{$e^{u+v}=e^ue^v$}\\ &=\iint e^{t\alpha}e^{t\beta}\,f_X(\alpha)\,f_Y(\beta)\,d\alpha\,d\beta &&\why{independence}\\ &=\int e^{t\alpha}f_X(\alpha)\,d\alpha\cdot\int e^{t\beta}f_Y(\beta)\,d\beta &&\why{the integral separates}\\ &=M_X(t)\,M_Y(t). \end{aligned}$$

    For $N$ independent RVs, $M_{\sum X_i}(t)=\prod_iM_{X_i}(t)$. For iid RVs, it is $[M_X(t)]^N$.

    MediumExample 8.0: the Gaussian case, worked (lecturer)

    $X\sim N(\mu_X,\sigma_X^2)$ and $Y\sim N(\mu_Y,\sigma_Y^2)$ are independent. Find the distribution of $Z=X+Y$.

    Solution

    The Gaussian MGF is $M(t)=e^{\mu t+\sigma^2t^2/2}$. Multiply, and add the exponents:

    $$\begin{aligned} M_Z(t)&=M_X(t)\,M_Y(t)\\ &=e^{\mu_Xt+\sigma_X^2t^2/2}\cdot e^{\mu_Yt+\sigma_Y^2t^2/2}\\ &=\exp\Big\{(\mu_X+\mu_Y)\,t+\frac{(\sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2)\,t^2}{2}\Big\}. \end{aligned}$$

    This has exactly the Gaussian form, with mean $\mu_X+\mu_Y$ and variance $\sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2$. By uniqueness:

    $$Z\sim N\big(\mu_X+\mu_Y,\ \sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2\big).$$

    Lecturer's slogan: the sum of two independent Gaussian RVs is always Gaussian; mean = sum of means, variance = sum of variances.

    Trap: if $Y,Z$ are iid $N(m,\sigma^2)$, then $Y+Z\sim N(2m,2\sigma^2)$. That is not the same as $2Y\sim N(2m,4\sigma^2)$. Two independent errors partly cancel; doubling one error doesn't.

    Joint MGF (lecturer) $$M_{XY}(t_1,t_2)=E\big[e^{t_1X+t_2Y}\big]=\iint e^{t_1\alpha+t_2\beta}\,f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)\,d\alpha\,d\beta .$$

    It packs all the joint moments into one function, just as $M_X(t)$ packed all the moments of $X$.

    How the moments come out (lecturer). Expand the exponential as a power series, $e^u=1+u+\frac{u^2}{2!}+\cdots$, with $u=t_1X+t_2Y$:

    $$\begin{aligned} M_{XY}(t_1,t_2)&=E\Big[1+(t_1X+t_2Y)+\frac{(t_1X+t_2Y)^2}{2!}+\cdots\Big]\\ &=1+t_1E[X]+t_2E[Y]+\frac12E\big[t_1^2X^2+2t_1t_2XY+t_2^2Y^2\big]+\cdots\\ &=1+t_1E[X]+t_2E[Y]+\frac{t_1^2}{2}E[X^2]+t_1t_2\,E[XY]+\frac{t_2^2}{2}E[Y^2]+\cdots \end{aligned}$$

    Each moment is the coefficient of one term. Differentiating and setting $t_1=t_2=0$ isolates it, because every other term either differentiates to zero or still contains a $t$:

    $$\frac{\partial M_{XY}}{\partial t_1}\Big|_{\mathbf0}=E[X],\qquad\frac{\partial^2M_{XY}}{\partial t_1\,\partial t_2}\Big|_{\mathbf0}=E[XY],\qquad\frac{\partial^{i+j}M_{XY}}{\partial t_1^i\,\partial t_2^j}\Big|_{\mathbf0}=E[X^iY^j].$$
    Joint MGF facts
    • Independence: $X,Y$ independent ⇔ $M_{XY}(t_1,t_2)=M_X(t_1)\,M_Y(t_2)$. For a vector, $M_{\mathbf X}(\mathbf t)=E[e^{\mathbf t^T\mathbf X}]=\prod_iM_{X_i}(t_i)$ if the components are independent (lecturer).
    • Gaussian vector: $M_{\mathbf X}(\mathbf t)=\exp\big\{\boldsymbol\mu_X^T\mathbf t+\tfrac12\mathbf t^T\mathbf K_X\mathbf t\big\}$ (lecturer).
    • Sum from the joint MGF: $M_{X+Y}(t)=M_{XY}(t,t)$. This works even when $X,Y$ are dependent.
    ExamExample 8.1 (lecturer): sum of two dependent Gaussians

    $(X_1,X_2)$ are jointly Gaussian with means $\mu_1,\mu_2$, variances $\sigma_1^2,\sigma_2^2$, correlation coefficient $\rho_{12}$. Show that $Z=X_1+X_2$ is Gaussian and find its parameters.

    Solution

    Step 1: link $M_Z$ to the joint MGF. Setting both $t$'s equal:

    $$M_Z(t)=E\big[e^{t(X_1+X_2)}\big]=E\big[e^{tX_1+tX_2}\big]=M_{X_1X_2}(t,t).$$

    Step 2: use the Gaussian joint MGF with $\mathbf t=[t\ \ t]^T$ and $\mathbf K=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1^2&K_{12}\\K_{21}&\sigma_2^2\end{bmatrix}$:

    $$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol\mu^T\mathbf t&=\mu_1t+\mu_2t=(\mu_1+\mu_2)\,t,\\[4pt] \mathbf t^T\mathbf K\,\mathbf t&=\begin{bmatrix}t&t\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1^2&K_{12}\\K_{21}&\sigma_2^2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}t\\t\end{bmatrix}\\ &=t^2\big(\sigma_1^2+K_{12}+K_{21}+\sigma_2^2\big) &&\why{sum of all four entries}\\ &=t^2\big(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2+2\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2\big) &&\why{$K_{12}=K_{21}=\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2$} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3: assemble.

    $$M_Z(t)=\exp\Big\{(\mu_1+\mu_2)\,t+\frac{t^2}{2}\big(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2+2\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2\big)\Big\}.$$

    This is the Gaussian MGF $e^{\mu t+\sigma^2t^2/2}$, so by uniqueness $Z$ is Gaussian with (lecturer's observation)

    $$\mu_Z=\mu_1+\mu_2,\qquad\sigma_Z^2=\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2+2\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2 .$$

    Compared with the independent case, the variance gains the cross term $2\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2$. Positive correlation makes the sum more spread out (the two tend to err in the same direction). Negative correlation makes it less spread out (errors cancel). The same argument with $\mathbf t=A^T\mathbf s$ proves the linear-transformation theorem of §7.

    9Functions of one random variable: $Y=g(X)$

    Why this matters

    Every electronic block transforms its input:

    • an amplifier gives $Y=aX+b$;
    • a power meter or square-law detector gives $Y=X^2$;
    • a full-wave rectifier gives $Y=|X|$, and a half-wave rectifier keeps only the positive part;
    • an over-driven amplifier clips its output (a limiter);
    • an analog-to-digital converter rounds to fixed levels (a quantizer).

    If the input $X$ is random with a known PDF, what is the PDF of the output $Y$? The lecturer lists three versions of this problem: (a) $Y=g(X)$, this section; (b) $Y=g(X_1,X_2)$, §10; (c) two outputs $Y_1=g_1(X_1,X_2)$, $Y_2=g_2(X_1,X_2)$, at the end of §10.

    The CDF method (lecturer): works for every $g$ $$\begin{aligned} F_Y(\beta)&=P(Y\le\beta)\\ &=P\big(g(X)\le\beta\big)\\ &=P\big(X\in C_\beta\big),\qquad\text{where }C_\beta=\{\alpha:\ g(\alpha)\le\beta\}. \end{aligned}$$

    In the lecturer's words: "identify the events (line segments) in the sample space of $X$ which result in $Y\le\beta$ after the transformation". Then differentiate: $f_Y(\beta)=\dfrac{dF_Y(\beta)}{d\beta}$.

    The idea in plain words. We can't talk about $Y$ directly, because we only know the distribution of $X$. So translate every question about $Y$ back into a question about $X$. "$Y\le\beta$" happens exactly when $X$ falls in a certain set of α-values, namely those that $g$ sends to heights ≤ β. Find that set, ask the distribution of $X$ for its probability, and you have $F_Y(\beta)$.

    Recipe: PDF of $Y=g(X)$
    1. Sketch $g$ (the transfer curve), with α on the horizontal axis and β on the vertical.
    2. Find the range of $Y$: the heights the curve actually reaches for α in the support of $X$. Below that range $F_Y=0$; above it $F_Y=1$.
    3. Draw a horizontal line at a typical height β. $C_\beta$ is the set of α where the curve is on or below this line. Write it as one or more intervals.
    4. Compute $F_Y(\beta)$ as the probability of those intervals, using $F_X$ or $\int f_X$.
    5. Differentiate to get $f_Y$. If the shape of $C_\beta$ changes as β moves (say, from two intervals to one), handle each range of β separately.
    6. Flat pieces of $g$: if the curve is constant at level $c$ over an interval $I$, all the probability of $I$ lands on the single value $c$. That is a jump in $F_Y$ at $c$, i.e. an impulse $P(X\in I)\,\delta(\beta-c)$ in $f_Y$. Jumps of $g$ do the opposite: they create values $Y$ never takes, so $F_Y$ is flat there.
    7. Check: $f_Y\ge0$, and (area under the density) + (impulse weights) = 1.

    Linear transformation $Y=aX+b$ (lecturer)

    Case $a\gt0$. Solve the inequality for $X$. Dividing by a positive number keeps its direction:

    $$\begin{aligned} F_Y(\beta)&=P(aX+b\le\beta)\\ &=P(aX\le\beta-b)\\ &=P\Big(X\le\frac{\beta-b}{a}\Big)\\ &=F_X\Big(\frac{\beta-b}{a}\Big). \end{aligned}$$

    Case $a\lt0$. Dividing by a negative number flips the inequality:

    $$\begin{aligned} F_Y(\beta)&=P\Big(X\ge\frac{\beta-b}{a}\Big)\\ &=1-F_X\Big(\frac{\beta-b}{a}\Big). \end{aligned}$$

    Differentiate (chain rule; the inner function $\frac{\beta-b}{a}$ has derivative $\frac1a$):

    $$\begin{aligned} a\gt0:&\quad f_Y(\beta)=\frac1a\,f_X\Big(\frac{\beta-b}{a}\Big),\\ a\lt0:&\quad f_Y(\beta)=-\frac1a\,f_X\Big(\frac{\beta-b}{a}\Big). \end{aligned}$$

    In both cases the factor in front is positive: it is $\frac1{|a|}$.

    Linear transformation $$\boxed{f_Y(\beta)=\frac{1}{|a|}\,f_X\Big(\frac{\beta-b}{a}\Big)}$$

    Meaning: the graph of $f_Y$ is the graph of $f_X$ stretched horizontally by $|a|$ and shifted by $b$. Stretching the base by $|a|$ would multiply the area by $|a|$, so the height is divided by $|a|$ to keep the area equal to 1. (Lecturer: "shifted by $b$ and compressed due to $1/a$".)

    EasyExample 9.0 (lecturer): $X\sim U[1,3]$, $Y=2X+3$
    Solution $$\begin{aligned} f_Y(\beta)&=\frac12\,f_X\Big(\frac{\beta-3}{2}\Big). \end{aligned}$$

    $f_X$ equals ½ when its argument is between 1 and 3:

    $$1\le\frac{\beta-3}{2}\le3\iff5\le\beta\le9 .$$

    So $f_Y=\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac14$ on $[5,9]$: $Y\sim U[5,9]$. The interval $[1,3]$ of length 2 was mapped to $[5,9]$ of length 4, and the height halved from ½ to ¼.

    Conclusions (lecturer): a linear function of a uniform RV is uniform; a linear function of a Gaussian RV is Gaussian, $aX+b\sim N(am+b,\ a^2\sigma^2)$. For a decreasing line such as $Y=-2X-5$, the event $\{Y\le\beta\}$ turns into $\{X\ge\alpha\}$ with $\alpha=-(\beta+5)/2$: the region of α flips to the right-hand half-line.

    Monotone $g$: the derivative formula

    Theorem (one-to-one $g$)

    If $g$ is strictly increasing or strictly decreasing and differentiable, with inverse $\alpha=g^{-1}(\beta)$, then

    $$\boxed{f_Y(\beta)=\frac{f_X(\alpha)}{|g'(\alpha)|}\bigg|_{\alpha=g^{-1}(\beta)}=f_X\big(g^{-1}(\beta)\big)\,\Big|\frac{d\alpha}{d\beta}\Big|}$$

    Proof, increasing case. $g(X)\le\beta$ exactly when $X\le g^{-1}(\beta)$:

    $$\begin{aligned} F_Y(\beta)&=P\big(X\le g^{-1}(\beta)\big)=F_X\big(g^{-1}(\beta)\big),\\ f_Y(\beta)&=f_X\big(g^{-1}(\beta)\big)\cdot\frac{d\alpha}{d\beta} &&\why{chain rule; here $d\alpha/d\beta\gt0$} \end{aligned}$$

    Decreasing case. Now $g(X)\le\beta$ exactly when $X\ge g^{-1}(\beta)$:

    $$\begin{aligned} F_Y(\beta)&=1-F_X\big(g^{-1}(\beta)\big),\\ f_Y(\beta)&=-f_X\big(g^{-1}(\beta)\big)\cdot\frac{d\alpha}{d\beta} &&\why{here $d\alpha/d\beta\lt0$, so this is positive} \end{aligned}$$

    Both cases are $f_X\cdot\big|\frac{d\alpha}{d\beta}\big|$, and $\frac{d\alpha}{d\beta}=\frac{1}{g'(\alpha)}$. ∎

    Intuition: probability is conserved. Take a thin strip of width $d\alpha$ around α. It holds probability $f_X(\alpha)\,d\alpha$. The function $g$ maps it to a thin strip of width $d\beta=|g'(\alpha)|\,d\alpha$ around $\beta=g(\alpha)$. The probability doesn't change, it just moves:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Y(\beta)\,|d\beta|&=f_X(\alpha)\,|d\alpha|\\ f_Y(\beta)&=\frac{f_X(\alpha)}{|d\beta/d\alpha|}=\frac{f_X(\alpha)}{|g'(\alpha)|}. \end{aligned}$$

    Where the curve is steep, a narrow α-strip is spread over a wide β-strip, so the output density is low. Where the curve is flat, a wide α-strip is squeezed into a narrow β-strip, so the output density is high. In the extreme case of a perfectly flat piece, everything is squeezed onto one point: an impulse.

    Several roots (non-monotone $g$)

    If the equation $g(\alpha)=\beta$ has several roots $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\dots$, probability arrives at β from each of them, and the contributions add:

    $$f_Y(\beta)=\sum_k\frac{f_X(\alpha_k)}{|g'(\alpha_k)|}.$$

    If there is no root (the curve never reaches height β), then $f_Y(\beta)=0$. This is the quickest method when the curve has no flat parts. When it does have flat parts, use the CDF method, because this formula misses the impulses.

    Square-law device $Y=X^2$ (lecturer)

    β < 0: a square is never negative, so the event $\{Y\le\beta\}$ is impossible and $F_Y(\beta)=0$.

    β ≥ 0: the parabola is below height β exactly between its two crossing points $\pm\sqrt\beta$. So $C_\beta=[-\sqrt\beta,\sqrt\beta]$:

    $$\begin{aligned} F_Y(\beta)&=P\big(-\sqrt\beta\le X\le\sqrt\beta\big)\\ &=F_X(\sqrt\beta)-F_X(-\sqrt\beta). \end{aligned}$$

    Differentiate each term with the Leibniz rule. The inner function $-\sqrt\beta$ has derivative $-\frac{1}{2\sqrt\beta}$, so the minus signs combine into a plus:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Y(\beta)&=f_X(\sqrt\beta)\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt\beta}-f_X(-\sqrt\beta)\cdot\Big(-\frac{1}{2\sqrt\beta}\Big)\\ &=\boxed{\frac{1}{2\sqrt\beta}\Big[f_X(\sqrt\beta)+f_X(-\sqrt\beta)\Big],\quad\beta\gt0} \end{aligned}$$

    Same answer from the roots formula: the roots are $\pm\sqrt\beta$, and at both $|g'(\alpha)|=|2\alpha|=2\sqrt\beta$.

    MediumExample 9.1 (lecturer): $Y=X^2$ for different uniform inputs

    Plot $f_Y$ when (a) $X\sim U[0,2]$, (b) $X\sim U[-2,2]$, (d) $X\sim U[-1,2]$.

    Solution

    In every case, use $f_Y(\beta)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt\beta}\big[f_X(\sqrt\beta)+f_X(-\sqrt\beta)\big]$. The whole job is to ask, for each β, which of the two roots lie inside the support of $X$.

    (a) $X\sim U[0,2]$, so $f_X=\frac12$ on $[0,2]$. The range of $Y$ is $[0,4]$. For $0\lt\beta\lt4$: the root $+\sqrt\beta$ lies in $[0,2]$, while $-\sqrt\beta$ is negative, outside the support.

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Y(\beta)&=\frac{1}{2\sqrt\beta}\Big[\tfrac12+0\Big]\\ &=\frac{1}{4\sqrt\beta},\qquad0\lt\beta\lt4 . \end{aligned}$$

    (b) $X\sim U[-2,2]$, so $f_X=\frac14$. For $0\lt\beta\lt4$ both roots lie in $[-2,2]$:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Y(\beta)&=\frac{1}{2\sqrt\beta}\Big[\tfrac14+\tfrac14\Big]\\ &=\frac{1}{4\sqrt\beta},\qquad0\lt\beta\lt4 . \end{aligned}$$

    The same as (a). Why? Squaring only sees $|X|$. And folding $U[-2,2]$ at zero gives $U[0,2]$: each half has density ¼, and stacking them gives ½.

    (d) $X\sim U[-1,2]$, so $f_X=\frac13$ on $[-1,2]$. Now the answer depends on β:

    • $0\lt\beta\lt1$: $\sqrt\beta\lt1$, so both $+\sqrt\beta$ and $-\sqrt\beta$ lie in $[-1,2]$. Two roots count.
    • $1\lt\beta\lt4$: $-\sqrt\beta\lt-1$ falls outside the support. Only $+\sqrt\beta$ counts.
    $$\begin{aligned} 0\lt\beta\lt1:&\quad f_Y(\beta)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt\beta}\Big[\tfrac13+\tfrac13\Big]=\frac{1}{3\sqrt\beta},\\[4pt] 1\lt\beta\lt4:&\quad f_Y(\beta)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt\beta}\Big[\tfrac13+0\Big]=\frac{1}{6\sqrt\beta}. \end{aligned}$$

    Check (d) integrates to 1, using $\int\frac{d\beta}{\sqrt\beta}=2\sqrt\beta$:

    $$\begin{aligned} \int_0^1\frac{d\beta}{3\sqrt\beta}+\int_1^4\frac{d\beta}{6\sqrt\beta}&=\frac13\Big[2\sqrt\beta\Big]_0^1+\frac16\Big[2\sqrt\beta\Big]_1^4\\ &=\frac23+\frac16(4-2)\\ &=\frac23+\frac13=1\ ✓ \end{aligned}$$

    Why does $f_Y$ shoot up near 0? The parabola is flat at the bottom. A wide band of α near 0 is squeezed into a tiny band of β near 0, so the density there is huge. It is infinite at β = 0, but the area stays finite. A density can be unbounded.

    MediumExample 9.2 (lecturer): Gaussian input → chi-square

    $X\sim N(0,1)$ and $Y=X^2$. Find $f_Y$.

    Solution

    The standard Gaussian PDF is even, so both roots give the same value:

    $$f_X(\sqrt\beta)=f_X(-\sqrt\beta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\beta/2}.$$

    Substitute into the square-law formula:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Y(\beta)&=\frac{1}{2\sqrt\beta}\cdot2\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\beta/2}\\ &=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\beta}}\,e^{-\beta/2},\qquad\beta\gt0 . \end{aligned}$$

    This is the chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom, written $\chi^2_1$. It describes the instantaneous power of Gaussian noise. Its mean is $E[Y]=E[X^2]=1$.

    Full-wave rectifier $Y=|X|$ (lecturer)

    β < 0: $|X|$ is never negative, so $F_Y(\beta)=0$.

    β ≥ 0: the V-shaped curve is below height β between $-\beta$ and $\beta$:

    $$\begin{aligned} F_Y(\beta)&=P(-\beta\le X\le\beta)\\ &=F_X(\beta)-F_X(-\beta),\\[4pt] f_Y(\beta)&=f_X(\beta)+f_X(-\beta),\qquad\beta\gt0 &&\why{Leibniz; the two minus signs cancel} \end{aligned}$$

    Picture: fold the negative half of $f_X$ over onto the positive half, and add the two layers.

    EasyExample 9.3 (lecturer): $Y=|X|$, $X\sim U[-1,2]$
    Solution

    $f_X=\frac13$ on $[-1,2]$. Ask where $-\beta$ is still inside the support:

    $$\begin{aligned} 0\lt\beta\lt1:&\quad f_Y=f_X(\beta)+f_X(-\beta)=\tfrac13+\tfrac13=\tfrac23 &&\why{$-\beta\in(-1,0)$: inside}\\ 1\lt\beta\lt2:&\quad f_Y=\tfrac13+0=\tfrac13 &&\why{$-\beta\lt-1$: outside} \end{aligned}$$

    Area: $\frac23\times1+\frac13\times1=1$ ✓. The part of $X$ in $[-1,0]$ folded onto $[0,1]$ and doubled the density there.

    Half-wave rectifier $Y=\frac12(X+|X|)$ (lecturer)

    For $X\gt0$, $|X|=X$, so $Y=\frac12(2X)=X$. For $X\le0$, $|X|=-X$, so $Y=0$. The rectifier passes positive inputs and blocks negative ones.

    Key point: the whole negative half-line of inputs is mapped to the single output 0. All the probability $P(X\le0)$ piles up on one value:

    $$P(Y=0)=P(X\le0)=F_X(0)\gt0 .$$

    A continuous RV can't have positive probability at a point, so $Y$ is a mixed RV.

    The CDF, case by case.

    • β < 0: $Y$ is never negative, so $F_Y(\beta)=0$.
    • β ≥ 0: $Y\le\beta$ happens if $X\le0$ (then $Y=0\le\beta$) or if $0\lt X\le\beta$ (then $Y=X\le\beta$). Together that is just $X\le\beta$: $F_Y(\beta)=F_X(\beta)$.

    So as β passes 0, $F_Y$ jumps from 0 up to $F_X(0)$. A jump of size $F_X(0)$ in the CDF is an impulse of that weight in the PDF:

    $$f_Y(\beta)=F_X(0)\,\delta(\beta)+f_X(\beta)\,u(\beta).$$
    MediumExample 9.4 (lecturer): half-wave rectifier, $X\sim U[-1,2]$
    Solution

    $F_X(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha+1}{3}$ on $[-1,2]$, so the weight of the impulse is $F_X(0)=\frac13$.

    $$\begin{aligned} F_Y(\beta)&=\begin{cases}0,&\beta\lt0\\\dfrac{\beta+1}{3},&0\le\beta\lt2\\1,&\beta\ge2\end{cases}\\[6pt] f_Y(\beta)&=\tfrac13\,\delta(\beta)+\tfrac13\ \ \text{on }(0,2]. \end{aligned}$$

    Check: impulse weight + area $=\frac13+\frac13\times2=1$ ✓.

    Dead-zone, or centre clipper (lecturer Q1)

    The rule is: $Y=X-c$ for $X\gt c$; $Y=0$ for $|X|\le c$; $Y=X+c$ for $X\lt-c$. Small inputs (between $-c$ and $c$) are suppressed to 0; larger ones are shifted towards 0 by $c$.

    The CDF. The curve is increasing, apart from the flat piece at height 0.

    • β < 0: only the left branch reaches negative heights. $Y\le\beta$ means $X+c\le\beta$, i.e. $X\le\beta-c$. So $F_Y(\beta)=F_X(\beta-c)$.
    • β ≥ 0: everything on the left branch and the flat piece is ≤ 0 ≤ β. On the right branch, $X-c\le\beta$ means $X\le\beta+c$. So $F_Y(\beta)=F_X(\beta+c)$.

    As β crosses 0, $F_Y$ jumps from $F_X(-c)$ to $F_X(c)$. The jump is $F_X(c)-F_X(-c)=P(|X|\le c)$: exactly the probability of the flat piece, now concentrated at 0.

    MediumExample 9.5 (lecturer): dead-zone with $X\sim U[-2,2]$, threshold 1
    Solution

    $F_X(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha+2}{4}$ on $[-2,2]$, and $c=1$. The range of $Y$ is $[-1,1]$.

    $$\begin{aligned} -1\le\beta\lt0:&\quad F_Y(\beta)=F_X(\beta-1)=\frac{\beta+1}{4} &&\why{rises from 0 to ¼}\\[4pt] 0\le\beta\le1:&\quad F_Y(\beta)=F_X(\beta+1)=\frac{\beta+3}{4} &&\why{rises from ¾ to 1} \end{aligned}$$

    Jump at 0: $\frac34-\frac14=\frac12$, which equals $P(|X|\le1)=\frac24$ ✓. The two ramps both have slope ¼, so

    $$f_Y(\beta)=\tfrac12\,\delta(\beta)+\tfrac14\ \ \text{on }[-1,1].$$

    Check: $\frac12+\frac14\times2=1$ ✓.

    Limiter / clipper (lecturer Q2)

    The rule is: $Y=-c$ for $X\lt-c$; $Y=X$ for $|X|\le c$; $Y=c$ for $X\gt c$. This is an amplifier driven into saturation: it can't output more than $c$ or less than $-c$.

    The CDF.

    • β < −c: $Y$ is never below $-c$, so $F_Y=0$.
    • −c ≤ β < c: $Y\le\beta$ happens when $X\lt-c$ (output $-c\le\beta$) or $-c\le X\le\beta$ (output $X$). Together: $X\le\beta$. So $F_Y(\beta)=F_X(\beta)$.
    • β ≥ c: $Y$ is never above $c$, so $F_Y=1$.

    There are now two flat pieces, so two impulses:

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{at }-c:&\quad\text{weight }F_X(-c) &&\why{all inputs below $-c$}\\ \text{at }+c:&\quad\text{weight }1-F_X(c) &&\why{all inputs above $c$} \end{aligned}$$
    MediumExample 9.6 (lecturer): limiter with $X\sim U[-3,3]$, threshold 1.5
    Solution

    $F_X(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha+3}{6}$, $c=1.5$.

    $$\begin{aligned} P(Y=-1.5)&=F_X(-1.5)=\frac{1.5}{6}=\frac14,\\ P(Y=1.5)&=1-F_X(1.5)=1-\frac{4.5}{6}=\frac14,\\ f_Y(\beta)&=f_X(\beta)=\frac16\quad\text{for }-1.5\lt\beta\lt1.5 . \end{aligned}$$

    Check: $\frac14+\frac14+\frac16\times3=1$ ✓.

    Quantizer (lecturer)

    A staircase $g$ consists only of flat pieces. So $Y$ can take only the step heights: $Y$ is purely discrete. Its PMF is the probability of each step's α-interval. For the staircase shown:

    $$\begin{aligned} P(Y=0)&=P(-1\lt X\le1)=F_X(1)-F_X(-1),\\ P(Y=2)&=P(1\lt X\le2)=F_X(2)-F_X(1),\\ P(Y=-2)&=P(-2\lt X\le-1)=F_X(-1)-F_X(-2), \end{aligned}$$

    and so on. This is exactly what an analog-to-digital converter does to a random signal.

    More transformations (the lecturer's list Q3–Q5)

    MediumExample 9.7 (Q3): $Y=1/X$
    Solution

    For each β ≠ 0 there is exactly one root, $\alpha=1/\beta$, so use the one-root formula in the form $f_Y=f_X\cdot\big|\frac{d\alpha}{d\beta}\big|$. From $\alpha=1/\beta$:

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{d\alpha}{d\beta}&=-\frac{1}{\beta^2},\\ f_Y(\beta)&=f_X\Big(\frac1\beta\Big)\cdot\Big|\frac{d\alpha}{d\beta}\Big|\\ &=\frac{1}{\beta^2}\,f_X\Big(\frac1\beta\Big). \end{aligned}$$

    Example: $X\sim U(1,2)$. Then $f_X(1/\beta)=1$ when $1\lt1/\beta\lt2$, i.e. $\frac12\lt\beta\lt1$. So $f_Y=\frac{1}{\beta^2}$ on $(\frac12,1)$. Check:

    $$\int_{1/2}^1\frac{d\beta}{\beta^2}=\Big[-\frac1\beta\Big]_{1/2}^1=-1+2=1\ ✓$$
    Q4: $Y=F_X(X)$ is always $U(0,1)$ (probability integral transform)

    Let $X$ be continuous with a strictly increasing CDF. $Y=F_X(X)$ lies between 0 and 1. For $0\le y\le1$:

    $$\begin{aligned} F_Y(y)&=P\big(F_X(X)\le y\big)\\ &=P\big(X\le F_X^{-1}(y)\big) &&\why{apply the increasing $F_X^{-1}$ to both sides}\\ &=F_X\big(F_X^{-1}(y)\big)\\ &=y . \end{aligned}$$

    $F_Y(y)=y$ on $[0,1]$ is the CDF of $U(0,1)$. Feeding any continuous RV through its own CDF flattens it into a uniform RV.

    Q5: $Y=F_X^{-1}(Z)$ with $Z\sim U(0,1)$ has CDF $F_X$ (lecturer: "Monte-Carlo transformation")

    This is Q4 run backwards:

    $$\begin{aligned} F_Y(\alpha)&=P\big(F_X^{-1}(Z)\le\alpha\big)\\ &=P\big(Z\le F_X(\alpha)\big) &&\why{apply the increasing $F_X$ to both sides}\\ &=F_Z\big(F_X(\alpha)\big)\\ &=F_X(\alpha) &&\why{$F_Z(\theta)=\theta$ for $0\le\theta\le1$} \end{aligned}$$

    Why it matters: computers can only generate uniform random numbers directly. This theorem says: to get samples from any distribution, generate $z$ uniform on (0,1) and output $F_X^{-1}(z)$. In the picture, you pick a height $z$ on the vertical axis, go across to the CDF curve, and drop down to read off $x$.

    MediumExample 9.8 (lecturer): generating exponential RVs

    Find the transformation that turns $Z\sim U[0,1]$ into an exponentially distributed RV.

    Solution

    We need $F_X^{-1}$ for $F_X(\alpha)=1-e^{-\lambda\alpha}$. Set $\beta=F_X(\alpha)$ and solve for α:

    $$\begin{aligned} \beta&=1-e^{-\lambda\alpha}\\ e^{-\lambda\alpha}&=1-\beta\\ -\lambda\alpha&=\ln(1-\beta)\\ \alpha&=\frac1\lambda\ln\Big(\frac{1}{1-\beta}\Big). \end{aligned}$$

    So

    $$Y=\frac1\lambda\ln\Big(\frac{1}{1-Z}\Big)\ \sim\ \text{Exp}(\lambda).$$

    (Since $1-Z$ is also $U(0,1)$, $Y=-\frac1\lambda\ln Z$ works equally well.)

    ExamExample 9.9 (lecturer): $Y=\sin X$, $X\sim U[-\pi,\pi]$
    Solution

    Range: $Y\in[-1,1]$.

    Roots: over one full period, the sine curve crosses any height β with $|\beta|\lt1$ exactly twice. For β > 0 the roots are $\alpha_1=\sin^{-1}\beta$ and $\alpha_2=\pi-\sin^{-1}\beta$. (For β < 0, the second one is $-\pi-\sin^{-1}\beta$.)

    Slopes at the roots: $g'(\alpha)=\cos\alpha$. At both roots,

    $$|\cos\alpha_k|=\sqrt{1-\sin^2\alpha_k}=\sqrt{1-\beta^2}.$$

    Assemble, with $f_X=\frac1{2\pi}$:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Y(\beta)&=\frac{f_X(\alpha_1)}{|g'(\alpha_1)|}+\frac{f_X(\alpha_2)}{|g'(\alpha_2)|}\\ &=2\cdot\frac{1/(2\pi)}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}}\\ &=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-\beta^2}},\qquad|\beta|\lt1 . \end{aligned}$$

    This is the arcsine density. Why is it high near ±1? A sinusoid moves slowly near its peaks (slope ≈ 0) and fast through zero. So a sine wave with a random phase spends most of its time near ±1. Check the area:

    $$\frac1\pi\Big[\sin^{-1}\beta\Big]_{-1}^{1}=\frac1\pi\Big(\frac\pi2+\frac\pi2\Big)=1\ ✓$$
    EasyExample 9.10: a non-uniform input, both methods side by side

    $X\sim$ Exp(1), i.e. $f_X(x)=e^{-x}$ for $x\ge0$. Find the PDF of $Y=X^2$, and $P(Y\gt4)$.

    Solution

    Look at $g$ on the support. $X\ge0$, and on $[0,\infty)$ the parabola is strictly increasing. So for each $y\gt0$ there is one relevant root, $x=\sqrt y$. (The root $-\sqrt y$ exists for the parabola, but $f_X=0$ there, so it contributes nothing.)

    Method 1: CDF. For $y\gt0$:

    $$\begin{aligned} F_Y(y)&=P(X^2\le y)\\ &=P(X\le\sqrt y) &&\why{$X\ge0$, so no need for $-\sqrt y\le X$}\\ &=1-e^{-\sqrt y},\\[4pt] f_Y(y)&=\frac{d}{dy}\big(1-e^{-\sqrt y}\big)=e^{-\sqrt y}\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt y} &&\why{chain rule}\\ &=\frac{e^{-\sqrt y}}{2\sqrt y},\qquad y\gt0 . \end{aligned}$$

    Method 2: derivative formula. $g'(x)=2x$, so at $x=\sqrt y$:

    $$f_Y(y)=\frac{f_X(\sqrt y)}{|g'(\sqrt y)|}=\frac{e^{-\sqrt y}}{2\sqrt y}\ ✓$$

    $P(Y\gt4)$ without the PDF. Translate the event back to $X$: $X^2\gt4\iff X\gt2$ (as $X\ge0$), so $P(Y\gt4)=P(X\gt2)=e^{-2}=0.135$. Translating events is often quicker than finding $f_Y$.

    MediumExample 9.11: a capped (clipped) variable gives a mixed RV

    A server answers a request after a time $X\sim$ Exp(1) (in seconds), but a timer cuts every request off at 1 second, so the recorded time is $Y=\min(X,1)$. Find $F_Y$, $f_Y$ and $E[Y]$.

    Solution

    Sketch $g(x)=\min(x,1)$: it rises along $y=x$ up to $x=1$, then is flat at height 1. A flat piece ⇒ CDF method, and expect an impulse at height 1.

    CDF, zone by zone.

    • $y\lt0$: $Y$ is never negative, so $F_Y(y)=0$.
    • $0\le y\lt1$: the horizontal line at height $y$ is below the flat part. The curve is at or below it exactly for $x\le y$ (green interval). So $F_Y(y)=P(X\le y)=1-e^{-y}$.
    • $y\ge1$: the whole curve is at or below height 1, so $F_Y(y)=1$.

    The jump. Just below 1, $F_Y=1-e^{-1}$; at 1 it becomes 1. Jump $=e^{-1}=0.368=P(X\gt1)$: every request that would have taken longer than 1 s is recorded as exactly 1 s.

    $$f_Y(y)=e^{-y}\ \ (0\lt y\lt1)\quad+\quad e^{-1}\,\delta(y-1).$$

    Check: $\int_0^1e^{-y}dy+e^{-1}=(1-e^{-1})+e^{-1}=1$ ✓.

    Mean: continuous part plus impulse part:

    $$\begin{aligned} E[Y]&=\int_0^1y\,e^{-y}\,dy+1\cdot e^{-1}\\ &=\Big[-ye^{-y}-e^{-y}\Big]_0^1+e^{-1} &&\why{integration by parts}\\ &=(1-2e^{-1})+e^{-1}=1-e^{-1}\approx\mathbf{0.632}\ \text{s}. \end{aligned}$$

    Less than $E[X]=1$, as it must be: capping can only shorten times.

    Don't need the PDF? Don't find it.

    To get $E[g(X)]$, use $\int g(\alpha)f_X(\alpha)\,d\alpha$ directly (Unit 2). Find $f_Y$ only when the question asks for the distribution of $Y$, or for probabilities about $Y$.

    10Functions of two random variables: $Z=g(X,Y)$

    General method (lecturer)

    Given $f_{XY}$ and $Z=g(X,Y)$, find $f_Z$ (and if needed $F_Z$, $M_Z$, $\Phi_Z$):

    1. $F_Z(\theta)=P(Z\le\theta)=P\big((X,Y)\in C_\theta\big)$, where $C_\theta=\{(\alpha,\beta):g(\alpha,\beta)\le\theta\}$ is a region of the plane.
    2. Compute the volume under $f_{XY}$ over $C_\theta$: $F_Z(\theta)=\iint_{C_\theta}f_{XY}\,d\alpha\,d\beta$.
    3. Differentiate: $f_Z(\theta)=\dfrac{d}{d\theta}F_Z(\theta)$. Usually θ appears in a limit of the inner integral, so the Leibniz rule makes that integral disappear.

    It is the same CDF method as §9. The only difference is that the "set of inputs that give $Z\le\theta$" is now a region of the plane instead of a set of intervals on a line.

    Sum $Z=X+Y$ and the convolution integral (lecturer)

    Step 1: the region. $X+Y\le\theta$ means $\beta\le\theta-\alpha$: the half-plane below the line $\alpha+\beta=\theta$, which has slope −1.

    Step 2: the volume, with horizontal strips. For fixed β, the strip runs from α = −∞ to the line, at $\alpha=\theta-\beta$:

    $$F_Z(\theta)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\theta-\beta}f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)\,d\alpha\right]d\beta .$$

    Step 3: differentiate. Only the upper limit $\theta-\beta$ of the inner integral involves θ, and its θ-derivative is 1. By the Leibniz rule, differentiating the inner integral just substitutes $\alpha=\theta-\beta$ into the integrand. (The lecturer does the same with the antiderivative $G_{XY}$.)

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Z(\theta)&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\theta-\beta}f_{XY}(\alpha,\beta)\,d\alpha\right]d\beta\\ &=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{XY}(\theta-\beta,\ \beta)\cdot1\;d\beta . \end{aligned}$$
    PDF of a sum $$\begin{aligned} f_Z(\theta)&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{XY}(\theta-\beta,\ \beta)\,d\beta &&\why{always}\\[4pt] &=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(\theta-\beta)\,f_Y(\beta)\,d\beta &&\why{if X, Y are independent}\\[4pt] &=(f_X*f_Y)(\theta). \end{aligned}$$

    The PDF of a sum of independent RVs is the convolution of their PDFs (lecturer: "convolution integral").

    Why convolution, intuitively? To get the total θ, $Y$ can take any value β, provided $X$ takes the value $\theta-\beta$ that makes up the rest. Add up (integrate) over all the ways: density of $Y$ at β times density of $X$ at $\theta-\beta$.

    Variants (lecturer):

    • $W=aX+bY$ (independent). $aX$ has PDF $\frac1{|a|}f_X(\frac\alpha a)$ and $bY$ has $\frac1{|b|}f_Y(\frac\beta b)$ (§9). Convolve those: $$f_W(\theta)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|ab|}\,f_X\Big(\frac{\theta-\beta}{a}\Big)\,f_Y\Big(\frac\beta b\Big)\,d\beta .$$
    • $V=X-Y=X+(-Y)$. The PDF of $-Y$ is $f_Y(-\beta)$: $$\begin{aligned} f_V(\theta)&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(\theta-\beta)\,f_Y(-\beta)\,d\beta\\ &=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(\theta+u)\,f_Y(u)\,du &&\why{substitute $u=-\beta$} \end{aligned}$$
    MediumExample 10.1 (lecturer): the sum of two iid $U[0,1]$ is triangular
    Solution

    Set-up.

    $$f_Z(\theta)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(\alpha)\,f_Y(\theta-\alpha)\,d\alpha .$$

    Both PDFs are 1 on their interval and 0 elsewhere. So the integrand is 1 exactly where both are "on", and 0 elsewhere:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_X(\alpha)=1&\iff0\le\alpha\le1,\\ f_Y(\theta-\alpha)=1&\iff0\le\theta-\alpha\le1\iff\theta-1\le\alpha\le\theta . \end{aligned}$$

    So $f_Z(\theta)$ is the length of the overlap of $[0,1]$ and $[\theta-1,\theta]$. Slide the second box from left to right and watch the overlap:

    Case θ < 0: the sliding box $[\theta-1,\theta]$ lies entirely left of 0. No overlap: $f_Z=0$.

    Case 0 ≤ θ ≤ 1: the box has moved partly in. Its right end θ is inside $[0,1]$; its left end $\theta-1$ is still below 0. The overlap is $[0,\theta]$:

    $$f_Z(\theta)=\int_0^\theta1\,d\alpha=\theta .$$

    Case 1 < θ ≤ 2: the box is moving out. Its left end $\theta-1$ is inside $[0,1]$; its right end θ is beyond 1. The overlap is $[\theta-1,1]$:

    $$f_Z(\theta)=\int_{\theta-1}^11\,d\alpha=1-(\theta-1)=2-\theta .$$

    Case θ > 2: the box has left completely. $f_Z=0$.

    $$f_Z(\theta)=\begin{cases}\theta,&0\le\theta\le1\\2-\theta,&1\lt\theta\le2\\0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

    Meaning. "The PDF of the sum of two uniform numbers is triangular" (lecturer). Adding two flat distributions already creates a peak in the middle. Why? A total near 1 can be made in many ways (0.5+0.5, 0.2+0.8, 0.9+0.1…), but a total near 0 needs both numbers to be near 0. Middle totals have more "ways", just like 7 is the most common total with two dice. This is the first step towards the central limit theorem (§11).

    In general, $X,Y$ iid $U[a,b]$ give a triangle on $[2a,2b]$ with its peak $\frac1{b-a}$ at $a+b$.

    MediumExample 10.2: sum of two iid exponentials

    $X,Y$ iid Exp(λ). Find $f_Z$ for $Z=X+Y$.

    Solution

    The integrand:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_X(\theta-\beta)\,f_Y(\beta)&=\lambda e^{-\lambda(\theta-\beta)}\cdot\lambda e^{-\lambda\beta}\\ &=\lambda^2e^{-\lambda\theta+\lambda\beta-\lambda\beta}\\ &=\lambda^2e^{-\lambda\theta}. \end{aligned}$$

    Where is it non-zero? This is the step everyone forgets. Each exponential PDF is zero for negative arguments, so we need both

    $$\beta\ge0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\theta-\beta\ge0\iff\beta\le\theta .$$

    So β runs from 0 to θ (and θ itself must be ≥ 0):

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Z(\theta)&=\int_0^\theta\lambda^2e^{-\lambda\theta}\,d\beta\\ &=\lambda^2e^{-\lambda\theta}\cdot\theta &&\why{integrand doesn't depend on β}\\ &=\lambda^2\theta\,e^{-\lambda\theta},\qquad\theta\ge0 . \end{aligned}$$

    This is the Erlang-2 (gamma) PDF, matching the MGF $\big(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-t}\big)^2$ from §8. In a queue it is the waiting time until the second arrival. Unlike the exponential, it starts at 0: two events can't both happen almost immediately.

    Important results (lecturer)

    If $Y,Z$ are iid $N(m,\sigma^2)$ and $X=Y+Z$, then $f_X=f_Y*f_Z$, and $X\sim N(2m,\,2\sigma^2)$. The lecturer writes this as "$N(m,\sigma^2)+N(m,\sigma^2)=N(2m,2\sigma^2)$". A Gaussian convolved with a Gaussian is a Gaussian; the means add and the variances add.

    EasyExample 10.1b: the same triangle, by geometry instead of convolution

    $X,Y$ iid $U(0,1)$, $Z=X+Y$. Find $F_Z$ by measuring areas, then $f_Z$. (This is the general method from the start of §10, done with pictures. Many students find it easier than sliding boxes.)

    Solution

    The joint PDF is 1 on the unit square, so probability = area. $F_Z(\theta)=P(X+Y\le\theta)$ is the area of the part of the square below the line $x+y=\theta$ (gallery region ③). Its shape changes at θ = 1:

    • θ < 0: the line is below the square: $F_Z=0$.
    • 0 ≤ θ ≤ 1: a right triangle with legs θ and θ: $F_Z(\theta)=\dfrac{\theta^2}{2}$.
    • 1 < θ ≤ 2: the whole square except the top-right corner triangle, whose legs are $2-\theta$: $F_Z(\theta)=1-\dfrac{(2-\theta)^2}{2}$.
    • θ > 2: the whole square: $F_Z=1$.

    Differentiate each piece:

    $$\begin{aligned} 0\le\theta\le1:&\quad f_Z(\theta)=\frac{d}{d\theta}\frac{\theta^2}{2}=\theta,\\ 1\lt\theta\le2:&\quad f_Z(\theta)=\frac{d}{d\theta}\Big[1-\frac{(2-\theta)^2}{2}\Big]=-\frac{2(2-\theta)(-1)}{2}=2-\theta . \end{aligned}$$

    The same triangle as the convolution gave in Example 10.1. Two routes to the same answer: CDF via areas (good when the density is uniform) and convolution (good for any independent PDFs).

    Recipe: setting up a convolution without getting lost

    For independent $X,Y$: $f_Z(\theta)=\int f_X(\theta-\beta)\,f_Y(\beta)\,d\beta$.

    1. Write where each factor is non-zero as a condition on β: $f_Y(\beta)\ne0\iff\beta\in[c,d]$; $f_X(\theta-\beta)\ne0\iff\theta-\beta\in[a,b]\iff\beta\in[\theta-b,\ \theta-a]$.
    2. The integral runs over the overlap of the two β-intervals: from $\max(c,\theta-b)$ to $\min(d,\theta-a)$.
    3. Which end is the max/min depends on θ. Find the θ-values where the ends cross ($\theta-b=c$, $\theta-a=c$, $\theta-b=d$, $\theta-a=d$); they split θ into cases.
    4. Do the integral in each case. Check: $f_Z$ is continuous here (for bounded PDFs) and has area 1. The support of $Z$ runs from (smallest $X$ + smallest $Y$) to (largest + largest).
    MediumExample 10.2b: uniforms of different widths give a trapezoid

    $X\sim U(0,1)$ and $Y\sim U(0,2)$ are independent. Find the PDF of $Z=X+Y$.

    Solution

    Step 1: the two β-intervals. $f_Y(\beta)=\frac12$ for $\beta\in[0,2]$. $f_X(\theta-\beta)=1$ for $0\le\theta-\beta\le1$, i.e. $\beta\in[\theta-1,\ \theta]$.

    Step 2: the integrand is $1\times\frac12$ on the overlap, so

    $$f_Z(\theta)=\tfrac12\times\text{length of }\big([\theta-1,\theta]\cap[0,2]\big).$$

    Step 3: cases. $Z$ ranges over $[0,3]$. Slide the window $[\theta-1,\theta]$ (length 1) across $[0,2]$ (length 2):

    • $0\le\theta\le1$: window entering, overlap $[0,\theta]$, length θ ⇒ $f_Z=\frac\theta2$.
    • $1\le\theta\le2$: window completely inside, overlap length 1 ⇒ $f_Z=\frac12$.
    • $2\le\theta\le3$: window leaving, overlap $[\theta-1,2]$, length $3-\theta$ ⇒ $f_Z=\frac{3-\theta}{2}$.

    Check: area $=\frac14+\frac12+\frac14=1$ ✓. With equal widths the flat middle shrinks to a point (the triangle); unequal widths give a trapezoid.

    Product $Z=XY$ (lecturer)

    Step 1: the region. $C_\theta=\{\alpha\beta\le\theta\}$ is bounded by the hyperbola $\alpha\beta=\theta$. To turn $\alpha\beta\le\theta$ into a limit for α, we must divide by β. But dividing by a negative β flips the inequality, so split by the sign of β:

    $$\begin{aligned} \beta\gt0:&\quad\alpha\beta\le\theta\iff\alpha\le\theta/\beta,\\ \beta\lt0:&\quad\alpha\beta\le\theta\iff\alpha\ge\theta/\beta . \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2: the volume.

    $$F_Z(\theta)=\int_0^\infty\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\theta/\beta}f_{XY}\,d\alpha\right]d\beta+\int_{-\infty}^0\left[\int_{\theta/\beta}^{\infty}f_{XY}\,d\alpha\right]d\beta .$$

    Step 3: differentiate. The limit $\theta/\beta$ has θ-derivative $\frac1\beta$. In the first integral it is an upper limit (plus sign); in the second a lower limit (minus sign):

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Z(\theta)&=\int_0^\infty\frac1\beta\,f_{XY}\Big(\frac\theta\beta,\beta\Big)d\beta+\int_{-\infty}^0\Big(-\frac1\beta\Big)f_{XY}\Big(\frac\theta\beta,\beta\Big)d\beta . \end{aligned}$$

    For β > 0, $\frac1\beta=\frac1{|\beta|}$. For β < 0, $-\frac1\beta=\frac1{|\beta|}$. So both pieces combine into one integral:

    $$\boxed{f_Z(\theta)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|\beta|}\,f_{XY}\Big(\frac\theta\beta,\ \beta\Big)\,d\beta}$$

    Intuition: to get the product θ with $Y=\beta$, $X$ must equal $\theta/\beta$. The $\frac{1}{|\beta|}$ is the stretch factor from §9: for fixed β, $Z=\beta X$ is a linear function of $X$.

    MediumExample 10.3: product of two iid $U(0,1)$ (ES Nov–Dec 2024)
    Solution

    Fix $0\lt\theta\lt1$. In the formula, $f_{XY}(\theta/\beta,\beta)=1$ requires both coordinates inside $(0,1)$:

    $$\begin{aligned} 0\lt\beta\lt1\quad&\text{and}\quad0\lt\frac\theta\beta\lt1\\ &\Longleftrightarrow\quad\theta\lt\beta\lt1 . \end{aligned}$$

    (The second condition says $\beta\gt\theta$.) So

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Z(\theta)&=\int_\theta^1\frac{1}{\beta}\,d\beta\\ &=\Big[\ln\beta\Big]_\theta^1\\ &=-\ln\theta,\qquad0\lt\theta\lt1 . \end{aligned}$$

    Check: $\int_0^1-\ln\theta\,d\theta=\big[\theta-\theta\ln\theta\big]_0^1=1$ ✓. Products of numbers below 1 are smaller than either number, so the mass piles up near 0.

    Quotient $Z=X/Y$ Beyond class notes

    By the same method: $f_Z(\theta)=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^\infty|\beta|\,f_{XY}(\theta\beta,\ \beta)\,d\beta$. For $X,Y$ iid $N(0,1)$, this gives the Cauchy PDF $\frac{1}{\pi(1+\theta^2)}$.

    Maximum and minimum (lecturer)

    The trick for max: "the larger of the two is ≤ z" means "both are ≤ z". So the region is a quadrant, and we already know quadrant probabilities: they are the joint CDF.

    The trick for min: "the smaller is ≤ z" means "at least one is ≤ z", which is awkward. Its complement is easy: "the smaller is > z" means "both are > z".

    max and min

    $Z=\max(X,Y)$:

    $$\begin{aligned} F_Z(z)&=P(X\le z,\ Y\le z)=F_{XY}(z,z)\\ &=F_X(z)\,F_Y(z) &&\why{if independent}\\ f_Z(z)&=f_X(z)F_Y(z)+F_X(z)f_Y(z) &&\why{product rule}\\ &=2f(z)F(z) &&\why{if iid} \end{aligned}$$

    $Z=\min(X,Y)$:

    $$\begin{aligned} F_Z(z)&=1-P(X\gt z,\ Y\gt z)\\ &=1-\big[1-F_X(z)\big]\big[1-F_Y(z)\big] &&\why{if independent}\\ f_Z(z)&=f_X(z)\big[1-F_Y(z)\big]+f_Y(z)\big[1-F_X(z)\big]\\ &=2f(z)\big[1-F(z)\big] &&\why{if iid} \end{aligned}$$

    In the dependent case, $P(X\gt z,Y\gt z)=1-F_X(z)-F_Y(z)+F_{XY}(z,z)$ (§1).

    Where this shows up: a series system dies when its first component dies, so its lifetime is the min. A parallel (backup) system dies when its last component dies, so its lifetime is the max.

    EasyExample 10.4 (lecturer): max of two iid $U[-1,1]$
    Solution

    For $U[-1,1]$: $f=\frac12$ and $F(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha+1}{2}$ on $[-1,1]$.

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Z(\alpha)&=2f(\alpha)F(\alpha)\\ &=2\cdot\frac12\cdot\frac{\alpha+1}{2}\\ &=\frac{\alpha+1}{2},\qquad-1\lt\alpha\lt1 . \end{aligned}$$

    A rising ramp: the larger of two numbers tends to be large.

    Solution

    For $U[-2,2]$: $f=\frac14$ and $F(z)=\frac{z+2}{4}$.

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Z(z)&=2f(z)\big[1-F(z)\big]\\ &=2\cdot\frac14\Big[1-\frac{z+2}{4}\Big]\\ &=\frac12\cdot\frac{4-z-2}{4}\\ &=\frac{2-z}{8},\qquad-2\lt z\lt2 . \end{aligned}$$

    A falling ramp from ½ at $z=-2$ to 0 at $z=2$. Area $=\frac12\times4\times\frac12=1$ ✓.

    MediumExample 10.6 (lecturer): design a RV with a given triangular PDF

    How can we obtain a RV $W$ whose PDF falls linearly from 1 at α = −1 to 0 at α = 1?

    Solution

    Spot the shape. It is the mirror image of the rising ramp in Example 10.4. Mirroring a PDF left-to-right corresponds to negating the RV: if $W=-Z$, then $f_W(\alpha)=f_Z(-\alpha)$. So take

    $$W=-\max(X,Y),\qquad X,Y\ \text{iid }U[-1,1],$$ $$f_W(\alpha)=f_Z(-\alpha)=\frac{-\alpha+1}{2}=\frac{1-\alpha}{2},\qquad-1\lt\alpha\lt1 .$$

    Equivalently, $W=\min(-X,-Y)$, the minimum of two iid $U[-1,1]$. (For the area to be 1 the peak must be 1: $\frac12\times2\times1=1$ ✓.)

    MediumExample 10.7: minimum of exponentials (ES 2021)

    $X\sim$ Exp(λ) and $Y\sim$ Exp(μ) are independent. Find the distribution of $W=\min(X,Y)$.

    Solution $$\begin{aligned} P(W\gt w)&=P(X\gt w)\,P(Y\gt w)\\ &=e^{-\lambda w}\,e^{-\mu w}\\ &=e^{-(\lambda+\mu)w},\\[4pt] F_W(w)&=1-e^{-(\lambda+\mu)w},\\ f_W(w)&=(\lambda+\mu)\,e^{-(\lambda+\mu)w},\qquad w\ge0 . \end{aligned}$$

    So $W\sim$ Exp(λ + μ): the rates add. If two independent alarms go off at rates λ and μ, the first alarm of either kind comes at the combined rate λ + μ.

    EasyExample 10.8: series and parallel systems

    Two components have independent lifetimes $X,Y\sim$ Exp with mean 1000 hours each. Find the probability of surviving 1000 hours and the mean lifetime, (a) if they are in series (the system fails when either fails), (b) if they are in parallel (it fails when both have failed).

    Solution

    Each has $P(X\gt t)=e^{-t/1000}$, so $P(X\gt1000)=e^{-1}=0.368$.

    (a) Series ⇒ lifetime $\min(X,Y)$. It survives past $t$ only if both do:

    $$\begin{aligned} P(\min\gt1000)&=P(X\gt1000)\,P(Y\gt1000)=e^{-1}e^{-1}=e^{-2}=\mathbf{0.135}. \end{aligned}$$

    In general $P(\min\gt t)=e^{-2t/1000}$: the min is Exp with rate $\frac2{1000}$, mean 500 h (rates add, Example 10.7).

    (b) Parallel ⇒ lifetime $\max(X,Y)$. It has failed by $t$ only if both have:

    $$\begin{aligned} P(\max\le1000)&=(1-e^{-1})^2=0.632^2=0.400,\\ P(\max\gt1000)&=1-0.400=\mathbf{0.600}. \end{aligned}$$

    Mean of the max, by a trick: $\max+\min=X+Y$ always (one is the larger, one the smaller). Take expectations:

    $$E[\max]=E[X]+E[Y]-E[\min]=1000+1000-500=\mathbf{1500\ h}.$$

    Meaning. Adding a backup in parallel raises survival from 37% to 60%; putting a second part in series drops it to 14%.

    Standard results: $X^2+Y^2$ and the Rayleigh distribution (lecturer)

    $X,Y$ iid $N(0,\sigma^2)$
    1. $Z=X^2+Y^2$ is exponential with mean $2\sigma^2$ (chi-square with 2 degrees of freedom): $$f_Z(z)=\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\exp\Big\{-\frac{z}{2\sigma^2}\Big\}u(z).$$
    2. $R=\sqrt{X^2+Y^2}$ has the Rayleigh distribution: $$f_R(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha}{\sigma^2}\exp\Big\{-\frac{\alpha^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big\}u(\alpha).$$

    Proof. This is Example 3.3 with general σ. The joint PDF is $\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}e^{-(\alpha^2+\beta^2)/2\sigma^2}$. Use polar coordinates for the disc of radius $a$:

    $$\begin{aligned} P(R\le a)&=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^a\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}\,r\,dr\,d\phi\\ &=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\int_0^ae^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}\,r\,dr &&\why{the φ-integral gives 2π}\\ &=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\Big[-\sigma^2e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}\Big]_0^a\\ &=1-e^{-a^2/2\sigma^2}. \end{aligned}$$

    Differentiate in $a$ to get the Rayleigh PDF. For $Z=R^2$:

    $$\begin{aligned} P(Z\le z)&=P(R\le\sqrt z)\\ &=1-e^{-z/2\sigma^2}, \end{aligned}$$

    which is the CDF of an exponential with rate $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}$, i.e. mean $2\sigma^2$.

    Where it appears. Write complex baseband noise as $X+jY=Re^{j\Phi}$ (lecturer). The envelope $R$ is Rayleigh, the power $R^2$ is exponential, and the phase $\Phi=\tan^{-1}(Y/X)$ is uniform on $(-\pi,\pi]$, independent of $R$. This is also the standard model for fading in wireless channels.

    Two functions of two RVs (Jacobian method) Beyond class notes

    For a one-to-one map $V=g_1(X,Y)$, $W=g_2(X,Y)$, with inverse $(x,y)$:

    $$f_{VW}(v,w)=\frac{f_{XY}(x,y)}{|J(x,y)|},\qquad J=\det\begin{bmatrix}\partial v/\partial x&\partial v/\partial y\\\partial w/\partial x&\partial w/\partial y\end{bmatrix}.$$

    This is the 2-D version of $f_X/|g'|$: $|J|$ says how much the map stretches small areas. For the polar map $(X,Y)\to(R,\Phi)$, $|J|=1/r$, so

    $$\begin{aligned} f_{R\Phi}(r,\phi)&=r\,f_{XY}(r\cos\phi,\ r\sin\phi)\\ &=\frac{r}{\sigma^2}e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi} &&\why{for iid $N(0,\sigma^2)$} \end{aligned}$$

    This is (Rayleigh in $r$) × (uniform in φ), which proves that $R$ and Φ are independent.

    11Law of large numbers & the central limit theorem

    The big picture

    Take many independent copies $X_1,\dots,X_n$ of a RV: repeated measurements, the noise from many electrons, many coin tosses. Add them up. Two things happen:

    1. Their average settles down to the true mean. This is the law of large numbers, and it is why relative frequency estimates probability (Unit 1).
    2. The shape of the fluctuations around that mean becomes Gaussian, whatever distribution the $X_i$ had. This is the central limit theorem, and it is why Gaussian noise is everywhere.

    Law of large numbers Beyond class notes

    Sample mean and the weak law of large numbers

    Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be iid with mean μ and variance σ². The sample mean is $M_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i$.

    Its mean:

    $$\begin{aligned} E[M_n]&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nE[X_i] &&\why{linearity}\\ &=\frac1n\cdot n\mu=\mu . \end{aligned}$$

    Its variance:

    $$\begin{aligned} \Var[M_n]&=\frac1{n^2}\Var\Big[\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\Big] &&\why{$\Var[cX]=c^2\Var[X]$}\\ &=\frac1{n^2}\cdot n\sigma^2 &&\why{independent ⇒ variances add}\\ &=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}. \end{aligned}$$

    The law. Apply Chebyshev's inequality (Unit 2) to $M_n$. For any $\epsilon\gt0$:

    $$P\big(|M_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon\big)\le\frac{\Var[M_n]}{\epsilon^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}\ \xrightarrow{\ n\to\infty\ }\ 0 .$$

    However small a margin ε you choose, the chance that the average misses μ by more than ε goes to zero.

    Meaning. The average keeps the same mean μ, but its spread shrinks like $\sigma/\sqrt n$. Averaging 100 readings makes the error 10 times smaller, not 100 times. Now let $X_i$ be 1 if event $A$ happens on trial $i$ and 0 otherwise. Then $M_n$ is the relative frequency of $A$, and μ = P(A). So relative frequency → probability: the frequency interpretation from Unit 1 is now a theorem.

    MediumExample 11.1: how many samples?

    A voltage is measured repeatedly; each reading has standard deviation 0.5 V. How many readings guarantee, by Chebyshev, that the average is within 0.1 V of the true value with probability at least 0.95?

    Solution

    We need the miss probability to be at most 0.05:

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}&\le0.05\\ n&\ge\frac{\sigma^2}{0.05\,\epsilon^2}\\ &=\frac{0.25}{0.05\times0.01}\\ &=500 . \end{aligned}$$

    (The CLT, below, gives a much smaller but only approximate answer: $1.96\times0.5/\sqrt n\le0.1$ gives $n\ge97$. Chebyshev is a guarantee that works for any distribution, so it is pessimistic.)

    Central limit theorem

    Statement

    Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be iid with mean μ and finite variance σ². Let $S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nX_i$, so $E[S_n]=n\mu$ and $\Var S_n=n\sigma^2$. Standardise it (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation):

    $$\begin{aligned} Z_n&=\frac{S_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\\ &=\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{X_i-\mu}{\sigma}. \end{aligned}$$

    Then as $n\to\infty$, the distribution of $Z_n$ tends to $N(0,1)$: $P(Z_n\le z)\to\Phi(z)=1-Q(z)$ for every $z$.

    Why standardise? $S_n$ itself doesn't settle down: its mean $n\mu$ runs off and its spread $\sigma\sqrt n$ grows. $Z_n$ always has mean 0 and variance 1, so we can compare its shape as $n$ grows. The lecturer writes $Z_N=\frac1{\sqrt N}\sum X_i$, assuming zero-mean $X_i$. With a non-zero mean, $E[Z_N]=\frac{N m_X}{\sqrt N}=\sqrt N\,m_X$ would grow without bound, which is why we subtract the mean first.

    Proof via the MGF (asked in ES July 2024 Q3a and ES June 2026 Q4b)

    Step 1: standardise each term. Let $Y_i=\frac{X_i-\mu}{\sigma}$. The $Y_i$ are iid with

    $$E[Y_i]=0,\qquad E[Y_i^2]=\Var[Y_i]=1,\qquad Z_n=\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^nY_i .$$

    Let $M_Y(t)$ be their common MGF (we assume it exists near $t=0$).

    Step 2: Taylor-expand $M_Y$ around 0. We know its value and first two derivatives at 0 (Unit 2: derivatives of the MGF at 0 are the moments):

    $$\begin{aligned} M_Y(0)&=1,\\ M_Y'(0)&=E[Y]=0,\\ M_Y''(0)&=E[Y^2]=1,\\[4pt] \Rightarrow\quad M_Y(t)&=1+0\cdot t+\frac{1}{2}t^2+o(t^2)\\ &=1+\frac{t^2}{2}+o(t^2), \end{aligned}$$

    where $o(t^2)$ means terms that shrink faster than $t^2$ as $t\to0$.

    Step 3: MGF of $Z_n$. Two rules: the MGF of an independent sum is the product (§8), and $M_{cY}(t)=E[e^{tcY}]=M_Y(ct)$.

    $$\begin{aligned} M_{Z_n}(t)&=E\Big[\exp\Big\{\frac{t}{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^nY_i\Big\}\Big]\\ &=\prod_{i=1}^nE\Big[e^{(t/\sqrt n)Y_i}\Big] &&\why{independence}\\ &=\Big[M_Y\Big(\frac{t}{\sqrt n}\Big)\Big]^n &&\why{identically distributed}\\ &=\Big[1+\frac{(t/\sqrt n)^2}{2}+o\Big(\frac1n\Big)\Big]^n &&\why{Step 2 with $t\to t/\sqrt n$}\\ &=\Big[1+\frac{t^2}{2n}+o\Big(\frac1n\Big)\Big]^n . \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4: take the limit. Use the standard limit $\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(1+\frac{a}{n}\big)^n=e^{a}$, which still holds with an extra $o(\frac1n)$ term inside. Here $a=t^2/2$:

    $$\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{Z_n}(t)=e^{t^2/2}.$$

    Step 5: conclude. $e^{t^2/2}$ is the MGF of $N(0,1)$ (Unit 2). If MGFs converge, the distributions converge (the continuity theorem). So $Z_n\to N(0,1)$ in distribution. ∎

    Some books use the characteristic function instead, because it always exists. The steps are identical, with $j\omega$ in place of $t$, and the limit is $e^{-\omega^2/2}$.

    What the proof shows. Only the mean (0) and variance (1) of $Y$ survived into the limit. Every higher moment ended up inside $o(1/n)$ and vanished. That is why the original distribution doesn't matter: in a long sum, only its mean and variance leave a trace.

    The plots below show the standardised sum of $n$ uniform RVs (solid) against $N(0,1)$ (dashed). With $n=2$ it is the triangle of Example 10.1. By $n=12$ you can't tell the two curves apart.

    Using the CLT in numericals

    For large $n$ (rule of thumb $n\ge30$; fewer if the $X_i$ are symmetric):

    $$\begin{aligned} S_n&\approx N(n\mu,\ n\sigma^2),\\ M_n&\approx N\Big(\mu,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\Big),\\ P(a\lt S_n\le b)&\approx Q\Big(\frac{a-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\Big)-Q\Big(\frac{b-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\Big). \end{aligned}$$

    Binomial special case (a sum of $n$ Bernoulli($p$)): $X\approx N(np,\ npq)$, the De Moivre–Laplace approximation. For integer-valued RVs use the continuity correction, e.g. $P(X\ge k)\approx P(\text{normal}\ge k-0.5)$. Beyond class notes

    Compare with Unit 2: the Poisson approximation is for $n$ large and $p$ small. The Gaussian approximation is for $n$ large with $npq$ also large.

    MediumExample 11.2: spare components

    A device uses one component at a time and replaces it when it fails. Lifetimes are iid exponential with mean 10 h. With 100 components, what is the probability that the device runs for more than 1100 h?

    Solution

    The total running time is $S=\sum_{i=1}^{100}X_i$. For an exponential, the standard deviation equals the mean, so μ = 10 and σ = 10.

    MediumExample 11.3: coin tosses

    A fair coin is tossed 100 times. Approximate P(at least 60 heads).

    Solution

    $X\sim$ Binomial(100, ½): mean $np=50$, standard deviation $\sqrt{npq}=\sqrt{25}=5$. "At least 60" on the integers corresponds to "above 59.5" on the continuous scale (continuity correction):

    $$\begin{aligned} P(X\ge60)&\approx P(\text{normal}\gt59.5)\\ &=Q\Big(\frac{59.5-50}{5}\Big)\\ &=Q(1.9)\\ &=\mathbf{0.0287}. \end{aligned}$$

    The exact binomial answer is 0.0284. Without the correction you'd get $Q(2)=0.0228$, noticeably worse.

    12Mistakes to avoid & self-check

    Top mark-losers in Unit 3
    1. Limits that ignore the support. If $f_{XY}$ lives on a triangle, a marginal integral "from 0 to 1" is wrong. It must run along the strip inside the triangle. Always draw the support first.
    2. Declaring independence because the formula factors when the support is not a rectangle (e.g. $f=2$ on $x+y\le1$).
    3. Uncorrelated ⇒ independent. False in general (Example 6.1; PYQ ES 2026 Q3b). True only for jointly Gaussian RVs.
    4. Mixing up correlation $E[XY]$, covariance $E[XY]-m_Xm_Y$ and ρ; or "orthogonal" with "uncorrelated".
    5. Writing $\Var(X-Y)=\Var X-\Var Y$. The correct formula is $\Var X+\Var Y-2\Cov(X,Y)$.
    6. For $Y=g(X)$: forgetting the absolute value in $f_X/|g'|$, forgetting a root (such as $-\sqrt\beta$), or forgetting to say $f_Y=0$ outside the range of $Y$.
    7. Missing the impulses when $g$ is flat on an interval (rectifier, limiter, dead-zone). Without them, the density part won't integrate to 1.
    8. Doing a convolution without cases. The integrand is non-zero only where both factors are, and those limits change with θ.
    9. $Y,Z$ iid $N(m,\sigma^2)$: $Y+Z\sim N(2m,2\sigma^2)$, but $2Y\sim N(2m,4\sigma^2)$. They are different RVs.
    10. max/min: $F_{\max}=F_XF_Y$ only for independent RVs. For the min, work with $P(\min\gt z)$.
    11. CLT: the standard deviation of a sum is $\sigma\sqrt n$, and of a mean $\sigma/\sqrt n$. It is never $n\sigma$.

    $$\Big(x+\frac y2\Big)^2=x^2+xy+\frac{y^2}{4}.$$

    On a rectangle, the integral of any monomial $x^ay^b$ splits into two simple integrals. Every quantity in the question is a sum of such pieces:

    $$I(a,b)=\int_0^2\int_0^3x^ay^b\,dy\,dx=\frac{2^{a+1}}{a+1}\cdot\frac{3^{b+1}}{b+1}.$$

    (i) Total.

    $$\begin{aligned} \iint\Big(x^2+xy+\frac{y^2}{4}\Big)&=I(2,0)+I(1,1)+\tfrac14I(0,2)\\ &=\frac83\cdot3+\frac42\cdot\frac92+\frac14\cdot2\cdot\frac{27}{3}\\ &=8+9+4.5\\ &=21.5,\\[4pt] k&=\frac{1}{21.5}=\mathbf{\frac{2}{43}}. \end{aligned}$$

    (ii) Moments. Multiply the integrand by $x$, $y$, $x^2$, $y^2$ or $xy$, which just raises the powers. Then add three monomial integrals and multiply by $k$:

    $$\begin{aligned} E[X]&=k\big[I(3,0)+I(2,1)+\tfrac14I(1,2)\big]=k\,[12+12+4.5]=\tfrac{2}{43}(28.5)=\tfrac{57}{43}=1.326\\[4pt] E[Y]&=k\big[I(2,1)+I(1,2)+\tfrac14I(0,3)\big]=k\,[12+18+10.125]=\tfrac{321}{172}=1.866\\[4pt] E[X^2]&=k\big[I(4,0)+I(3,1)+\tfrac14I(2,2)\big]=k\,[19.2+18+6]=\tfrac{432}{215}=2.009\\[4pt] E[Y^2]&=k\big[I(2,2)+I(1,3)+\tfrac14I(0,4)\big]=k\,[24+40.5+24.3]=\tfrac{888}{215}=4.130\\[4pt] E[XY]&=k\big[I(3,1)+I(2,2)+\tfrac14I(1,3)\big]=k\,[18+24+10.125]=\tfrac{417}{172}=2.424 \end{aligned}$$

    (For example, $I(3,0)=\frac{2^4}{4}\cdot\frac{3^1}{1}=4\cdot3=12$ and $I(1,2)=\frac{2^2}{2}\cdot\frac{3^3}{3}=2\cdot9=18$.)

    Now the variances and covariance:

    $$\begin{aligned} \sigma_X^2&=E[X^2]-E[X]^2=2.009-1.326^2=\mathbf{0.252},\\ \sigma_Y^2&=E[Y^2]-E[Y]^2=4.130-1.866^2=\mathbf{0.647},\\ \Cov(X,Y)&=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]\\ &=2.424-1.326\times1.866\\ &=\mathbf{-0.0495}\qquad\big(\text{exactly }-\tfrac{183}{3698}\big),\\ \rho&=\frac{-0.0495}{\sqrt{0.252\times0.647}}=-0.12 . \end{aligned}$$

    A weak negative relationship.

    (iii) $P(X\lt Y)$. In the rectangle, the region $\{x\lt y\}$ lies above the diagonal. For fixed $x\in(0,2)$, $y$ runs from $x$ up to 3:

    Inner integral first:

    $$\begin{aligned} \int_x^3\Big(x^2+xy+\frac{y^2}{4}\Big)\,dy&=x^2(3-x)+\frac{x(9-x^2)}{2}+\frac{27-x^3}{12}\\ &=\frac94+\frac92x+3x^2-\Big(1+\frac12+\frac1{12}\Big)x^3\\ &=\frac94+\frac92x+3x^2-\frac{19}{12}x^3 . \end{aligned}$$

    Then the outer integral over $x$ from 0 to 2:

    $$\begin{aligned} P(X\lt Y)&=k\int_0^2\Big(\frac94+\frac92x+3x^2-\frac{19}{12}x^3\Big)dx\\ &=\frac{2}{43}\Big(\frac92+9+8-\frac{19}{3}\Big)\\ &=\frac{2}{43}\cdot\frac{91}{6}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{91}{129}\approx0.705} \end{aligned}$$

    Sense check: the region above the diagonal has area $\int_0^2(3-x)\,dx=4$, which is $\frac46=\frac23$ of the rectangle. The density $(x+y/2)^2$ grows with $y$, and the region above the diagonal contains all the large-$y$ points (up to $y=3$). So the probability should be a little more than ⅔. 0.705 fits ✓.

    ES July 2024 Q2c (5 marks)

    Explain the marginal probability density function. What is the condition for independent random variables $X$ and $Y$?

    Model answer

    Marginal PDF. Given the joint PDF $f_{XY}(x,y)$ of two RVs, the marginal PDF of $X$ is the PDF of $X$ on its own, ignoring $Y$:

    $$f_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{XY}(x,y)\,dy,\qquad f_Y(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{XY}(x,y)\,dx .$$

    It comes from the marginal CDF $F_X(x)=F_{XY}(x,\infty)$ by differentiation. Geometrically, integrating out $y$ projects the joint density surface onto the $x$-axis. The marginals can always be found from the joint PDF, but not the other way round: different joint PDFs can have the same marginals. Example: for $f_{XY}=x+y$ on the unit square, $f_X(x)=x+\frac12$.

    Independence. $X$ and $Y$ are independent if and only if $F_{XY}(x,y)=F_X(x)F_Y(y)$ for all $x,y$. Equivalently, $f_{XY}(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)$ (continuous) or $p_{XY}(x_i,y_j)=p_X(x_i)p_Y(y_j)$ (discrete), for all values.

    A practical test: the joint PDF factors into a function of $x$ times a function of $y$, and its support is a rectangle.

    Consequences of independence: $f_{Y|X}=f_Y$; $E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]$; $\Cov(X,Y)=0$; $\Var(X+Y)=\Var X+\Var Y$; $M_{X+Y}=M_XM_Y$.

    ES July 2024 Q2a (5 marks)

    $f_{XY}(x,y)=2$ for $x,y\gt0$, $x+y\le1$, and 0 otherwise. Find the covariance of $X$ and $Y$.

    Solution

    What to notice. This is a uniform density on a triangle of area ½, so the height 2 is correct. The support is a triangle, not a rectangle, so every integral needs the strip limits "$y$ from 0 to $1-x$". The plan: $E[X]$, $E[Y]$, $E[XY]$, then the shortcut formula.

    Step 1: marginal of X.

    $$\begin{aligned} f_X(x)&=\int_0^{1-x}2\,dy\\ &=2(1-x),\qquad0\lt x\lt1 . \end{aligned}$$

    The PDF and the triangle are symmetric in $x$ and $y$, so $f_Y(y)=2(1-y)$.

    Step 2: means.

    $$\begin{aligned} E[X]&=\int_0^1x\cdot2(1-x)\,dx\\ &=2\Big(\frac12-\frac13\Big)\\ &=\frac13=E[Y]. \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3: correlation.

    $$\begin{aligned} E[XY]&=\int_0^1\left[\int_0^{1-x}2xy\,dy\right]dx\\ &=\int_0^12x\cdot\frac{(1-x)^2}{2}\,dx\\ &=\int_0^1x(1-2x+x^2)\,dx\\ &=\frac12-\frac23+\frac14\\ &=\frac1{12}. \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4: covariance.

    $$\begin{aligned} \Cov(X,Y)&=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]\\ &=\frac1{12}-\frac13\cdot\frac13\\ &=\frac{3-4}{36}\\ &=\boxed{-\frac{1}{36}} \end{aligned}$$

    Interpretation. Negative, as expected: on this triangle, a large $X$ leaves little room for $Y$. Going one step further for ρ:

    $$\begin{aligned} E[X^2]&=\int_0^12x^2(1-x)\,dx=2\Big(\frac13-\frac14\Big)=\frac16,\\ \sigma_X^2&=\frac16-\frac19=\frac1{18},\\ \rho&=\frac{-1/36}{1/18}=-\frac12 . \end{aligned}$$
    ES July 2024 Q2b MS 2025 Q5b (5 / 1 marks)

    Explain (i) correlation, (ii) covariance, (iii) correlation coefficient. (MS 2025: define the correlation coefficient ρ and interpret its values.)

    Model answer

    (i) Correlation. The correlation of $X$ and $Y$ is the second-order joint moment

    $$R_{XY}=E[XY]=\iint xy\,f_{XY}(x,y)\,dx\,dy\qquad\Big(\text{or }\sum_i\sum_jx_iy_j\,p_{ij}\Big).$$

    If $E[XY]=0$, $X$ and $Y$ are called orthogonal.

    (ii) Covariance.

    $$\Cov(X,Y)=E\big[(X-m_X)(Y-m_Y)\big]=E[XY]-m_Xm_Y .$$

    It measures whether $X$ and $Y$ tend to be on the same side of their means (positive covariance) or on opposite sides (negative). $\Cov(X,X)=\Var X$. If $\Cov=0$, $X,Y$ are uncorrelated. Independence implies uncorrelated, but not conversely. Covariance appears in $\Var(X+Y)=\sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2+2\Cov(X,Y)$. Its size depends on the units of $X$ and $Y$.

    (iii) Correlation coefficient.

    $$\rho_{XY}=\frac{\Cov(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y},$$

    the covariance normalised to be unit-free. Always $-1\le\rho\le1$ (by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality). Interpretation:

    • $\rho=+1$: $Y=aX+b$ exactly, with $a\gt0$ (a perfect increasing line). $\rho=-1$: an exact line with $a\lt0$.
    • $0\lt\rho\lt1$: $X$ and $Y$ tend to increase together, more strongly as $\rho\to1$. $-1\lt\rho\lt0$: one tends to decrease as the other increases.
    • $\rho=0$: uncorrelated, meaning no linear relationship. A nonlinear one may still exist (e.g. $Y=X^2$ with symmetric $X$). For jointly Gaussian RVs, ρ = 0 means independent.
    ES 2021 Q3c (5 marks)

    $f_{XY}(x,y)=\dfrac{x^2+y^2}{4\pi}e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}$ for all $x,y$. Comment on whether $X$ and $Y$ are uncorrelated, with proper justification.

    Solution

    What to notice. The PDF depends on $x$ only through $x^2$, and on $y$ only through $y^2$. So it is even in $x$ and even in $y$. Multiplying an even function by $x$ gives an odd function, and the integral of an odd function over $(-\infty,\infty)$ is 0. That kills $E[X]$, $E[Y]$ and $E[XY]$ without any real integration.

    Validity check (polar coordinates, with $x^2+y^2=r^2$):

    $$\begin{aligned} \iint f\,dx\,dy&=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\infty r^2e^{-r^2/2}\,r\,dr\,d\phi\\ &=\frac{2\pi}{4\pi}\int_0^\infty r^3e^{-r^2/2}\,dr\\ &=\frac12\cdot2=1\ ✓ &&\why{substitute $t=r^2/2$: $\int_0^\infty2te^{-t}dt=2$} \end{aligned}$$

    The moments.

    $$\begin{aligned} E[X]&=\iint x\,f\,dx\,dy=0 &&\why{integrand odd in x}\\ E[Y]&=0 &&\why{odd in y}\\ E[XY]&=\iint xy\,f\,dx\,dy=0 &&\why{odd in x for each fixed y}\\ \Cov(X,Y)&=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]=0 . \end{aligned}$$

    Conclusion: $X$ and $Y$ are uncorrelated (and orthogonal).

    Going further: they are not independent. (Worth adding for full marks.) Compute the marginal, using $\int e^{-y^2/2}dy=\sqrt{2\pi}$ and $\int y^2e^{-y^2/2}dy=\sqrt{2\pi}$:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_X(x)&=\frac{e^{-x^2/2}}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty(x^2+y^2)\,e^{-y^2/2}\,dy\\ &=\frac{e^{-x^2/2}}{4\pi}\Big(x^2\sqrt{2\pi}+\sqrt{2\pi}\Big)\\ &=\frac{(1+x^2)\,e^{-x^2/2}}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}, \end{aligned}$$

    and similarly for $Y$. Then

    $$f_X(x)f_Y(y)=\frac{(1+x^2)(1+y^2)}{8\pi}e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}\ne f_{XY}(x,y).$$

    At the origin, for example, $f_{XY}=0$ but $f_Xf_Y=\frac{1}{8\pi}$. So this is another case of uncorrelated but dependent. (It also shows that $X,Y$ are not jointly Gaussian, since for jointly Gaussian RVs uncorrelated would mean independent.)

    $X$ and $Y$ are independent. Show that (i) $E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]$, (ii) $\Var(X+Y)=\Var(X)+\Var(Y)$, (iii) $\Cov(X,Y)=0$.

    ∎

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Y(y)&=\frac{f_X(\tan^{-1}y)}{|g'(x)|}\\ &=\frac{1/\pi}{1+y^2}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{1}{\pi(1+y^2)},\quad-\infty\lt y\lt\infty} \end{aligned}$$

    This is the Cauchy distribution.

    Check: $\frac1\pi\big[\tan^{-1}y\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty}=\frac1\pi\big(\frac\pi2+\frac\pi2\big)=1$ ✓.

    Via the CDF (an equally good answer):

    $$\begin{aligned} F_Y(y)&=P(\tan X\le y)=P(X\le\tan^{-1}y)\\ &=\frac{\tan^{-1}y+\pi/2}{\pi}, \end{aligned}$$

    and differentiating gives the same PDF.

    Interpretation: when $X$ is near ±π/2, tan shoots off to ±∞. So the Cauchy has very heavy tails, so heavy that $E[Y]$ does not exist.

    ES 2022 Q3c (5 marks)

    Let $Y=e^X$. Find (i) the CDF of $Y$ in terms of the CDF of $X$; (ii) the PDF, mean and variance of $Y$ when $X$ is standard Gaussian.

    Solution

    What to notice. $e^x$ is increasing with range $(0,\infty)$, so $\{e^X\le y\}=\{X\le\ln y\}$. For the moments, don't integrate the log-normal PDF: $E[Y^k]=E[e^{kX}]$ is just the Gaussian MGF evaluated at $k$.

    (i) For $y\gt0$:

    $$\begin{aligned} F_Y(y)&=P(e^X\le y)\\ &=P(X\le\ln y) &&\why{ln is increasing}\\ &=\boxed{F_X(\ln y)}, \end{aligned}$$

    and $F_Y(y)=0$ for $y\le0$ ($e^X$ is always positive).

    (ii) PDF. Differentiate with the chain rule:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Y(y)&=f_X(\ln y)\cdot\frac{d}{dy}\ln y\\ &=\frac1y\,f_X(\ln y)\\ &=\frac{1}{y\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{(\ln y)^2}{2}\Big\},\qquad y\gt0 . \end{aligned}$$

    This is the log-normal distribution.

    Moments. For $X\sim N(0,1)$, $M_X(t)=e^{t^2/2}$. So $E[Y^k]=E[e^{kX}]=M_X(k)=e^{k^2/2}$:

    $$\begin{aligned} E[Y]&=M_X(1)=e^{1/2}=\mathbf{1.649},\\ E[Y^2]&=M_X(2)=e^{2}=7.389,\\ \Var Y&=e^2-\big(e^{1/2}\big)^2\\ &=e^2-e\\ &=e(e-1)=\mathbf{4.671}. \end{aligned}$$

    Interpretation: the mean $e^{1/2}$ is bigger than $e^{E[X]}=e^0=1$. Large values of $X$ get exponentially magnified, which drags the mean of $Y$ upwards (the long right tail in the plot).

    G. Functions of two random variables

    ES 2021 Q3b (5 marks)

    $X,Y$ have joint PDF $f_{XY}$ and joint CDF $F_{XY}$, and $W=\min(X,Y)$. (a) Find the CDF of $W$. (b) Find the PDF of $W$ if $X,Y$ are independent exponentials with parameters λ, μ.

    Solution

    What to notice. "The minimum is ≤ w" means "at least one is ≤ w", which is awkward. Its complement, "the minimum is > w", means "both are > w": a single quadrant. Work with the complement.

    (a) Use the north-east-quadrant formula from §1:

    $$\begin{aligned} F_W(w)&=1-P(W\gt w)\\ &=1-P(X\gt w,\ Y\gt w)\\ &=1-\big[1-F_X(w)-F_Y(w)+F_{XY}(w,w)\big]\\ &=F_X(w)+F_Y(w)-F_{XY}(w,w), \end{aligned}$$

    where $F_X(w)=F_{XY}(w,\infty)$ and $F_Y(w)=F_{XY}(\infty,w)$. (The region is the complement of the NE quadrant; see the figure in §10.)

    (b) With independence, $P(X\gt w,Y\gt w)=P(X\gt w)P(Y\gt w)$:

    $$\begin{aligned} F_W(w)&=1-\big[1-F_X(w)\big]\big[1-F_Y(w)\big]\\ &=1-e^{-\lambda w}\,e^{-\mu w}\\ &=1-e^{-(\lambda+\mu)w},\qquad w\ge0,\\[4pt] f_W(w)&=\frac{d}{dw}F_W(w)\\ &=(\lambda+\mu)\,e^{-(\lambda+\mu)w},\qquad w\ge0 . \end{aligned}$$

    So $W\sim$ Exp(λ + μ).

    Check with the general formula $f_X(1-F_Y)+f_Y(1-F_X)$:

    $$\lambda e^{-\lambda w}\cdot e^{-\mu w}+\mu e^{-\mu w}\cdot e^{-\lambda w}=(\lambda+\mu)e^{-(\lambda+\mu)w}\ ✓$$

    Interpretation: a series system of two components with failure rates λ and μ fails at the combined rate λ + μ.

    ES Nov–Dec 2024 Q3c (5 marks)

    $X$ and $Y$ are independent uniform RVs on $(0,1)$. Find the PDF of $Z=XY$.

    Solution

    What to notice. Two equally good routes: the CDF method (find the area under the hyperbola $xy=z$ inside the unit square), or the product formula from §10. Doing both is a good self-check. The answer lives on $(0,1)$, since a product of numbers in (0, 1) is in (0, 1).

    Method 1: CDF. Fix $0\lt z\lt1$. It's easier to compute $P(XY\gt z)$, the region above the hyperbola $y=z/x$. That region only exists where $z/x\lt1$, i.e. $x\gt z$. For such $x$, $y$ runs from $z/x$ up to 1:

    $$\begin{aligned} P(XY\gt z)&=\int_z^1\Big(1-\frac zx\Big)\,dx\\ &=\Big[x-z\ln x\Big]_z^1\\ &=(1-0)-(z-z\ln z)\\ &=1-z+z\ln z,\\[4pt] F_Z(z)&=1-P(XY\gt z)\\ &=z-z\ln z,\\[4pt] f_Z(z)&=\frac{d}{dz}\big(z-z\ln z\big)\\ &=1-\big(\ln z+1\big) &&\why{product rule on $z\ln z$}\\ &=-\ln z . \end{aligned}$$

    Method 2: formula. $f_Z(z)=\int\frac{1}{|x|}f_X(x)\,f_Y(z/x)\,dx$. The integrand is $\frac1x$ when both $0\lt x\lt1$ and $0\lt z/x\lt1$, i.e. when $z\lt x\lt1$:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Z(z)&=\int_z^1\frac{1}{x}\,dx\\ &=\Big[\ln x\Big]_z^1\\ &=-\ln z . \end{aligned}$$

    Answer:

    $$f_Z(z)=\begin{cases}-\ln z,&0\lt z\lt1\\0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

    Sketch: see Example 10.3. It is unbounded as $z\to0$, decreases, and reaches 0 at $z=1$. Area check: $\big[z-z\ln z\big]_0^1=1$ ✓.

    ES 2025 Q3c (5 marks)

    $X,Y$ are iid with $f(x)=e^{-x}u(x)$. Find the PDF of (1) $Z=X-Y$, (2) $R=XY$.

    Solution

    What to notice. For the difference, use $f_Z(z)=\int f_X(z+y)f_Y(y)\,dy$. The crucial point is where the integrand is non-zero: we need $y\gt0$ and $z+y\gt0$. That condition depends on the sign of $z$, so there are two cases. For the product, the formula gives an integral with no elementary closed form; say so rather than forcing an answer.

    (1) Z = X − Y. The integrand is $f_X(z+y)f_Y(y)=e^{-(z+y)}e^{-y}=e^{-z-2y}$ wherever both arguments are positive.

    Case z ≥ 0: $z+y\gt0$ holds automatically for every $y\gt0$:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Z(z)&=\int_0^\infty e^{-z-2y}\,dy\\ &=e^{-z}\Big[-\tfrac12e^{-2y}\Big]_0^\infty\\ &=\tfrac12e^{-z}. \end{aligned}$$

    Case z < 0: now $z+y\gt0$ requires $y\gt-z$ (a positive number):

    $$\begin{aligned} f_Z(z)&=\int_{-z}^\infty e^{-z-2y}\,dy\\ &=e^{-z}\cdot\tfrac12e^{-2(-z)}\\ &=\tfrac12e^{-z}e^{2z}\\ &=\tfrac12e^{z}. \end{aligned}$$

    Both cases together:

    $$f_Z(z)=\frac12e^{-|z|},\qquad-\infty\lt z\lt\infty\qquad(\text{Laplace}).$$

    Sense check: symmetric about 0, as it must be, because $X-Y$ and $Y-X$ have the same distribution when $X,Y$ are iid.

    (2) R = XY. Product formula, with $x\gt0$ and $r/x\gt0$:

    $$\begin{aligned} f_R(r)&=\int_0^\infty\frac{1}{x}\,f_X(x)\,f_Y\Big(\frac rx\Big)\,dx\\ &=\int_0^\infty\frac1x\,e^{-x}\,e^{-r/x}\,dx,\qquad r\gt0 . \end{aligned}$$

    This integral has no elementary closed form. (It equals $2K_0(2\sqrt r)$, where $K_0$ is a modified Bessel function; you are not expected to know this.) In the exam, leave the answer as the integral. Its CDF can be written as

    $$\begin{aligned} F_R(r)&=1-P(XY\gt r)\\ &=1-\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\,P\Big(Y\gt\frac rx\Big)\,dx\\ &=1-\int_0^\infty e^{-x-r/x}\,dx . \end{aligned}$$

    Exact facts you can state: $E[R]=E[X]E[Y]=1$ and $E[R^2]=E[X^2]E[Y^2]=2\times2=4$ (independence).

    H. Central limit theorem

    ES July 2024 Q3a ES June 2026 Q4b (5 marks)

    State and prove the central limit theorem.

    Model answer

    Statement. Let $X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n$ be iid with mean μ and finite variance σ², and let $S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n$. Then the standardised sum

    $$Z_n=\frac{S_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}$$

    converges in distribution to $N(0,1)$:

    $$\lim_{n\to\infty}P(Z_n\le z)=\Phi(z)=\int_{-\infty}^z\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^2/2}\,dt .$$

    Equivalently, for large $n$, $S_n\approx N(n\mu,\,n\sigma^2)$, whatever the distribution of the $X_i$.

    Proof (MGF method; assume the MGF exists near 0).

    1. Standardise. Let $Y_i=(X_i-\mu)/\sigma$. The $Y_i$ are iid with $E[Y_i]=0$ and $E[Y_i^2]=1$, and

    $$Z_n=\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^nY_i .$$

    2. Expand the MGF. By Taylor's theorem, using $M_Y(0)=1$, $M_Y'(0)=E[Y]=0$, $M_Y''(0)=E[Y^2]=1$:

    $$M_Y(t)=1+\frac{t^2}{2}+o(t^2).$$

    3. MGF of $Z_n$. Independence makes the MGF of the sum a product, and $M_{cY}(t)=M_Y(ct)$:

    $$\begin{aligned} M_{Z_n}(t)&=\Big[M_Y\Big(\frac{t}{\sqrt n}\Big)\Big]^n\\ &=\Big[1+\frac{t^2}{2n}+o\Big(\frac1n\Big)\Big]^n . \end{aligned}$$

    4. Limit. Since $\big(1+\frac an+o(\frac1n)\big)^n\to e^a$:

    $$\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{Z_n}(t)=e^{t^2/2}.$$

    5. Conclude. $e^{t^2/2}$ is the MGF of $N(0,1)$. By the uniqueness (continuity) theorem for MGFs, $Z_n\to N(0,1)$ in distribution. ∎

    Significance (one or two lines earn credit). It explains why noise, being the sum of many small independent effects, is Gaussian. It justifies the normal approximation to the binomial (mean $np$, variance $npq$) and to sums and averages of measurements. A sketch of the sum of uniforms turning bell-shaped (§11) is a good addition.
