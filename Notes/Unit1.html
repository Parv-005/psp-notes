    1Random experiments, sample space & events

    Why this matters

    Probability is a mathematical model of chance. Before we can calculate anything, we must say exactly what can happen (the sample space) and which questions we are allowed to ask (the events). Everything later in the course (random variables, processes, Markov chains) is built on this.

    Probabilistic model (lecturer's three conditions)

    An experiment can be modelled probabilistically if:

    1. it can be repeated under identical conditions;
    2. on every trial the outcome is subject to chance, and the measure of this chance is called probability;
    3. over a large number of trials the outcomes show statistical regularity: the relative frequencies settle down (see the graph in §5).

    For a die: a mathematical model needs three things:

    1. a sample space $\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$;
    2. events $E\subset\Omega$, e.g. $E=\{2,4,6\}$ = "even";
    3. a probability $E\mapsto P(E)$, the measure of the chance that $E$ occurs.

    The model is complete if and only if we can assign a probability to every event associated with $\Omega$.

    Definitions
    • Outcome $\zeta$: one possible result of a single trial.
    • Sample space $\Omega$: the set of all outcomes. It can be finite (die), countably infinite (number of tosses until the first head: $\{1,2,3,\dots\}$), or uncountable (a voltage reading in $[0,5]$).
    • Event: a subset of $\Omega$. An event "occurs" if the outcome lies in it.
    • Elementary (simple) event: a single outcome $\{\zeta\}$. Sure event: $\Omega$. Impossible event: $\varnothing$.
    EasyExample 1.1: writing sample spaces
    1. Toss a coin 3 times: $\Omega=\{HHH,HHT,HTH,HTT,THH,THT,TTH,TTT\}$, so 8 outcomes.
      Event "exactly two heads" $=\{HHT,HTH,THH\}$.
    2. Roll a die until a 6 appears; record the number of rolls: $\Omega=\{1,2,3,\dots\}$, countably infinite.
    3. Measure the lifetime of a bulb: $\Omega=[0,\infty)$, uncountable.

    2Set operations (event algebra)

    Since events are sets, combining events means doing set operations on them. Picture each one with a Venn diagram: the rectangle is $\Omega$, and the shaded part is the event.

    WordsSet notation
    at least one of $A$, $B$ occurs$A\cup B$
    both occur$A\cap B = AB$
    $A$ does not occur$A^c$
    $A$ occurs but not $B$$A\cap B^c = A-B$
    exactly one occurs (symmetric difference)$A\,\triangle\, B=(A\cap B^c)\cup(A^c\cap B)=(A\cup B)\cap\overline{(A\cap B)}$
    neither occurs$A^c\cap B^c=(A\cup B)^c$
    $A$ and $B$ cannot happen together (mutually exclusive / disjoint)$A\cap B=\varnothing$
    if $A$ occurs then $B$ occurs$A\subset B$
    Laws you must know
    • Commutative, associative: $A\cup B=B\cup A$, $(A\cup B)\cup C=A\cup(B\cup C)$, and the same for $\cap$.
    • Distributive: $A\cap(B\cup C)=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C)$ and $A\cup(B\cap C)=(A\cup B)\cap(A\cup C)$.
    • De Morgan: $\overline{A\cup B}=\bar A\cap\bar B$ and $\overline{A\cap B}=\bar A\cup\bar B$. These extend to any number of events.
    • Splitting a set: $A=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap B^c)$, and the two pieces are disjoint. This one identity solves half the exam problems.
    Partition

    Events $A_1,\dots,A_n$ form a partition of $\Omega$ if they are pairwise disjoint ($A_i\cap A_j=\varnothing$ for $i\ne j$) and together cover everything ($\bigcup_i A_i=\Omega$). Exactly one of them happens on every trial. Partitions are the basis of the Total Probability Theorem (§9).

    3σ-field (sigma algebra) & probability space

    Why do we need a σ-field?

    We want to assign probabilities to events and still be able to combine them. If we know $P(A)$, we must also be able to talk about $P(A^c)$. If we know $P(A_1),P(A_2),\dots$, we must be able to talk about "at least one of them". A σ-field is simply a collection of events that is closed under these operations, so the calculations never take us outside it.

    Definition: σ-field

    A collection $\mathcal F$ of subsets of a non-empty set $\Omega$ is a σ-field over $\Omega$ if

    1. $\Omega\in\mathcal F$;
    2. if $E\in\mathcal F$ then $E^c\in\mathcal F$ (closed under complement);
    3. if $E_1,E_2,\ldots\in\mathcal F$ then $\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_i\in\mathcal F$ (closed under countable union).

    Consequences: $\varnothing=\Omega^c\in\mathcal F$, and by De Morgan, $\mathcal F$ is also closed under countable intersections.

    Examples from class

    1. $\mathcal F_1=\{\varnothing,\Omega\}$: valid, the trivial (smallest) σ-field.
    2. With one event $E_1$: $\mathcal F_2=\{\Omega,\varnothing,E_1,E_1^c\}$ is valid. It is the smallest σ-field containing $E_1$.
    3. With two events $E_1,E_2$ we must include all their complements, unions and intersections: $\mathcal F_3=\{\Omega,\varnothing,E_1,E_1^c,E_2,E_2^c,E_1\cup E_2,\ E_1\cup E_2^c,\ E_1^c\cup E_2,\ E_1^c\cup E_2^c,\ \dots\}$. In general this has 16 members, generated by the 4 "atoms" $E_1E_2,\ E_1E_2^c,\ E_1^cE_2,\ E_1^cE_2^c$.
    4. $\Omega=\{1,2,3\}$:
      • $\mathcal F_0=\{\Omega,\varnothing\}$ (trivial)
      • $\mathcal F_1=\{\Omega,\varnothing,\{1\},\{2,3\}\}$
      • $\mathcal F_2=\{\Omega,\varnothing,\{1\},\{2\},\{2,3\},\{1,3\}\}$ (as written in class; note it is not closed under union, since $\{1\}\cup\{2\}=\{1,2\}$ is missing. Adding $\{1,2\}$ and $\{3\}$ gives the power set.)
      • $\mathcal F_3=$ the power set, all $2^3=8$ subsets. This is the largest σ-field.
    5. $\Omega=\mathbb R$: $\mathcal F_0=\{\varnothing,\mathbb R\}$ and $\mathcal F_1=\{\varnothing,\mathbb R,(-\infty,0),[0,\infty)\}$ are both valid.
    MediumExample 3.1: checking a σ-field

    $\Omega=\{1,2,3,4\}$. Is $\mathcal G=\{\varnothing,\Omega,\{1\},\{2,3,4\},\{1,2\}\}$ a σ-field? If not, find the smallest σ-field containing $\{1\}$ and $\{1,2\}$.

    Solution

    No. $\{1,2\}^c=\{3,4\}\notin\mathcal G$, so condition (ii) fails.

    The smallest σ-field containing $\{1\},\{1,2\}$ is built from the atoms $\{1\},\{2\},\{3,4\}$ (the finest pieces the two sets cut $\Omega$ into). Take all unions of atoms, $2^3=8$ sets:

    $\{\varnothing,\{1\},\{2\},\{3,4\},\{1,2\},\{1,3,4\},\{2,3,4\},\Omega\}$.

    Probability space

    A probability space is the 3-tuple $(\Omega,\mathcal F,P)$, where $P:\mathcal F\to[0,1]$ maps sets (the domain) to real numbers between 0 and 1 (the range). It measures the chance of occurrence of each element of $\mathcal F$.

    4Joint events (combined experiments)

    If experiment 1 has sample space $\Omega_1$ and experiment 2 has $\Omega_2$, doing both gives the combined sample space, which is the Cartesian product:

    $$\Omega_{12}=\Omega_1\times\Omega_2=\{(\zeta_1,\zeta_2):\zeta_1\in\Omega_1,\ \zeta_2\in\Omega_2\}.$$
    EasyExample 4.1 (class): coin + die

    Toss a coin, $\Omega_1=\{0,1\}$, then throw a die, $\Omega_2=\{1,\dots,6\}$. Then $\Omega_{12}=\{01,02,\dots,06,\ 11,12,\dots,16\}$, which has $2\times6=12$ outcomes. Any subset, e.g. $B=\{11,12,06,05\}\subset\Omega_{12}$, is a joint event.

    5Three definitions of probability

    (1) Laplace's classical definition

    $$P(A)=\frac{\text{number of outcomes favourable to }A}{\text{total number of possible outcomes}}$$

    Valid only when there are finitely many outcomes and they are equally likely. It is circular ("equally likely" already means "equally probable"), and it fails for a loaded die. This is where counting (§7) comes in.

    (2) Relative-frequency definition

    Repeat the experiment $n$ times. If $A$ occurs $n_A$ times, then

    $$P(A)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n_A}{n}.$$

    This captures "statistical regularity". The graph below simulates 600 throws of a fair die (fixed random seed) and plots the running fraction of sixes. It wanders a lot early on, then settles near $1/6\approx0.167$. Its weakness is that we can never actually take $n\to\infty$.

    (3) Kolmogorov's axiomatic definition (the one we actually use)

    Axioms of probability

    Let $\Omega$ be a sample space and $\mathcal F$ a σ-field over $\Omega$. A real-valued set function $P$ on $\mathcal F$ is a probability if

    1. $P(\Omega)=1$
    2. $P(E)\ge0$ for every $E\in\mathcal F$
    3. if $E_1\cap E_2=\varnothing$ (mutually exclusive) then $P(E_1\cup E_2)=P(E_1)+P(E_2)$.
      Countable version: if $A_1,A_2,\dots$ are pairwise disjoint, $P\big(\bigcup_{k=1}^\infty A_k\big)=\sum_{k=1}^\infty P(A_k)$.

    The axioms do not say what the probabilities are. They are the rules any sensible assignment must obey. The classical and relative-frequency definitions both satisfy them.

    6Consequences of the axioms (with proofs)

    Every rule below follows from the three axioms. Exams often say "prove using the axioms", so know these proofs.

    Result 1: $P(\varnothing)=0$

    Proof. $\Omega=\Omega\cup\varnothing$, and $\Omega,\varnothing$ are mutually exclusive. By axiom (iii), $P(\Omega)=P(\Omega)+P(\varnothing)$, so $P(\varnothing)=0$. ∎

    Result 2: complement rule $P(E^c)=1-P(E)$

    Proof. $E\cup E^c=\Omega$ and $E\cap E^c=\varnothing$. By axioms (iii) and (i), $P(E)+P(E^c)=1$. ∎

    Use it whenever a question says "at least one": $P(\text{at least one})=1-P(\text{none})$.

    Result 3: $P(A\cap B^c)=P(A)-P(A\cap B)$

    Proof. $A=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap B^c)$, and the two pieces are disjoint. By axiom (iii), $P(A)=P(AB)+P(AB^c)$. ∎

    Result 4: monotonicity. If $A\subset B$ then $P(A)\le P(B)$; also $0\le P(A)\le1$

    Proof. If $A\subset B$ then $B=A\cup(B\cap A^c)$ (disjoint), so $P(B)=P(A)+P(BA^c)\ge P(A)$ by axiom (ii). Taking $B=\Omega$ gives $P(A)\le1$. ∎

    Result 5: addition rule $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$

    Proof. $A\cup B=A\cup(A^c\cap B)$ is a disjoint union, so $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(A^cB)$. By Result 3 (with the roles swapped), $P(A^cB)=P(B)-P(AB)$. ∎

    Why subtract? The overlap $A\cap B$ is counted once inside $A$ and again inside $B$.

    Result 6: inclusion–exclusion for three events PYQ ×2 $$P(A\cup B\cup C)=P(A)+P(B)+P(C)-P(AB)-P(AC)-P(BC)+P(ABC)$$

    Proof. Put $D=B\cup C$. By Result 5, $P(A\cup D)=P(A)+P(D)-P(A\cap D)$.

    • $P(D)=P(B)+P(C)-P(BC)$.
    • By the distributive law $A\cap D=(AB)\cup(AC)$, so $P(A\cap D)=P(AB)+P(AC)-P(AB\cap AC)=P(AB)+P(AC)-P(ABC)$.

    Substituting both gives the result. ∎

    Useful inequalities
    • Boole: $P(A\cup B)\le P(A)+P(B)$, and in general $P(\bigcup A_i)\le\sum P(A_i)$.
    • Bonferroni: $P(A\cap B)\ge P(A)+P(B)-1$. For example $P(A)=0.9$ and $P(B)=0.8$ give $P(AB)\ge0.7$.
    • $P(A\cap B)\le \min\{P(A),P(B)\}\le \max\{P(A),P(B)\}\le P(A\cup B)$.
    MediumExample 6.1: everything from three numbers

    Given $P(A)=0.9$, $P(B)=0.8$ and $P(A\cap B)=0.75$, find $P(A\cup B)$, $P(A\cap B^c)$, $P(A^c\cap B)$, $P(A^c\cap B^c)$ and $P(A^c\cup B^c)$.

    Solution
    • $P(A\cup B)=0.9+0.8-0.75=\mathbf{0.95}$
    • $P(AB^c)=P(A)-P(AB)=0.9-0.75=\mathbf{0.15}$
    • $P(A^cB)=P(B)-P(AB)=0.8-0.75=\mathbf{0.05}$
    • $P(A^cB^c)=P\big((A\cup B)^c\big)=1-0.95=\mathbf{0.05}$ (De Morgan)
    • $P(A^c\cup B^c)=P\big((AB)^c\big)=1-0.75=\mathbf{0.25}$

    Check: the four "atoms" $AB, AB^c, A^cB, A^cB^c$ must add to 1: $0.75+0.15+0.05+0.05=1$ ✓.

    Two examples from class (infinite sequences)

    MediumExample 6.2 (class)

    (a) A fair coin is tossed repeatedly. What is the probability that the first head occurs on the $n$-th toss?
    (b) What is the probability that a head occurs within the first $n$ tosses? What is the probability that a head occurs eventually?

    Solution

    (a) "First head on toss $n$" means the sequence $TT\cdots TH$ ($n-1$ tails, then a head). Its probability is $(1/2)^{n-1}\cdot(1/2)=2^{-n}$.

    (b) Let $A_i$ = "first head on toss $i$". These events are mutually exclusive, so by axiom (iii)

    $$P(\text{head within }n\text{ tosses})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}2^{-i}=1-2^{-n}.$$

    Equivalently, $1-P(\text{all }n\text{ tails})=1-2^{-n}$. As $n\to\infty$ this tends to $1$: a head occurs eventually with probability 1 (countable additivity).

    7Counting (combinatorial analysis) Beyond class notes

    Why

    The classical definition needs "number of favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes". When outcomes number in the thousands you can't list them, so you count them with these rules. The syllabus lists "Basics of Combinatorial Analysis".

    The counting toolkit
    1. Multiplication principle: if a task is done in $k$ stages with $n_1,n_2,\dots,n_k$ choices each, there are $n_1n_2\cdots n_k$ ways. For example, a 4-digit PIN has $10^4$ possibilities.
    2. Permutations (order matters, no repetition): arranging $k$ out of $n$ distinct objects gives $^nP_k=\dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!}$. Arranging all $n$ gives $n!$.
    3. Combinations (order doesn't matter): choosing $k$ out of $n$ gives $\binom nk=\dfrac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$.
    4. With repetition, ordered: $n^k$, e.g. $k$ tosses of a coin give $2^k$ sequences.
    5. Arrangements with identical objects: $\dfrac{n!}{n_1!\,n_2!\cdots n_r!}$ (multinomial). For example, BANANA has $\frac{6!}{3!\,2!\,1!}=60$ arrangements.
    Recipe: which formula?
    1. Does order matter (positions, ranks, sequences)? Yes → permutations / $n^k$. No → combinations.
    2. Is repetition allowed (with replacement)? Yes → $n^k$. No → $^nP_k$ or $\binom nk$.
    3. For "exactly $r$ of type 1 and $s$ of type 2" from a mixed group, use $\dfrac{\binom{N_1}{r}\binom{N_2}{s}}{\binom{N_1+N_2}{r+s}}$ (hypergeometric).
    4. For "at least one", count the complement.
    EasyExample 7.1: committee (Schaum 1.33)

    A committee of 5 is chosen at random from 5 men and 10 women. Find P(2 men and 3 women) and P(all women).

    Solution

    Total number of committees: $\binom{15}{5}=3003$, all equally likely.

    P(2M, 3W) $=\dfrac{\binom52\binom{10}3}{3003}=\dfrac{10\cdot120}{3003}=\dfrac{1200}{3003}\approx\mathbf{0.400}$.

    P(all women) $=\dfrac{\binom{10}5}{3003}=\dfrac{252}{3003}\approx\mathbf{0.084}$.

    MediumExample 7.2: birthday problem (Schaum 1.32)

    $n$ people are in a room (365 equally likely birthdays, ignoring leap years). Find P(at least two share a birthday).

    Solution

    Count the complement A = "all birthdays different". In order, the people can have $365\cdot364\cdots(365-n+1)$ distinct-birthday assignments, out of $365^n$ total.

    $$P(\text{at least two share})=1-\frac{365\cdot364\cdots(365-n+1)}{365^n}.$$

    For $n=23$ this is $\approx\mathbf{0.507}$, which is already more than half. This surprises most people.

    MediumExample 7.3: drawing cards

    Two cards are drawn without replacement from a 52-card deck. Find P(both aces).

    Solution

    Counting: $\dfrac{\binom42}{\binom{52}2}=\dfrac{6}{1326}=\dfrac1{221}$.

    Sequentially (multiplication rule, §8): $\dfrac4{52}\cdot\dfrac3{51}=\dfrac1{221}$. Both methods agree.

    8Conditional probability & the multiplication rule

    Why

    Information changes probabilities. If you're told "the die shows an even number", the chance of a 6 rises from 1/6 to 1/3. Conditioning on $B$ means throwing away every outcome outside $B$ and re-scaling, so $B$ becomes the new sample space.

    Definition $$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)\gt0.$$

    Relative-frequency motivation (class): in $n$ trials, $B$ occurs $n_B$ times and both occur $n_{AB}$ times. Among the trials where $B$ occurred, the fraction where $A$ also occurred is

    $$\frac{n_{AB}}{n_B}=\frac{n_{AB}/n}{n_B/n}\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{P(AB)}{P(B)}.$$

    Rearranging the definition both ways gives $P(AB)=P(A\mid B)P(B)=P(B\mid A)P(A)$, and therefore

    $$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A)\,P(B\mid A)}{P(B)}\qquad\text{(the seed of Bayes' rule)}$$
    Observations (class) and properties
    1. $P(A\mid B)\ge0$.
    2. If $B\subset A$ then $P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(B)}{P(B)}=1$: if $B$ happened, $A$ surely happened.
    3. If $A\subset B$ then $P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(A)}{P(B)}\ge P(A)$: knowing $B$ can only make $A$ more likely.
    4. If $A\cap B=\varnothing$ then $P(A\mid B)=0$.
    5. $P(\Omega\mid B)=1$, and $P(A\cup C\mid B)=P(A\mid B)+P(C\mid B)$ if $A\cap C=\varnothing$. So $P(\cdot\mid B)$ satisfies all three axioms: it is a genuine probability on the reduced space $B$.
    6. Hence every rule from §6 holds conditionally, e.g. $P(A^c\mid B)=1-P(A\mid B)$.
    Multiplication (chain) rule

    If $P(A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_{n-1})\gt0$, then

    $$P(A_1\cap A_2\cap\cdots\cap A_n)=P(A_1)\,P(A_2\mid A_1)\,P(A_3\mid A_1A_2)\cdots P(A_n\mid A_1\cdots A_{n-1}).$$

    Use it for sequential draws without replacement. It is also the same idea behind Markov chains in Unit 5.

    EasyExample 8.1: dice with information (Schaum 1.40)

    Two fair dice are thrown. Let A = "both faces equal". Find $P(A)$, and $P(A\mid B)$ where B = "sum ≤ 3".

    Solution

    $P(A)=6/36=1/6$. $B=\{(1,1),(1,2),(2,1)\}$ and $A\cap B=\{(1,1)\}$.

    $P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{1/36}{3/36}=\mathbf{\tfrac13}$. Equivalently, the reduced space $B$ has 3 equally likely points and 1 of them is in A. The information doubled the probability.

    MediumExample 8.2: chips without replacement (Schaum 1.42)

    A lot of 100 chips contains 20 defective. Two are drawn without replacement. Find P(both defective).

    Solution

    Let $D_1$, $D_2$ be the events that the first and second chips are defective. Then $P(D_1)=20/100$ and $P(D_2\mid D_1)=19/99$, since 99 chips remain and 19 of them are defective.

    By the chain rule, $P(D_1D_2)=0.2\times\frac{19}{99}\approx\mathbf{0.0384}$.

    ExamExample 8.3: proving the chain rule for 3 events MS 2024 Q1a(c)

    Show that $P(A\cap B\cap C)=P(A\mid B\cap C)\,P(B\mid C)\,P(C)$.

    Solution

    Use the definition of conditional probability twice:

    $$\text{RHS}=\frac{P(A\cap B\cap C)}{P(B\cap C)}\cdot\frac{P(B\cap C)}{P(C)}\cdot P(C)=P(A\cap B\cap C).\ \blacksquare$$

    This assumes $P(B\cap C)\gt0$.

    9Independence

    Definition

    Events $A$ and $B$ are independent if

    $$P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B).$$

    Equivalently, when $P(B)\gt0$: $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$. Knowing that B happened doesn't change the chance of A.

    Common mistake: independent ≠ mutually exclusive

    Mutually exclusive means $P(AB)=0$: if one happens, the other cannot. That is strong dependence. If $P(A),P(B)\gt0$, disjoint events are never independent, because $0\ne P(A)P(B)$.

    Independence is about probabilities. You usually can't see it on a Venn diagram.

    Theorem (class): complements of independent events are independent

    If $A,B$ are independent, so are $(A,\bar B)$, $(\bar A,B)$ and $(\bar A,\bar B)$.

    Proof.

    1. $P(A\bar B)=P(A)-P(AB)=P(A)-P(A)P(B)=P(A)\big(1-P(B)\big)=P(A)P(\bar B)$.
    2. $(\bar A,B)$ follows by the same argument with the roles swapped.
    3. $P(\bar A\bar B)=P(\overline{A\cup B})=1-P(A)-P(B)+P(A)P(B)=\big(1-P(A)\big)\big(1-P(B)\big)=P(\bar A)P(\bar B)$. ∎
    Three or more events

    $A,B,C$ are (mutually) independent if all four of these hold:

    $$P(AB)=P(A)P(B),\quad P(AC)=P(A)P(C),\quad P(BC)=P(B)P(C),\quad P(ABC)=P(A)P(B)P(C).$$

    If only the first three hold, they are pairwise independent. Pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence (see the dice PYQ in §12). For $n$ events, every sub-collection must factorise, which is $2^n-n-1$ conditions.

    Reliability of systems (independent components)

    Component $k$ fails with probability $p_k$, independently of the others.

    • Series (works only if all work): $P(\text{works})=\prod_{k=1}^n(1-p_k)$.
    • Parallel (works if at least one works): $P(\text{works})=1-\prod_{k=1}^n p_k$.

    10Total probability theorem & Bayes' rule

    Why

    Often $P(B)$ is hard to get directly, but easy once you know which "case" you're in: which factory, which basket, male or female. Total probability averages over the cases. Bayes then reverses the conditioning: from "P(effect | cause)" to "P(cause | effect)".

    Total probability theorem

    Let $A_1,A_2,\dots,A_n$ be a partition of $\Omega$, i.e. $A_i\cap A_j=\varnothing$ for $i\ne j$ and $\bigcup A_i=\Omega$. Then for any event $B$,

    $$P(B)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}P(B\mid A_i)\,P(A_i).$$

    Proof (class). $B=B\cap\Omega=B\cap\big(\bigcup_i A_i\big)=\bigcup_i(B\cap A_i)$, and the pieces $B\cap A_i$ are disjoint. By axiom (iii), $P(B)=\sum_iP(B\cap A_i)=\sum_iP(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)$. ∎

    Bayes' theorem $$P(A_i\mid B)=\frac{P(A_i)\,P(B\mid A_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(A_j)\,P(B\mid A_j)}$$

    Here $P(A_i)$ is the prior (before seeing B), $P(B\mid A_i)$ is the likelihood, and $P(A_i\mid B)$ is the posterior (after seeing B). The denominator is just $P(B)$ from total probability.

    Recipe for every Bayes problem
    1. Name the cases $A_1,\dots,A_n$ (they must form a partition) and the observed event $B$.
    2. Write the priors $P(A_i)$ and the likelihoods $P(B\mid A_i)$ from the question.
    3. Draw a tree: first branch = case, second branch = B or not B. Multiply along each path.
    4. $P(B)$ = sum of the path products that end in B.
    5. $P(A_i\mid B)$ = (path product for $A_i$ and B) ÷ $P(B)$.
    MediumExample 10.1 (class): smokers

    45% of a population are female and 55% male. 70% of males and 10% of females smoke. Find the probability that a randomly chosen smoker is male.

    Solution

    Cases: M, F with $P(M)=0.55$ and $P(F)=0.45$. Observed: S (smoker), with $P(S\mid M)=0.7$ and $P(S\mid F)=0.1$.

    $P(S)=0.55(0.7)+0.45(0.1)=0.385+0.045=0.43$

    $$P(M\mid S)=\frac{0.385}{0.43}=\mathbf{0.895}$$

    Before the information, P(male) was 0.55. Learning "smoker" raised it to 0.895.

    MediumExample 10.2: two plants (Schaum 1.41)

    Plant 1 makes 1000 parts, of which 100 are defective. Plant 2 makes 2000 parts, of which 150 are defective. A randomly chosen part is defective. Find P(it came from plant 1).

    Solution

    $P(P_1)=1/3$, $P(D\mid P_1)=0.1$, $P(P_2)=2/3$, $P(D\mid P_2)=0.075$.

    $P(D)=\frac13(0.1)+\frac23(0.075)=\frac{250}{3000}$.

    $P(P_1\mid D)=\dfrac{100/3000}{250/3000}=\mathbf{0.4}$.

    Shortcut: 100 of the 250 defective parts come from plant 1.

    ExamExample 10.3: binary channel

    A binary channel sends 0 with probability 0.6 and 1 with probability 0.4. A sent 0 is received as 1 with probability 0.1. A sent 1 is received as 0 with probability 0.2. Find (a) P(receive 1), (b) P(sent 1 | received 1), (c) P(error).

    2. $P(S_1\mid R_1)=0.32/0.38\approx\mathbf{0.842}$
    3. An error happens when 0 is sent and 1 received, or 1 is sent and 0 received: $0.6(0.1)+0.4(0.2)=\mathbf{0.14}$

    11Common mistakes & self-check

    Common mistakes in Unit 1
    • Writing $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)$ without checking that $A\cap B=\varnothing$.
    • Assuming independence when the problem doesn't say so, especially for draws without replacement, which are dependent.
    • Confusing $P(A\mid B)$ with $P(B\mid A)$. In general they are very different, and Bayes converts one into the other.
    • Thinking "disjoint" means "independent". It's the opposite (see §9).
    • Using Bayes with cases that don't form a partition (overlapping, or not covering everything).
    • For "at least one", adding probabilities instead of using $1-P(\text{none})$.
    • Taking pairwise independence of three events as enough for $P(ABC)=P(A)P(B)P(C)$.

    Self-check (answers in the Answer Key)

    1. Can two events with positive probability be both mutually exclusive and independent?
    2. If $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$, is $P(B\mid A)=P(B)$? (Assume both probabilities are positive.)
    3. Is $\{\varnothing,\Omega,\{a\},\{b\}\}$ a σ-field on $\Omega=\{a,b,c\}$?
    4. If $A\subset B$, which is bigger: $P(A\mid B)$ or $P(A)$?
    5. True or false: $P(A\cup B\mid C)=P(A\mid C)+P(B\mid C)$ always.
    6. How many members does the power-set σ-field of a 4-element $\Omega$ have?

    A. Axioms, set identities & the addition rule

    ES 2022/23 Q1a (5 marks)

    $P[A]=1/4$, $P[B]=1/8$, $P[C]=5/8$, $P[D]=3/8$. A and B are disjoint, while C and D are independent.
    (i) Find $P[A\cap B]$, $P[A\cup B]$, $P[A\cap B^c]$, $P[A\cup B^c]$. (ii) Are A and B independent? (iii) Find $P[C\cap D^c]$ and $P[C^c\cup D^c]$. (iv) Are $C^c$ and $D^c$ independent?

    Solution

    (i) Disjoint means $P[A\cap B]=\mathbf 0$, so $P[A\cup B]=\frac14+\frac18=\mathbf{\frac38}$.
    $P[A\cap B^c]=P[A]-P[A\cap B]=\mathbf{\frac14}$.
    Since $A\cap B=\varnothing$, $A\subset B^c$, hence $A\cup B^c=B^c$ and $P[A\cup B^c]=1-\frac18=\mathbf{\frac78}$.

    (ii) $P[A]P[B]=\frac1{32}\ne0=P[A\cap B]$, so they are not independent. Disjoint events with positive probabilities are always dependent.

    (iii) $C$, $D^c$ are independent (theorem in §9), so $P[C\cap D^c]=\frac58\cdot\frac58=\mathbf{\frac{25}{64}}$.
    $P[C^c\cup D^c]=1-P[C\cap D]=1-\frac58\cdot\frac38=1-\frac{15}{64}=\mathbf{\frac{49}{64}}$.

    (iv) Yes. If C and D are independent, so are their complements: $P[C^cD^c]=\frac38\cdot\frac58=\frac{15}{64}=P[C^c]P[D^c]$.

    ES 2022/23 Q1b (5 marks)

    Show that (i) $P[A\cup B]\ge P[A]$, (ii) $P[A\cup B]\ge P[B]$, (iii) $P[A\cap B]\le P[A]$, (iv) $P[A\cap B]\le P[B]$.

    Solution

    All four follow from monotonicity: if $E\subset F$ then $P(E)\le P(F)$. Proof: $F=E\cup(F\cap E^c)$ is a disjoint union, so $P(F)=P(E)+P(F\cap E^c)\ge P(E)$ by axioms (iii) and (ii).

    • (i), (ii): $A\subset A\cup B$ and $B\subset A\cup B$.
    • (iii), (iv): $A\cap B\subset A$ and $A\cap B\subset B$.

    Alternatively, for (i): $P[A\cup B]-P[A]=P[B]-P[AB]=P[A^cB]\ge0$.

    ES 2022/23 Q1c (5 marks)

    The symmetric difference is $A\triangle B=(A\cup B)\cap\overline{(A\cap B)}$. Show that (i) $P(\bar A\cup\bar B)=1-P(A\cap B)$, (ii) $P(A\cap B)=1-P(\bar A)-P(\bar B)$, (iii) $P(A\triangle B)=P(A\cup B)-P(A\cap B)$.

    Solution

    (i) De Morgan: $\bar A\cup\bar B=\overline{A\cap B}$. So by the complement rule, $P(\bar A\cup\bar B)=1-P(A\cap B)$. ∎

    (ii) Careful: as printed, this is not true in general. The correct identity is

    $$P(A\cap B)=1-P(\bar A\cup\bar B)=1-\big[P(\bar A)+P(\bar B)-P(\bar A\cap\bar B)\big]=1-P(\bar A)-P(\bar B)+P(\bar A\cap\bar B).$$

    It reduces to the printed form exactly when $P(\bar A\cap\bar B)=0$, i.e. when $A\cup B$ is the whole sample space. In general we only get the inequality $P(A\cap B)\ge1-P(\bar A)-P(\bar B)$ (Bonferroni). In the exam, prove the correct identity and state this condition.

    (iii) $A\cap B\subset A\cup B$, so $A\cup B=(A\triangle B)\cup(A\cap B)$, a disjoint union: the overlap and the "exactly one" part. By axiom (iii), $P(A\cup B)=P(A\triangle B)+P(A\cap B)$. ∎

    Equivalently, $P(A\triangle B)=P(A)+P(B)-2P(A\cap B)$.

    B. Conditional probability & independence

    MS 2025 Q1b (1 mark)

    Find $P[A\mid B]$ if (i) $A\cap B=\varnothing$, (ii) $A\subset B$, (iii) $A\supset B$.

    Solution

    (i) $0$. (ii) $P(A\cap B)=P(A)$, so the answer is $P(A)/P(B)$. (iii) $A\cap B=B$, so the answer is $1$.

    ES 2025 Q1a (5 marks)

    Events A and B are independent. Which of the following are true and which are false? Give reasons.
    (i) $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$   (ii) $P(A\cup B\mid C)=P(A\mid C)+P(B\mid C)$   (iii) $P(A)=0$, or $P(B)=0$, or both   (iv) $\dfrac{P(A\mid B)}{P(B)}=\dfrac{P(B\mid A)}{P(A)}$   (v) $P(AB)=P(A)P(B)$

    Solution
    1. True (for $P(B)\gt0$): $P(A\mid B)=P(AB)/P(B)=P(A)P(B)/P(B)=P(A)$.
    2. False in general. In fact $P(A\cup B\mid C)=P(A\mid C)+P(B\mid C)-P(AB\mid C)$, so it holds only if $P(ABC)=0$. Independence doesn't make that term vanish. Counterexample: take $C=\Omega$. Then LHS $=P(A)+P(B)-P(A)P(B)\ne P(A)+P(B)$ when both are positive.
    3. False. Independence doesn't force a zero probability. For two fair coin tosses, A = "first is H" and B = "second is H" are independent with $P=\frac12$ each. (Statement (iii) would follow if A and B were both independent and mutually exclusive.)
    4. False in general. With independence, LHS $=P(A)/P(B)$ and RHS $=P(B)/P(A)$. These are equal only if $P(A)=P(B)$.
    5. True: this is the definition of independence.
    ES 2025 Q1c (5 marks)

    Two fair dice are thrown. A = first die odd, B = second die odd, C = sum odd. Show that A, B, C are pairwise independent but not independent.

    Solution

    $P(A)=P(B)=\frac12$. The sum is odd exactly when one die is odd and the other even, so $P(C)=\frac12\cdot\frac12+\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac12$.

    • $P(AB)=\frac14=P(A)P(B)$ ✓
    • $A\cap C$ = first odd and second even, so $P(AC)=\frac14=P(A)P(C)$ ✓
    • $B\cap C$ = second odd and first even, so $P(BC)=\frac14=P(B)P(C)$ ✓

    So they are pairwise independent. But $A\cap B\cap C$ = both odd and sum odd, which is impossible (odd + odd = even). So $P(ABC)=0\ne\frac18=P(A)P(B)P(C)$, and they are not mutually independent. ∎

    MS 2023 Q4b (1 mark)

    A number is chosen at random from $\{1,\dots,100\}$. Given that it is divisible by 2, find P(it is divisible by 3 or 5).

    Solution

    Given "even", the reduced space has 50 numbers. Even numbers divisible by 3 are multiples of 6: 16 of them. Even numbers divisible by 5 are multiples of 10: 10 of them. Both are multiples of 30: 3 of them.

    14Formula sheet: Unit 1

    Axioms

    $P(\Omega)=1$, $P(E)\ge0$, and $P(\cup A_k)=\sum P(A_k)$ for disjoint $A_k$.

    Basic rules

    $P(\varnothing)=0$
    $P(A^c)=1-P(A)$
    $P(AB^c)=P(A)-P(AB)$
    $A\subset B\Rightarrow P(A)\le P(B)$

    Addition

    $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(AB)$
    $P(A\cup B\cup C)=\sum P(A)-\sum P(AB)+P(ABC)$

    De Morgan

    $\overline{A\cup B}=\bar A\bar B$,   $\overline{AB}=\bar A\cup\bar B$

    Counting

    $^nP_k=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}$,   $\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$
    ordered with replacement: $n^k$
    identical objects: $\frac{n!}{n_1!\cdots n_r!}$

    Conditional

    $P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(AB)}{P(B)}$
    $P(A_1\cdots A_n)=P(A_1)P(A_2\mid A_1)\cdots$

    Independence

    $P(AB)=P(A)P(B)$. It carries over to complements. For 3 events, all 4 product conditions must hold.

    Systems

    series: $\prod(1-p_k)$;   parallel: $1-\prod p_k$

    Total probability

    $P(B)=\sum_i P(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)$, with $\{A_i\}$ a partition

    Bayes

    $P(A_i\mid B)=\dfrac{P(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)}{\sum_j P(B\mid A_j)P(A_j)}$

    Symmetric difference

    $P(A\triangle B)=P(A\cup B)-P(AB)=P(A)+P(B)-2P(AB)$

    Bounds

    $P(AB)\ge P(A)+P(B)-1$ (Bonferroni)
    $P(\cup A_i)\le\sum P(A_i)$ (Boole)
