    How to use this unit

    Unit 4 said that a random process is "a random variable for every instant of time", and that describing all those variables jointly is usually hopeless. This unit studies the most useful family of processes for which it is easy: processes with a short memory. In a Markov process, once you know where the process is now, its past history tells you nothing extra about its future. That one assumption turns hard probability into simple matrix arithmetic, and it is the basis of queueing theory (how long will I wait at the bank?), of Google's PageRank, of weather and speech models, and of much of communication-network design.

    You said you are starting from zero, so this unit is written slowly. Every derivation is shown one step per line, with the reason in grey, and after every result there is a paragraph saying what it means. The exam questions in this unit are very predictable. They are almost always one of these:

    • a Markov chain numerical: draw the diagram, find a path probability, P², p(n), the long-run (stationary) distribution;
    • "is this process Markov?";
    • "explain / derive" M/M/1 (L, Lq), M/M/1/N, or the birth–death equilibrium equations;
    • an M/M/1 numerical (watch-repair shop, drive-in bank, computer centre).

    All of them are solved in §17. Read §1–§8 carefully first, though: the queueing part (§10–§13) is just a special Markov chain, and it only makes sense after the chain machinery.

    0Unit map, notation & maths warm-up

    The unit has four blocks.

    1. Discrete-time Markov chains: the machinery (§1–§6). The Markov property; why a chain is completely described by a starting PMF plus a transition matrix $P$; how to go $n$ steps ahead ($P^n$, the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation); how the PMF of the state evolves, $\mathbf p(n)=\mathbf p(0)P^n$.
    2. Long-run behaviour (§7–§8). Which states are visited forever and which are eventually abandoned (classification of states); the stationary distribution π with $\pi P=\pi$; when $\mathbf p(n)\to\pi$; and ergodicity: π is also the long-run fraction of time spent in each state.
    3. Continuous time (§9–§10). Chains that can jump at any instant: exponential holding times, transition rates, the generator matrix $Q$, the balance equations $\pi Q=0$. The Poisson process and the birth–death process are the key examples.
    4. Queues (§11–§13). A queue is a birth–death process: arrivals are births, service completions are deaths. This gives the M/M/1 and M/M/1/N formulas for the mean number in the system and the mean waiting time.

    Maths warm-up

    This unit needs only school algebra plus a little matrix multiplication. Make sure each of these is comfortable before §3.

    W1. Row vector × matrix

    A row vector times a square matrix gives a row vector. Entry $j$ of the result is the row vector "dotted" with column $j$ of the matrix:

    $$\begin{bmatrix}a&b\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}p_{00}&p_{01}\\p_{10}&p_{11}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a\,p_{00}+b\,p_{10}&\ \ a\,p_{01}+b\,p_{11}\end{bmatrix}.$$

    How to read it in this unit: $a,b$ are "probability I am in state 0 / state 1 now". Entry 0 of the answer is "(be in 0 and stay in 0) + (be in 1 and move to 0)", which is the total probability of being in 0 next step. Numerical check:

    $$\begin{bmatrix}0.5&0.5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0.9&0.1\\0.3&0.7\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0.45+0.15&\ \ 0.05+0.35\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0.6&0.4\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The answer still adds up to 1, as a PMF must.

    W2. Matrix × matrix

    Entry $(i,j)$ of $AB$ is row $i$ of $A$ dotted with column $j$ of $B$:

    $$(AB)_{ij}=\sum_k a_{ik}\,b_{kj}.$$

    If you only need one entry of $P^2$ (very common in exams), compute only that row-times-column; don't compute the whole matrix. Example:

    $$\begin{bmatrix}0.9&0.1\\0.3&0.7\end{bmatrix}^2_{\ (0,1)}=\underbrace{0.9}_{p_{00}}\times\underbrace{0.1}_{p_{01}}+\underbrace{0.1}_{p_{01}}\times\underbrace{0.7}_{p_{11}}=0.09+0.07=0.16.$$
    W3. Solving a linear system with a normalisation condition

    In §8 we solve equations like $\pi=\pi P$. These equations are always dependent (one of them is redundant), so on their own they only fix π up to a constant multiple. The missing information comes from "probabilities add to 1". Procedure: write the equations, throw one away, add $\sum_j\pi_j=1$, and solve. Example: $0.1\pi_0=0.3\pi_1$ and $\pi_0+\pi_1=1$:

    $$\begin{aligned} \pi_0&=3\pi_1 &&\why{from the first equation}\\ 3\pi_1+\pi_1&=1 &&\why{substitute into the normalisation}\\ \pi_1&=\tfrac14,\qquad \pi_0=\tfrac34. \end{aligned}$$
    W4. Geometric series and the derivative trick

    For $|\rho|\lt1$:

    $$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\rho^n=\frac{1}{1-\rho},\qquad \sum_{n=0}^{N}\rho^n=\frac{1-\rho^{N+1}}{1-\rho}\ \ (\rho\ne1).$$

    To get $\sum n\rho^n$, differentiate the first identity with respect to ρ and multiply by ρ:

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{d\rho}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\rho^n&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\rho^{n-1} &&\why{differentiate term by term}\\ \frac{d}{d\rho}\frac{1}{1-\rho}&=\frac{1}{(1-\rho)^2} &&\why{differentiate the closed form}\\ \Rightarrow\ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\rho^n&=\rho\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\rho^{n-1}=\frac{\rho}{(1-\rho)^2}. &&\why{multiply both by ρ} \end{aligned}$$

    These two sums give every M/M/1 formula in §12.

    W5. The exponential distribution (recap from Units 2 and 4)

    $T\sim$ Exp(λ): $f_T(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}$, $t\ge0$; $P[T\gt t]=e^{-\lambda t}$; mean $1/\lambda$. Three facts we use again and again:

    1. Memoryless: $P[T\gt s+t\mid T\gt s]=P[T\gt t]$. Having waited $s$ already doesn't change how much longer you wait. Proof: $\frac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}}=e^{-\lambda t}$.
    2. Small intervals: $P[T\le\Delta t]=1-e^{-\lambda\Delta t}=\lambda\Delta t+o(\Delta t)$. So "rate λ" means "probability ≈ λΔt of happening in the next tiny interval Δt".
    3. Competing clocks: if $T_1\sim$ Exp($\lambda_1$) and $T_2\sim$ Exp($\lambda_2$) are independent, then $\min(T_1,T_2)\sim$ Exp($\lambda_1+\lambda_2$) and $P[T_1\lt T_2]=\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}$.

    Here $o(\Delta t)$ ("little-o") means a term that is negligible compared with Δt, i.e. $o(\Delta t)/\Delta t\to0$; for example $(\Delta t)^2$.

    W6. The recursion $x_{n+1}=c\,x_n+d$

    Its fixed point is $x^*=\frac{d}{1-c}$ (solve $x^*=cx^*+d$). Subtract $x^*=cx^*+d$ from the recursion:

    $$\begin{aligned} x_{n+1}-x^*&=c\,(x_n-x^*)\\ \Rightarrow\ x_n-x^*&=c^n\,(x_0-x^*)\\ \Rightarrow\ x_n&=x^*+c^n\,(x_0-x^*). \end{aligned}$$

    If $|c|\lt1$ the gap shrinks geometrically and $x_n\to x^*$. The continuous-time twin: $y'(t)=-k\,y(t)+m$ has solution $y(t)=y^*+(y(0)-y^*)e^{-kt}$ with $y^*=m/k$. These solve the two-state chain in §6 and §9.

    1The Markov property

    Why this idea matters

    Think of a board game played with a die. To predict where your token will be after the next roll, you need to know only where it is now. How it got there (which rolls, in which order) is irrelevant. Or think of the number of people in a bank queue: the chance that it is 6 a minute from now depends on how many are there now, not on how the queue looked an hour ago. A process with this "only the present matters" property is called a Markov process (after the Russian mathematician A. A. Markov).

    Why it is so useful: in general, to compute probabilities about a process you need the joint PMF of all its values (Unit 4, §2), which is an enormous object. For a Markov process, the joint PMF breaks into a product of simple one-step pieces (§2), so the whole process is described by a small table of numbers, the transition matrix.

    Markov process / Markov chain (lecturer + Schaum)

    A discrete-time process $\{X_n,\ n=0,1,2,\dots\}$ is a Markov process if, for every $n$ and every choice of values,

    $$P\big[X_{n+1}=k_{n+1}\mid X_n=k_n,\ X_{n-1}=k_{n-1},\ \dots,\ X_0=k_0\big]=P\big[X_{n+1}=k_{n+1}\mid X_n=k_n\big].$$

    In words: given the present, the future is independent of the past. The lecturer calls this the Markov property or the memoryless property.

    If the state space (the set of values) is discrete, i.e. integer-valued, the Markov process is called a Markov chain. A discrete-time Markov chain is a DTMC.

    General form (continuous time, any values): $X(t)$ is Markov if for any times $t_1\lt t_2\lt\dots\lt t_k\lt t_{k+1}$,

    $$P\big[X(t_{k+1})\le x_{k+1}\mid X(t_k)=x_k,\dots,X(t_1)=x_1\big]=P\big[X(t_{k+1})\le x_{k+1}\mid X(t_k)=x_k\big].$$

    Reading the definition carefully. The left side conditions on the whole history; the right side conditions only on the latest value. Equality says "once you know $X_n$, learning $X_{n-1},\dots,X_0$ as well changes nothing". It does not say that $X_{n+1}$ is independent of $X_{n-1}$: the past does influence the future, but only through the present. The present is a sufficient summary of the past.

    Examples from the lecture

    Easy Binomial counting process (lecturer)

    Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be iid Bernoulli: $P[X_n=1]=p$, $P[X_n=0]=1-p$. Define the sum process (Unit 4, §10)

    $$S_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_k=S_{n-1}+X_n .$$

    Is $S_n$ Markov?

    Solution

    Key observation. $S_{n+1}=S_n+X_{n+1}$. The new increment $X_{n+1}$ is independent of $X_1,\dots,X_n$, and therefore independent of $S_1,\dots,S_n$ (which are built only from $X_1,\dots,X_n$).

    So given $S_n=k$, the next value is $k+1$ (if $X_{n+1}=1$) or $k$ (if $X_{n+1}=0$), whatever happened before:

    $$\begin{aligned} P[S_{n+1}=k+1\mid S_n=k]&=P[X_{n+1}=1]=p,\\ P[S_{n+1}=k\mid S_n=k]&=P[X_{n+1}=0]=1-p,\\ P[S_{n+1}=k\mid S_n=k-m]&=0\quad\text{for }m\ge2\ \text{or}\ m\lt0 &&\why{can only go up by 0 or 1} \end{aligned}$$

    and adding more history changes nothing:

    $$\begin{aligned} P[S_{n+1}=k\mid S_1=i_1,\ S_2=i_2,\dots,S_n=k]&=P[X_{n+1}=0\mid S_1=i_1,\dots,S_n=k]\\ &=P[X_{n+1}=0] &&\why{$X_{n+1}$ independent of the past}\\ &=1-p\\ &=P[S_{n+1}=k\mid S_n=k]. \end{aligned}$$

    So $S_n$ is a Markov chain on states $\{0,1,2,\dots\}$. Its state-transition diagram (lecturer) has one circle per state, an arrow $k\to k+1$ labelled $p$ and a self-loop labelled $1-p$:

    Theorem: independent increments ⇒ Markov

    Any process with independent increments is Markov. In particular every sum process $S_n=X_1+\dots+X_n$ of iid (or just independent) steps is Markov: the binomial counting process, the random walk, the Poisson process and the Wiener process are all Markov.

    Proof (discrete time). Write $S_{n+1}=S_n+X_{n+1}$. Conditioning on the whole history $S_0,\dots,S_n$ is the same as conditioning on $X_1,\dots,X_n$. Then

    $$\begin{aligned} P[S_{n+1}=j\mid S_n=i,\ S_{n-1},\dots,S_0]&=P[X_{n+1}=j-i\mid S_n=i,\ S_{n-1},\dots,S_0] &&\why{$S_{n+1}=j \Leftrightarrow X_{n+1}=j-i$ when $S_n=i$}\\ &=P[X_{n+1}=j-i] &&\why{the new step is independent of the history} \end{aligned}$$

    which depends only on the present value $i$. The same calculation without the older conditions gives the same answer, so the two conditional probabilities are equal. ∎

    Easy Random walk: transition matrix (lecturer)

    The random walk $W_n=W_{n-1}+D_n$ with $D_n=\pm1$ (probabilities $p$, $1-p$) is a sum process, so it is Markov. Draw its diagram and write its transition matrix.

    Solution

    From state $i$ the walk goes to $i+1$ with probability $p$ and to $i-1$ with probability $1-p$; it never stays put.

    The matrix is infinite; rows are the current state, columns the next state. Only the two entries next to the diagonal are non-zero:

    $$P=\begin{array}{c|ccccc} &\cdots&i-1&i&i+1&\cdots\\\hline \vdots&\ddots&&&&\\ i&\cdots&1-p&0&p&\cdots\\ \vdots&&&&&\ddots \end{array}$$

    The lecturer also notes: for consecutive times the joint PMF is easy (one step), but for times far apart we need the $n$-step transition probabilities, which is the subject of §4.

    A process that is not Markov (lecturer)

    Medium Two-term moving average (lecturer)

    Let $X_n$ be iid Bernoulli($p$) and $Z_n=\frac12\,[X_n+X_{n-1}]$. Is $Z_n$ a Markov process? (Take $p=\frac12$.)

    Solution

    Idea. $Z_n$ can be $0$, $\frac12$ or $1$. The value $\frac12$ is ambiguous: it means "one of $X_n,X_{n-1}$ is 1", without saying which. Knowing an older value of $Z$ can remove that ambiguity, and then the past tells us something the present does not. That breaks the Markov property. To prove it we exhibit one case where conditioning on more history changes the probability.

    Step 1: PMF of $Z_n$ (each $X$ is 0 or 1 with probability ½, independently):

    $$\begin{aligned} P[Z_n=0]&=P[X_n=0,\ X_{n-1}=0]=\tfrac14,\\ P[Z_n=1]&=P[X_n=1,\ X_{n-1}=1]=\tfrac14,\\ P[Z_n=\tfrac12]&=1-\tfrac14-\tfrac14=\tfrac12. \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2: condition on the present only. "$Z_n=1$ and $Z_{n-1}=\frac12$" means $X_n=X_{n-1}=1$ (for $Z_n=1$) and then $X_{n-2}=0$ (so that $Z_{n-1}=\frac12$):

    $$\begin{aligned} P[Z_n=1\mid Z_{n-1}=\tfrac12]&=\frac{P[Z_n=1,\ Z_{n-1}=\tfrac12]}{P[Z_{n-1}=\tfrac12]}\\ &=\frac{P[X_n=1,\ X_{n-1}=1,\ X_{n-2}=0]}{1/2}\\ &=\frac{(1/2)^3}{1/2}\\ &=\frac14 . \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3: condition on one more past value. Add $Z_{n-2}=1$, which means $X_{n-2}=X_{n-3}=1$. Then $Z_{n-1}=\frac12$ forces $X_{n-1}=0$, and so $Z_n=\frac12[X_n+0]\le\frac12$. Therefore

    $$P[Z_n=1\mid Z_{n-1}=\tfrac12,\ Z_{n-2}=1]=0 .$$

    Step 4: compare. $0\ne\frac14$. Knowing the extra past value $Z_{n-2}$ changed the conditional probability of the future, so $Z_n$ is not a Markov process.

    Why this happened. $Z_n$ is built from two consecutive $X$'s, so $Z_{n-1}$ and $Z_n$ share $X_{n-1}$. The present value $Z_{n-1}=\frac12$ does not reveal $X_{n-1}$, but $Z_{n-2}$ partly does. Rule of thumb: if the "state" you were given hides information that matters for the next step, the process is not Markov. (The pair $(X_n,X_{n-1})$, on the other hand, is a Markov chain with 4 states: enlarging the state can restore the Markov property.)

    Recipe: "Is this process Markov?"
    1. Try to write the next value as a function of the present value and fresh, independent randomness: $X_{n+1}=f(X_n,\ U_{n+1})$ with $U_{n+1}$ independent of everything before. If you can, the process is Markov. (Sum processes: $f(x,u)=x+u$. Running maximum: $f(x,u)=\max(x,u)$.)
    2. If you can't (the next value needs something the present value hides), look for a counterexample: find a present value $b$, a future value $c$ and a past value $a$ with $$P[X_{n+1}=c\mid X_n=b]\ \ne\ P[X_{n+1}=c\mid X_n=b,\ X_{n-1}=a].$$ Compute each side as (joint probability) ÷ (probability of the condition), expressing every event in terms of the underlying independent variables. Choose $a$ so that it pins down the hidden information; often one side becomes 0.
    3. Check that the conditioning event in step 2 has positive probability (otherwise the conditional probability is undefined).
    Three things the Markov property does NOT mean
    • Not independence. $X_{n+1}$ usually depends strongly on $X_n$ (and so indirectly on $X_{n-1}$). Independence would be the special case where it doesn't even depend on $X_n$ (the iid process, §3).
    • Not stationarity. The binomial counting process is Markov but its mean $np$ grows. A Markov chain is stationary only if it starts in its stationary distribution (§8).
    • Not "depends on one past value" in a loose sense. The condition is about conditional probabilities given the whole history; you must check that extra history adds nothing.

    2The joint PMF of a Markov chain

    The point of this section

    We prove the claim from §1: a Markov chain is completely described by (i) the PMF of its starting value and (ii) its one-step transition probabilities. Everything else in the unit (n-step probabilities, long-run behaviour, queues) is computed from these two ingredients.

    Derivation (lecturer). Start from the multiplication rule of Unit 1, $P[A\cap B]=P[A\mid B]\,P[B]$, applied with $A=\{X_n=k_n\}$ and $B=$ "all the earlier values":

    $$\begin{aligned} &P[X_n=k_n,\ X_{n-1}=k_{n-1},\ \dots,\ X_0=k_0]\\ &\quad=P[X_n=k_n\mid X_{n-1}=k_{n-1},\dots,X_0=k_0]\cdot P[X_{n-1}=k_{n-1},\dots,X_0=k_0] &&\why{multiplication rule}\\ &\quad=P[X_n=k_n\mid X_{n-1}=k_{n-1}]\cdot P[X_{n-1}=k_{n-1},\dots,X_0=k_0] &&\why{Markov property} \end{aligned}$$

    The second factor is the same kind of joint PMF, one step shorter. Repeat the same two steps on it, again and again, until only $X_0$ is left:

    $$\begin{aligned} &P[X_n=k_n,\ \dots,\ X_0=k_0]\\ &\quad=P[X_n=k_n\mid X_{n-1}=k_{n-1}]\cdot P[X_{n-1}=k_{n-1}\mid X_{n-2}=k_{n-2}]\cdots P[X_1=k_1\mid X_0=k_0]\cdot P[X_0=k_0]. \end{aligned}$$
    Joint PMF of a Markov chain $$P[X_0=k_0,\ X_1=k_1,\ \dots,\ X_n=k_n]=\underbrace{p_{k_0}(0)}_{\text{initial PMF}}\ \cdot\ \underbrace{p_{k_0k_1}\,p_{k_1k_2}\cdots p_{k_{n-1}k_n}}_{\text{one transition probability per step}}$$

    The $(n+1)$th-order joint PMF needs only (i) the initial state probabilities $P[X_0=k_0]$ and (ii) the one-step transition probabilities $P[X_{m+1}=k_{m+1}\mid X_m=k_m]$.

    How to use it: the probability of a particular path through the states is "probability of the starting state × the probability of each arrow you follow".

    Homogeneous chain (lecturer)

    If the transition probabilities do not depend on the time $m$,

    $$P[X_{m+1}=j\mid X_m=i]=p_{ij}\quad\text{for all }m,$$

    the chain is called homogeneous (or "has stationary transition probabilities"). $p_{ij}$ is the probability that, if the current state is $i$, the next state will be $j$. Every chain in this unit and in the exam papers is homogeneous.

    Careful with words: "stationary transition probabilities" ≠ "stationary process". A homogeneous chain's PMF $\mathbf p(n)$ usually still changes with $n$ (e.g. the binomial counting process). It is a stationary process only if it starts in its stationary distribution (§8).

    Lecturer's notation for multi-step probabilities.

    1. $P[X_0=j]=p_j(0)$: initial PMF.
    2. $P[X_n=j]=p_j(n)$: PMF of $X_n$.
    3. $P[X_n=k\mid X_m=j]=p_{jk}(m,n)$: the $(n-m)$-step transition probability ("from $j$ at time $m$ to $k$ at time $n$").
    4. For a homogeneous chain this depends only on the gap $r=n-m$: $p_{jk}(r)=P[X_{m+r}=k\mid X_m=j]$ for every $m$. The one-step case is $p_{jk}(1)=p_{jk}$.
    Easy A multi-step probability for the binomial counting process (lecturer)

    For the binomial counting process, find $p_{1,1}(2,5)=P[S_5=1\mid S_2=1]$.

    Solution

    To go from 1 (at time 2) to 1 (at time 5) the count must not increase during steps 3, 4 and 5. Each of those steps independently "does nothing" with probability $1-p$:

    $$p_{1,1}(2,5)=P[X_3=0,\ X_4=0,\ X_5=0]=(1-p)^3 .$$

    It depends only on the gap $5-2=3$: the chain is homogeneous.

    Easy Probability of a path

    A chain on $\{0,1\}$ has $p_{00}=0.9$, $p_{01}=0.1$, $p_{10}=0.3$, $p_{11}=0.7$ and $P[X_0=0]=P[X_0=1]=0.5$. Find $P[X_0=0,\ X_1=0,\ X_2=1,\ X_3=1]$.

    Solution

    The path is $0\to0\to1\to1$. Multiply the starting probability by one transition probability per arrow:

    $$\begin{aligned} P[X_0=0,X_1=0,X_2=1,X_3=1]&=p_0(0)\cdot p_{00}\cdot p_{01}\cdot p_{11}\\ &=0.5\times0.9\times0.1\times0.7\\ &=0.0315 . \end{aligned}$$

    Note that the order matters: $p_{01}$ (0 then 1) is not $p_{10}$.

    Common slip

    When a question gives the path in "reverse" order, e.g. $P(X_3=1,\ X_2=2,\ X_1=3)$, the path in time order is $3\to2\to1$. Re-order by time before multiplying: $P(X_1=3)\,p_{32}\,p_{21}$. (This exact trap appears in ES 2022 Q5c.)

    3Transition probability matrix & state-transition diagram

    Transition probability matrix (lecturer)

    For a homogeneous chain with states $0,1,\dots,m-1$, collect the one-step probabilities into an $m\times m$ matrix, row = present state, column = next state:

    $$P=\begin{bmatrix}p_{00}&p_{01}&\cdots&p_{0,m-1}\\p_{10}&p_{11}&\cdots&p_{1,m-1}\\\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\p_{m-1,0}&p_{m-1,1}&\cdots&p_{m-1,m-1}\end{bmatrix},\qquad p_{ij}=P[X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i].$$

    Its entries satisfy

    1. $p_{ij}\ge0$ for all $i,j$ (they are probabilities);
    2. $\displaystyle\sum_j p_{ij}=\sum_jP[X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i]=1$: every row sums to 1, because from state $i$ the chain must go somewhere (possibly back to $i$).

    A square matrix with these two properties is called a stochastic (or Markov) matrix. Columns need not sum to 1.

    State-transition diagram

    A picture of the same information: one circle (node) per state, and an arrow $i\to j$ labelled $p_{ij}$ whenever $p_{ij}\gt0$. A self-loop at $i$ carries $p_{ii}$. Arrows with probability 0 are not drawn.

    Check: the labels on the arrows leaving each node (including its self-loop) add to 1. That is the "row sums to 1" rule in picture form.

    4n-step transitions: the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation

    The question

    $P$ tells us what happens in one step. Exams ask "given the state today, what is the probability of state $j$ in 2 days? in 5 days?" The answer: multiply the matrix by itself. This section shows why.

    Two-step derivation (lecturer). Define $p_{ij}(2)=P[X_2=j\mid X_0=i]$. At time 1 the chain must be in some state $k$, so split over all possible $k$ (law of total probability):

    $$\begin{aligned} p_{ij}(2)&=P[X_2=j\mid X_0=i]\\ &=\sum_kP[X_2=j,\ X_1=k\mid X_0=i] &&\why{split over the state $k$ at time 1}\\ &=\sum_k\frac{P[X_2=j,\ X_1=k,\ X_0=i]}{P[X_0=i]} &&\why{definition of conditional probability}\\ &=\sum_k\frac{P[X_2=j\mid X_1=k]\ P[X_1=k\mid X_0=i]\ P[X_0=i]}{P[X_0=i]} &&\why{joint PMF of §2 (Markov property)}\\ &=\sum_kp_{ik}\,p_{kj}. &&\why{cancel $P[X_0=i]$} \end{aligned}$$

    Now compare with W2: $\sum_kp_{ik}p_{kj}$ is exactly row $i$ of $P$ times column $j$ of $P$, i.e. entry $(i,j)$ of $P\cdot P$. So the two-step matrix is

    $$P(2)=\begin{bmatrix}p_{00}(2)&\cdots&p_{0,m-1}(2)\\\vdots&p_{ij}(2)&\vdots\\p_{m-1,0}(2)&\cdots&p_{m-1,m-1}(2)\end{bmatrix}=P\cdot P=P^2 .$$
    Chapman–Kolmogorov equation

    The same argument with "$n$ steps, then $m$ steps" (split at the intermediate time $n$) gives

    $$p_{ij}(n+m)=\sum_kp_{ik}(n)\,p_{kj}(m),\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad P(n+m)=P(n)\,P(m).$$

    Applying it repeatedly with one-step pieces: $P(2)=P^2$, $P(3)=P(2)P=P^3$, and in general

    $$\boxed{\,P(n)=P^n\,}\qquad p_{ij}(n)=(P^n)_{ij}.$$

    Meaning: to go from $i$ to $j$ in $n+m$ steps, you must be somewhere ($k$) after $n$ steps; by the Markov property the two legs are independent given $k$, so multiply; then add over all possible $k$. $P^n$ is again a stochastic matrix (rows sum to 1), and $P^0=I$.

    Exam Bulb chain: r-step matrix (lecturer, with a correction)

    For the bulb chain of §3 find $P^2$, and then the $r$-step transition probabilities.

    Solution

    $P^2$ by multiplication (lecturer):

    $$P^2=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\p&q&0\\0&p&q\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\p&q&0\\0&p&q\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\p+pq&q^2&0\\p^2&2pq&q^2\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Entry (2,1), for example: row 2 $=[0,p,q]$ times column 1 $=[0,q,p]^T$ gives $0+pq+qp=2pq$.

    General $r$ by thinking, not multiplying. Each day on which a spare bulb exists, a bulb is needed with probability $p$, independently. So:

    • From 1, still at 1 after $r$ days ⇔ no bulb needed on any of the $r$ days: $p_{11}(r)=q^r$. Otherwise it reached 0: $p_{10}(r)=1-q^r$.
    • From 2, still at 2 ⇔ no bulb needed in $r$ days: $p_{22}(r)=q^r$.
    • From 2, at 1 after $r$ days ⇔ exactly one "need" among the $r$ days (binomial): $p_{21}(r)=\binom r1p\,q^{r-1}=rp\,q^{r-1}$.
    • From 2, at 0 ⇔ two or more needs: $p_{20}(r)=1-q^r-rp\,q^{r-1}$.
    $$P^r=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\1-q^r&q^r&0\\1-q^r-rpq^{r-1}&\ rpq^{r-1}&q^r\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Check with $r=2$: $1-q^2=(1-q)(1+q)=p(1+q)=p+pq$ ✓; $2pq$ ✓; $1-q^2-2pq=(1-q)^2=p^2$ ✓. All match the multiplied $P^2$.

    Note on the handwritten notes. The lecturer's $r$-step matrix writes $rq^{r-1}p$ for $p_{10}(r)$ and $\binom r2p^2q^{r-2}$ for $p_{20}(r)$. These are not right in general. $rq^{r-1}p$ is the probability of exactly one need in $r$ days, but from state 1 the chain reaches 0 after at least one need. Even at $r=2$ it gives $2pq$, while the multiplied $P^2$ has $p+pq$. Likewise $\binom r2p^2q^{r-2}$ (exactly two needs) is right at $r=2$ but not at $r=3$, where three needs also end in state 0. Use $1-q^r$ and $1-q^r-rpq^{r-1}$.

    5State probabilities: transient behaviour, $\mathbf p(n)=\mathbf p(0)P^n$

    The question

    The lecturer lists the typical questions asked about a chain: (1) its transient behaviour (the PMF at a particular time $n$), (2) its long-term behaviour ($n\to\infty$), (3) the PMF of $X_n$ or a joint PMF. For all of them you need (1) the PMF of $X_0$ and (2) the transition probabilities. Here is how they combine.

    Derivation (lecturer). Condition on the state one step earlier (total probability):

    $$\begin{aligned} p_j(n)&=P[X_n=j]\\ &=\sum_iP[X_n=j\mid X_{n-1}=i]\,P[X_{n-1}=i] &&\why{total probability over the previous state}\\ &=\sum_ip_i(n-1)\,p_{ij}. \end{aligned}$$

    The right side is the row vector $\mathbf p(n-1)$ times column $j$ of $P$ (W1). Doing this for every $j$ at once:

    $$\underbrace{\mathbf p(n)}_{1\times m}=\underbrace{\mathbf p(n-1)}_{1\times m}\ \underbrace{P}_{m\times m}.$$

    Repeat: $\mathbf p(n)=\mathbf p(n-2)P^2=\dots$

    PMF of the state at time n $$\mathbf p(n)=\mathbf p(n-1)\,P=\mathbf p(0)\,P^n .$$

    The PMF of an $m$-state DTMC at time $n$ is obtained from (1) the initial PMF $\mathbf p(0)$ and (2) $P^n$, the one-step matrix to the power $n$. If the chain starts surely in state $i$, $\mathbf p(0)$ is all zeros except a 1 in position $i$, and $\mathbf p(n)$ is just row $i$ of $P^n$.

    Always multiply the row vector on the left. $P\,\mathbf p^T$ is a different (wrong) calculation.

    Easy Speech chain, three steps

    Speech chain with α = 0.1, β = 0.3. The first packet is silent: $\mathbf p(0)=[1,\ 0]$. Find $\mathbf p(1)$, $\mathbf p(2)$, $\mathbf p(3)$.

    Solution $$P=\begin{bmatrix}0.9&0.1\\0.3&0.7\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Step by step, $\mathbf p(n)=\mathbf p(n-1)P$:

    $$\begin{aligned} \mathbf p(1)&=[1,\ 0]\,P=[0.9,\ 0.1]\\ \mathbf p(2)&=[0.9(0.9)+0.1(0.3),\ \ 0.9(0.1)+0.1(0.7)]=[0.84,\ 0.16]\\ \mathbf p(3)&=[0.84(0.9)+0.16(0.3),\ \ 0.84(0.1)+0.16(0.7)]=[0.804,\ 0.196] \end{aligned}$$

    The speech probability creeps up 0.1 → 0.16 → 0.196 → … towards 0.25. That limit is the stationary value $\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}=\frac{0.1}{0.4}$ (§6, §8). The values at finite $n$ are the transient distribution; the limit is the steady-state distribution.

    6The two-state chain in closed form

    Why a whole section

    Two-state chains appear in almost every end-sem paper (ES 2025 Q5c asks for exactly the formulas below; ES 2026 has three of them). For two states we can find $P^n$ and $\mathbf p(n)$ for every $n$ with one short calculation, and see the approach to equilibrium explicitly.

    Take states 0, 1 with $a=p_{01}$ (leave 0) and $b=p_{10}$ (leave 1):

    $$P=\begin{bmatrix}1-a&a\\b&1-b\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Step 1: a recursion for $x_n=P[X_n=1]$. From §5, $p_1(n+1)=p_0(n)\,p_{01}+p_1(n)\,p_{11}$, and $p_0(n)=1-x_n$:

    $$\begin{aligned} x_{n+1}&=(1-x_n)\,a+x_n(1-b)\\ &=a+(1-a-b)\,x_n . \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2: solve it with W6 ($c=1-a-b$, $d=a$). The fixed point is

    $$x^*=\frac{a}{1-(1-a-b)}=\frac{a}{a+b},$$

    so

    $$P[X_n=1]=\frac{a}{a+b}+(1-a-b)^n\Big(P[X_0=1]-\frac{a}{a+b}\Big),\qquad P[X_n=0]=1-P[X_n=1].$$

    Step 3: the matrix $P^n$. Row 0 of $P^n$ is $\mathbf p(n)$ when $X_0=0$ (put $P[X_0=1]=0$); row 1 is when $X_0=1$ (put $P[X_0=1]=1$):

    $$\begin{aligned} p_{01}(n)&=\frac{a}{a+b}-(1-a-b)^n\frac{a}{a+b}=\frac{a}{a+b}\big[1-(1-a-b)^n\big],\\ p_{11}(n)&=\frac{a}{a+b}+(1-a-b)^n\frac{b}{a+b}. \end{aligned}$$
    Two-state chain: n-step matrix $$P^n=\frac{1}{a+b}\begin{bmatrix}b&a\\b&a\end{bmatrix}+\frac{(1-a-b)^n}{a+b}\begin{bmatrix}a&-a\\-b&b\end{bmatrix}\qquad(a+b\gt0).$$

    Checks: $n=0$ gives $\frac1{a+b}\begin{bmatrix}a+b&0\\0&a+b\end{bmatrix}=I$ ✓; $n=1$ gives $P$ ✓ (entry (0,0): $\frac{b+a(1-a-b)}{a+b}=\frac{(a+b)(1-a)}{a+b}=1-a$).

    Long run: if $0\lt a+b\lt2$ then $|1-a-b|\lt1$, the second term dies out, and

    $$P^n\ \longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}\frac{b}{a+b}&\frac{a}{a+b}\\[2pt]\frac{b}{a+b}&\frac{a}{a+b}\end{bmatrix},\qquad \pi=\Big[\frac{b}{a+b},\ \frac{a}{a+b}\Big].$$

    Both rows tend to the same vector π: the chain forgets where it started. The speed of forgetting is the factor $|1-a-b|$ per step.

    Handy expanded form (no division): with $r=1-a-b$, $1-r^n=(a+b)(1+r+\dots+r^{n-1})$, so

    $$p_{01}(n)=a\,(1+r+r^2+\dots+r^{n-1}),\qquad p_{10}(n)=b\,(1+r+\dots+r^{n-1}).$$

    Special cases worth knowing.

    • $a+b=1$: then $r=0$ and $P^n=$ the limit already at $n=1$. Both rows of $P$ are equal: this is the iid process.
    • $a=b=1$: the chain flips deterministically $0,1,0,1,\dots$; $r=-1$ and $(−1)^n$ never dies out. $\mathbf p(n)$ oscillates and has no limit (a periodic chain, §7), although $\pi=[\frac12,\frac12]$ still satisfies $\pi P=\pi$.
    • $a=b=0$: the chain never moves; $P^n=I$; the limit depends on the start (reducible chain).

    A binary digit passes through a chain of identical noisy stages. At each stage a 0 is flipped to 1 with probability $a=0.1$ and a 1 is flipped to 0 with probability $b=0.2$. The input is 0 or 1 with probability 0.5 each. Find the distribution of the digit after $n$ stages, and as $n\to\infty$.

    Solution

    Here $a+b=0.3$, $r=1-a-b=0.7$, $\frac{a}{a+b}=\frac13$, $P[X_0=1]=0.5$. From Step 2:

    7Classification of states Beyond class notes

    Why classify?

    Before asking "what happens in the long run?" we must know which states the chain keeps coming back to and which it eventually leaves forever. In the bulb chain, states 2 and 1 are visited for a while and then abandoned; state 0 traps the chain. In the weather chain every state is visited again and again. These differences decide whether a long-run distribution exists, whether it is unique, and whether it depends on the starting state. Exams phrase this as "draw the state-transition diagram and comment on the states" (ES 2022 Q5c) or "classify the states".

    Everything in this section can be read off the diagram; you rarely need numbers.

    Accessibility, communication, classes

    Accessible, communicate, class, irreducible
    • State $j$ is accessible from state $i$, written $i\to j$, if $p_{ij}(n)\gt0$ for some $n\ge0$. On the diagram: you can walk from $i$ to $j$ following the arrows.
    • $i$ and $j$ communicate, written $i\leftrightarrow j$, if $i\to j$ and $j\to i$ (you can go there and come back).
    • Communication splits the states into disjoint communicating classes: groups in which every state can reach every other.
    • A class is closed if no arrow leaves it (once inside, the chain never gets out).
    • The chain is irreducible if all states communicate (only one class). Otherwise it is reducible.
    • State $i$ is absorbing if $p_{ii}=1$ (a closed class on its own).

    Why "classes" work: communication is reflexive ($i\leftrightarrow i$ with $n=0$), symmetric (by definition) and transitive. Transitivity follows from Chapman–Kolmogorov: if $p_{ij}(n)\gt0$ and $p_{jk}(m)\gt0$ then $p_{ik}(n+m)=\sum_lp_{il}(n)p_{lk}(m)\ge p_{ij}(n)\,p_{jk}(m)\gt0$.

    Recurrent and transient states

    Recurrent / transient (Schaum)

    Start the chain in state $j$ and let $f_{jj}=P[\text{the chain ever returns to }j\mid X_0=j]$.

    • $j$ is recurrent if $f_{jj}=1$: return is certain. Then it returns again, and again: it is visited infinitely often.
    • $j$ is transient if $f_{jj}\lt1$: there is a positive chance of never coming back. Then it is visited only a finite number of times (the number of returns is geometric with mean $\frac{f_{jj}}{1-f_{jj}}$).
    • A recurrent state is positive recurrent if the mean return time $E[T_j\mid X_0=j]$ is finite, and null recurrent if it is infinite (only possible with infinitely many states).

    Equivalent test: $j$ is recurrent ⇔ $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{jj}(n)=\infty$ (the expected number of visits is infinite).

    Easy Computing a return probability

    In the bulb chain, is state 1 recurrent or transient?

    Solution

    From 1 the chain either stays (probability $q$: it has "returned" at step 1) or moves to 0 (probability $p$), and from 0 it can never come back. So

    $$f_{11}=q\lt1\quad(\text{if }p\gt0).$$

    State 1 is transient. The same argument shows state 2 is transient ($f_{22}=q$), while state 0 is absorbing ($f_{00}=1$, recurrent).

    Facts that make classification quick
    1. Class property: all states in one communicating class are of the same type: all recurrent or all transient, and they all have the same period. So you only need to examine one state per class.
    2. Open class ⇒ transient. If an arrow leaves the class to somewhere the chain can't return from, there is a positive chance of leaving for ever.
    3. Finite chains: a class is recurrent if and only if it is closed, and then it is positive recurrent. Not all states of a finite chain can be transient (the chain has to be somewhere forever).
    4. Hence: a finite irreducible chain has all states positive recurrent.
    5. Infinite chains are subtler: the simple random walk is recurrent only when $p=\frac12$ (and then null recurrent); for $p\ne\frac12$ it drifts away and every state is transient.

    Periodicity

    Period, aperiodic, ergodic state

    The period of state $j$ is

    $$d(j)=\gcd\{\,n\ge1:\ p_{jj}(n)\gt0\,\},$$

    the greatest common divisor of all possible return times. If $d(j)=1$ the state is aperiodic; if $d(j)\gt1$ it is periodic (returns can only happen at multiples of $d$).

    • Self-loop ⇒ aperiodic. If $p_{jj}\gt0$, a return in 1 step is possible and the gcd is 1.
    • On the diagram: list the lengths of the cycles through $j$; the period is their gcd. Cycles of lengths 2 and 3 ⇒ gcd 1 ⇒ aperiodic.
    • A positive recurrent, aperiodic state is called ergodic. An irreducible chain whose states are ergodic is an ergodic chain.
    Recipe: "Draw the diagram and comment on (classify) the states"
    1. Draw the diagram from $P$ (arrow $i\to j$ for every $p_{ij}\gt0$).
    2. Find the classes: for each state, which states can it reach, and which can reach it back?
    3. Closed or open? Any arrow leaving the class ⇒ open ⇒ transient. Closed (finite) ⇒ positive recurrent. $p_{ii}=1$ ⇒ absorbing.
    4. Period of each recurrent class: self-loop anywhere in the class ⇒ aperiodic. Otherwise take the gcd of the cycle lengths.
    5. Summarise: "irreducible / reducible; states … are transient, … positive recurrent, aperiodic/periodic with period d; hence the chain is (not) ergodic and a unique limiting distribution does (not) exist."
    Medium Four chains to classify (adapted from Schaum 5.35)

    Classify the states of each chain.

    Solution

    (a) $P=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\\frac12&0&\frac12\\1&0&0\end{bmatrix}$. 0→1→0 and 0→1→2→0, so every state reaches every other: irreducible, finite ⇒ all positive recurrent. Cycles through 0 have lengths 2 (0→1→0) and 3 (0→1→2→0); $\gcd(2,3)=1$ ⇒ aperiodic. So the chain is ergodic.

    (b) 0→1→2→0 with probability 1 each. Irreducible, positive recurrent, but every return to 0 takes 3, 6, 9, … steps: period 3. Not ergodic; $\mathbf p(n)$ cycles for ever (unless it starts uniform).

    (c) 0⇄1⇄2. Irreducible, positive recurrent. Every move changes the "parity" (0 and 2 are even, 1 is odd, and each step goes even↔odd), so returns take an even number of steps: period 2. Same for the random walk.

    (d) $\{0,1\}$ communicate, but arrows leave to 2 and to 4 and never come back: an open class ⇒ transient. $\{2,3\}$ communicate and no arrow leaves: closed ⇒ positive recurrent; 3 has a self-loop ⇒ aperiodic. $\{4\}$: $p_{44}=1$, absorbing. The chain is reducible; where it ends up (class {2,3} or state 4) depends on luck and on the start.

    8Stationary & limiting distributions, ergodicity Beyond class notes

    The long-run question

    "In the long run, how often does he smoke filter cigarettes?" "In the long run, how often will the gambler win?" (ES 2026). These ask for the distribution of $X_n$ as $n\to\infty$, which (for a nice chain) no longer depends on the start. That limit is the solution of a simple linear system, $\pi=\pi P$. This section explains what π is, when $\mathbf p(n)\to\pi$, and why π is also the long-run fraction of time in each state (ergodicity).

    Stationary distribution

    A row vector $\pi=[\pi_0,\pi_1,\dots]$ is a stationary distribution (also: steady-state, equilibrium or invariant distribution) of the chain if

    $$\pi P=\pi,\qquad \pi_j\ge0,\qquad \sum_j\pi_j=1 .$$

    Component by component, $\pi P=\pi$ reads

    $$\pi_j=\sum_i\pi_i\,p_{ij}\qquad\text{for every }j\qquad(\text{π times column }j\text{ of }P).$$

    Why "stationary". If the chain starts with $\mathbf p(0)=\pi$, then $\mathbf p(1)=\pi P=\pi$, $\mathbf p(2)=\pi P=\pi$, and so on: the PMF never changes. Moreover every joint PMF $P[X_n=k_0,\dots,X_{n+r}=k_r]=\pi_{k_0}p_{k_0k_1}\cdots$ is then the same for every $n$, so the chain is a strict-sense stationary random process (Unit 4, §4). Started anywhere else, it is not stationary, but (for a nice chain) it becomes stationary in the limit.

    Balance interpretation. Subtract $\pi_jp_{jj}$ from both sides of $\pi_j=\sum_i\pi_ip_{ij}$ and use $\sum_kp_{jk}=1$:

    $$\underbrace{\pi_j\sum_{k\ne j}p_{jk}}_{\text{probability flow out of }j}=\underbrace{\sum_{i\ne j}\pi_i\,p_{ij}}_{\text{probability flow into }j}.$$

    In equilibrium, the probability flowing out of each state per step equals the probability flowing in. For two states this is simply $\pi_0\,a=\pi_1\,b$ ("flow 0→1 = flow 1→0").

    Limit theorem (finite chains)

    Let the chain be finite, irreducible and aperiodic (equivalently: regular, meaning some power $P^m$ has all entries $\gt0$). Then

    1. there is exactly one stationary distribution π, and every $\pi_j\gt0$;
    2. $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P^n=\begin{bmatrix}\pi\\\pi\\\vdots\\\pi\end{bmatrix}$ (all rows equal to π), so $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf p(n)=\pi$ for every initial $\mathbf p(0)$;
    3. $\pi_j=\dfrac{1}{E[T_j\mid X_0=j]}$: the stationary probability is the reciprocal of the mean return time (visit $j$ every 4 steps on average ⇒ $\pi_j=\frac14$).

    π is then called the limiting (steady-state) distribution.

    Ergodic theorem for Markov chains (syllabus: "ergodicity")

    For a finite irreducible chain (aperiodic or not), the fraction of time spent in state $j$ along a single long sample path converges to $\pi_j$ with probability 1:

    $$\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb 1\{X_n=j\}\ \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\ \pi_j .$$

    This is exactly the ergodicity idea of Unit 4, §7: a time average along one realisation equals the ensemble average (the probability $\pi_j$). It is why "in the long run, how often does he win?" is answered by π: π is both "the probability of winning a game far in the future" and "the long-run fraction of games won".

    When things go wrong
    • Periodic chain: a unique π exists and the time-averages converge to it, but $\mathbf p(n)$ itself oscillates and has no limit (e.g. the flip chain $a=b=1$: $\mathbf p(n)$ alternates $[1,0],[0,1],\dots$).
    • Reducible chain with two or more closed classes: many stationary distributions (any mixture of the classes' own ones), and the limit depends on the start (e.g. $P=I$).
    • Transient states always get $\pi_j=0$: in the long run the chain is never there.
    • Infinite chains may have no stationary distribution at all (random walk; M/M/1 with $\rho\ge1$, §12).
    Recipe: finding the stationary (long-run) distribution
    1. Check the chain is irreducible and aperiodic (diagram), so that π is also the limit. (Exams usually assume this.)
    2. Write one equation per state: $\pi_j=\sum_i\pi_ip_{ij}$, using column $j$ of $P$. Simplify each to "flow out = flow in" form.
    3. Discard one of them (they are dependent) and add $\sum_j\pi_j=1$.
    4. Solve: express all π's in terms of one of them, then normalise.
    5. Check one discarded equation, and that the π's are positive and add to 1.

    Two-state shortcut: $P=\begin{bmatrix}1-a&a\\b&1-b\end{bmatrix}$ ⇒ $\pi=\Big[\dfrac{b}{a+b},\ \dfrac{a}{a+b}\Big]$. Memory aid: the probability of a state is proportional to the rate of coming into it.

    Easy Speech chain in the long run

    What fraction of packets carry speech in the long run?

    Solution

    Balance between the two states: flow silence→speech $=\pi_0\alpha$, flow speech→silence $=\pi_1\beta$:

    $$\begin{aligned} \pi_0\,\alpha&=\pi_1\,\beta\\ \pi_0+\pi_1&=1\\ \Rightarrow\ \pi_1&=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta},\qquad\pi_0=\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}. \end{aligned}$$

    With α = 0.1, β = 0.3: $\pi_1=0.25$. One packet in four carries speech, which is why packet voice systems can multiplex about 4 calls per channel. (The simulated path above shows this: its running fraction of time in state 1 settles at 0.25.)

    Medium Weather in the long run

    Find the long-run proportion of sunny, cloudy and rainy days for the weather chain of §3.

    Solution

    All entries of $P$ are positive, so the chain is regular: π exists, is unique and is the limit. Equations from the columns of $P$:

    $$\begin{aligned} \pi_S&=0.6\pi_S+0.3\pi_C+0.2\pi_R &&\why{column S}\\ \pi_C&=0.3\pi_S+0.4\pi_C+0.3\pi_R &&\why{column C}\\ \pi_R&=0.1\pi_S+0.3\pi_C+0.5\pi_R &&\why{column R (we will discard this one)} \end{aligned}$$

    Simplify the first two:

    $$\begin{aligned} 0.4\pi_S&=0.3\pi_C+0.2\pi_R\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ 4\pi_S=3\pi_C+2\pi_R &&(1)\\ 0.6\pi_C&=0.3\pi_S+0.3\pi_R\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ 2\pi_C=\pi_S+\pi_R &&(2) \end{aligned}$$

    Use the normalisation $\pi_S+\pi_C+\pi_R=1$ together with (2):

    $$\begin{aligned} \pi_S+\pi_R&=2\pi_C &&\why{(2)}\\ 2\pi_C+\pi_C&=1 &&\why{substitute into the normalisation}\\ \pi_C&=\tfrac13,\qquad \pi_S+\pi_R=\tfrac23 . \end{aligned}$$

    Now (1) with $\pi_R=\frac23-\pi_S$:

    $$\begin{aligned} 4\pi_S&=3\cdot\tfrac13+2\big(\tfrac23-\pi_S\big)\\ 4\pi_S&=1+\tfrac43-2\pi_S\\ 6\pi_S&=\tfrac73\\ \pi_S&=\tfrac{7}{18},\qquad \pi_R=\tfrac23-\tfrac7{18}=\tfrac{5}{18}. \end{aligned}$$ $$\pi=\Big[\tfrac7{18},\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac5{18}\Big]\approx[0.389,\ 0.333,\ 0.278].$$

    Check the discarded equation: $0.1\cdot\frac7{18}+0.3\cdot\frac6{18}+0.5\cdot\frac5{18}=\frac{0.7+1.8+2.5}{18}=\frac5{18}=\pi_R$ ✓. Compare with the rows of $P^2$ in §4 (row S was $[0.47,0.33,0.20]$): they are drifting towards π. Mean time between rainy days: $1/\pi_R=3.6$ days.

    Medium Absorption: how long do the bulbs last? (first-step analysis)

    For the bulb chain, find the expected number of days until no spare bulbs are left, starting from 2.

    Solution

    The bulb chain is not irreducible: it is eventually absorbed in 0, so its stationary distribution is just $[1,0,0]$. The interesting question is how long absorption takes. First-step analysis: let $m_i$ = expected days to reach 0 from state $i$. Condition on what happens on the first day:

    $$\begin{aligned} m_0&=0\\ m_1&=1+q\,m_1+p\,m_0 &&\why{one day passes; then stay at 1 or move to 0}\\ \Rightarrow\ m_1(1-q)&=1\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ m_1=\tfrac1p\\ m_2&=1+q\,m_2+p\,m_1\\ \Rightarrow\ p\,m_2&=1+p\cdot\tfrac1p=2\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ m_2=\tfrac2p . \end{aligned}$$

    Sensible: each bulb lasts a geometric number of days with mean $1/p$, and there are two of them. The same "condition on the first step" idea gives absorption probabilities (e.g. gambler's ruin, Schaum 5.38).

    9Continuous-time Markov chains Beyond class notes

    From steps to clocks

    Customers don't arrive at a bank on the tick of a clock; they arrive at any instant. The number of customers $N(t)$ is a process in continuous time that jumps up or down at random instants. If its future depends only on its present value, it is a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC). Instead of "probability of moving per step" we now speak of "rate of moving per unit time", and the matrix $P$ is replaced by a rate matrix $Q$. The Poisson process (Unit 4) and every queue in §11–§13 are CTMCs.

    CTMC

    $\{X(t),\ t\ge0\}$ with a discrete state space is a (homogeneous) CTMC if for all $s,t\ge0$

    $$P\big[X(t+s)=j\mid X(s)=i,\ X(u)\text{ for }u\lt s\big]=P\big[X(t+s)=j\mid X(s)=i\big]=p_{ij}(t),$$

    a function of the elapsed time $t$ only. The matrix $P(t)=[p_{ij}(t)]$ satisfies $P(0)=I$ and the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation $P(t+s)=P(t)P(s)$ (same proof as §4, splitting at the intermediate time).

    How a CTMC moves: exponential holding times and rates

    Claim: the time a CTMC stays in a state before jumping (the holding time) must be exponential.

    Why. Let $T_i$ be the holding time in state $i$. Suppose the chain has already been in $i$ for $s$ time units. By the Markov property only the present state matters, not how long it has been there, so the remaining time has the same distribution as a fresh holding time:

    $$P[T_i\gt s+t\mid T_i\gt s]=P[T_i\gt t].$$

    That is the memoryless property, and the exponential is the only continuous distribution that has it (W5). So $T_i\sim$ Exp($\nu_i$) for some rate $\nu_i$.

    The structure of a CTMC
    1. In state $i$, wait an Exp($\nu_i$) time (mean $1/\nu_i$).
    2. Then jump to $j\ne i$ with probability $\tilde p_{ij}$ (the embedded jump chain, a DTMC with $\tilde p_{ii}=0$).

    Equivalently, there is an independent exponential "alarm clock" with rate $q_{ij}=\nu_i\tilde p_{ij}$ for each possible move $i\to j$; the first clock to ring decides the move (competing clocks, W5). The transition rates $q_{ij}$ are what we put on the diagram. For a small interval Δt:

    $$\begin{aligned} p_{ij}(\Delta t)&=q_{ij}\,\Delta t+o(\Delta t)\qquad(j\ne i)\\ p_{ii}(\Delta t)&=1-\nu_i\,\Delta t+o(\Delta t),\qquad \nu_i=\sum_{j\ne i}q_{ij}. \end{aligned}$$

    Generator (rate) matrix $Q$: off-diagonal entries $q_{ij}$, diagonal entries $q_{ii}=-\nu_i$, so every row of $Q$ sums to 0 (compare: every row of $P$ sums to 1).

    Kolmogorov forward equations (transient distribution)

    Let $p_j(t)=P[X(t)=j]$. To be in $j$ at time $t+\Delta t$, either be in $j$ at $t$ and not leave, or be in some $i\ne j$ and jump to $j$:

    $$\begin{aligned} p_j(t+\Delta t)&=p_j(t)\,(1-\nu_j\Delta t)+\sum_{i\ne j}p_i(t)\,q_{ij}\Delta t+o(\Delta t)\\ \frac{p_j(t+\Delta t)-p_j(t)}{\Delta t}&=-\nu_j\,p_j(t)+\sum_{i\ne j}p_i(t)\,q_{ij}+\frac{o(\Delta t)}{\Delta t}\\ p_j'(t)&=-\nu_j\,p_j(t)+\sum_{i\ne j}p_i(t)\,q_{ij}. &&\why{let Δt → 0} \end{aligned}$$

    In matrix form: $\boxed{\mathbf p'(t)=\mathbf p(t)\,Q}$. Its solution for given $\mathbf p(0)$ is the transient distribution.

    Steady state: if $\mathbf p(t)\to\pi$ then $\mathbf p'(t)\to0$, so

    $$\pi\,Q=0,\quad\sum_j\pi_j=1\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\underbrace{\pi_j\,\nu_j}_{\text{rate out of }j}=\underbrace{\sum_{i\ne j}\pi_i\,q_{ij}}_{\text{rate into }j}\ \ \text{for every }j .$$

    These are the global balance equations: in equilibrium, the long-run rate of leaving each state equals the rate of entering it. For an irreducible finite CTMC there is no periodicity problem: $\mathbf p(t)\to\pi$ always, and π is also the long-run fraction of time spent in each state.

    Medium Two-state CTMC: full transient solution

    A machine is up (state 0) or down (state 1). It fails at rate α and is repaired at rate β. Find $P[X(t)=0]$ for all $t$ and its limit.

    Solution $$Q=\begin{bmatrix}-\alpha&\alpha\\\beta&-\beta\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Forward equation for state 0 (leave at rate α, enter from 1 at rate β):

    $$\begin{aligned} p_0'(t)&=-\alpha\,p_0(t)+\beta\,p_1(t)\\ &=-\alpha\,p_0(t)+\beta\,(1-p_0(t)) &&\why{$p_1=1-p_0$}\\ &=-(\alpha+\beta)\,p_0(t)+\beta . \end{aligned}$$

    This is $y'=-ky+m$ with $k=\alpha+\beta$, $m=\beta$ (W6), so $y^*=\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}$ and

    $$P[X(t)=0]=\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}+\Big(p_0(0)-\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\Big)e^{-(\alpha+\beta)t}.$$

    Starting up ($p_0(0)=1$): $p_{00}(t)=\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}e^{-(\alpha+\beta)t}$.

    Steady state (check with balance: rate 0→1 = rate 1→0): $\pi_0\alpha=\pi_1\beta$ ⇒ $\pi_0=\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}$, the long-run availability of the machine.

    Numbers: failure rate α = 0.05 per hour (mean up-time 20 h), repair rate β = 0.5 per hour (mean repair 2 h). Availability $=\frac{0.5}{0.55}=\frac{10}{11}=0.909$. If it is up now, the probability it is up 2 hours later is $\frac{10}{11}+\frac1{11}e^{-1.1}=0.909+0.091(0.333)=0.939$.

    Compare with the discrete two-state chain (§6): there the gap shrank like $(1-a-b)^n$; here it shrinks like $e^{-(\alpha+\beta)t}$. Same picture, continuous clock.

    The Poisson process as a Markov chain

    The Poisson counting process $N(t)$ (Unit 4, §12) has independent increments, so it is Markov. It only ever jumps up by 1, at rate λ, from every state: a pure-birth CTMC.

    Deriving the Poisson PMF from the forward equations. Here $\nu_n=\lambda$ and $q_{n-1,n}=\lambda$, with $N(0)=0$:

    $$\begin{aligned} p_0'(t)&=-\lambda\,p_0(t)\\ p_n'(t)&=-\lambda\,p_n(t)+\lambda\,p_{n-1}(t),\qquad n\ge1 . \end{aligned}$$

    First, $p_0(t)=e^{-\lambda t}$ (since $p_0(0)=1$). For $n\ge1$ use the integrating factor $e^{\lambda t}$:

    $$\begin{aligned} e^{\lambda t}p_n'(t)+\lambda e^{\lambda t}p_n(t)&=\lambda e^{\lambda t}p_{n-1}(t)\\ \frac{d}{dt}\big[e^{\lambda t}p_n(t)\big]&=\lambda e^{\lambda t}p_{n-1}(t). &&\why{product rule, read backwards} \end{aligned}$$

    For $n=1$: the right side is $\lambda e^{\lambda t}e^{-\lambda t}=\lambda$, so $e^{\lambda t}p_1(t)=\lambda t$ (using $p_1(0)=0$), i.e. $p_1(t)=\lambda t\,e^{-\lambda t}$. Inductively, if $p_{n-1}(t)=\frac{(\lambda t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}e^{-\lambda t}$:

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\big[e^{\lambda t}p_n(t)\big]&=\lambda\frac{(\lambda t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\\ e^{\lambda t}p_n(t)&=\frac{(\lambda t)^n}{n!} &&\why{integrate from 0, $p_n(0)=0$}\\ p_n(t)&=\frac{(\lambda t)^n}{n!}e^{-\lambda t}. &&\why{Poisson(λt) ✓} \end{aligned}$$

    This is the "Definition 3" route promised in Unit 4: rare independent events at rate λ ⇒ Poisson counts. A pure-birth chain has no stationary distribution (it runs off to infinity), so here only the transient distribution is meaningful.

    10Birth–death processes Beyond class notes

    What it models

    Many systems change by one unit at a time: a customer arrives (+1) or one leaves (−1); a cell divides or dies; a call starts or ends. A CTMC that only moves to neighbouring states is a birth–death process. Its steady state can be found in closed form, and that solution is queueing theory.

    Birth–death process (Schaum §9.3)

    $N(t)\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}$ (think: number of customers in the system) is a birth–death process if it is a CTMC such that

    1. from state $n$ it can move only to $n+1$ or $n-1$ (from 0, only to 1);
    2. in state $n$, the probability of a transition to $n+1$ (a birth, an arrival) in $(t,t+\Delta t)$ is $\lambda_n\Delta t+o(\Delta t)$; $\lambda_n$ is the birth (arrival) rate;
    3. in state $n$, the probability of a transition to $n-1$ (a death, a departure) in $(t,t+\Delta t)$ is $\mu_n\Delta t+o(\Delta t)$; $\mu_n$ is the death (departure) rate, with $\mu_0=0$;
    4. the probability of two or more transitions in Δt is $o(\Delta t)$.

    The differential equations (Schaum 9.2)

    Let $p_n(t)=P[N(t)=n]$. At time $t+\Delta t$ the system is in state $n$ in one of three ways (anything else needs two transitions, probability $o(\Delta t)$):

    1. it was in $n$ at $t$ and nothing happened: probability $(1-\lambda_n\Delta t)(1-\mu_n\Delta t)=1-(\lambda_n+\mu_n)\Delta t+o(\Delta t)$;
    2. it was in $n-1$ and a birth happened: probability $\lambda_{n-1}\Delta t$;
    3. it was in $n+1$ and a death happened: probability $\mu_{n+1}\Delta t$.

    By the Markov property and total probability:

    $$\begin{aligned} p_n(t+\Delta t)&=p_n(t)\big[1-(\lambda_n+\mu_n)\Delta t\big]+p_{n-1}(t)\,\lambda_{n-1}\Delta t+p_{n+1}(t)\,\mu_{n+1}\Delta t+o(\Delta t)\\ \frac{p_n(t+\Delta t)-p_n(t)}{\Delta t}&=-(\lambda_n+\mu_n)p_n(t)+\lambda_{n-1}p_{n-1}(t)+\mu_{n+1}p_{n+1}(t)+\frac{o(\Delta t)}{\Delta t}. \end{aligned}$$

    Letting Δt → 0:

    Birth–death (Kolmogorov) equations $$\begin{aligned} p_n'(t)&=-(\lambda_n+\mu_n)\,p_n(t)+\lambda_{n-1}\,p_{n-1}(t)+\mu_{n+1}\,p_{n+1}(t),\qquad n\ge1,\\ p_0'(t)&=-\lambda_0\,p_0(t)+\mu_1\,p_1(t). \end{aligned}$$

    Steady state: the equilibrium equations and their solution

    In steady state $p_n(t)\to p_n$ and $p_n'(t)\to0$. Setting the derivatives to zero gives the steady-state equilibrium (balance) equations:

    Equilibrium equations $$\begin{aligned} (\lambda_n+\mu_n)\,p_n&=\lambda_{n-1}\,p_{n-1}+\mu_{n+1}\,p_{n+1},\qquad n\ge1,\\ \lambda_0\,p_0&=\mu_1\,p_1 . \end{aligned}$$

    Left: rate of leaving state $n$ (up or down). Right: rate of entering $n$ (from below by a birth, from above by a death). "Rate out = rate in", state by state.

    Solving them: the cut equations. Define $g_n=\lambda_np_n-\mu_{n+1}p_{n+1}$ (net flow upward across the gap between $n$ and $n+1$). Rearranging the equation for state $n$:

    $$\begin{aligned} \lambda_np_n-\mu_{n+1}p_{n+1}&=\lambda_{n-1}p_{n-1}-\mu_np_n\\ \text{i.e. }\ g_n&=g_{n-1}. \end{aligned}$$

    And the equation for state 0 says $g_0=\lambda_0p_0-\mu_1p_1=0$. So $g_n=g_{n-1}=\dots=g_0=0$ for every $n$:

    $$\lambda_n\,p_n=\mu_{n+1}\,p_{n+1}\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots$$

    Meaning: the chain can only cross the cut by single steps, and in the long run it crosses up exactly as often as down. Now apply it repeatedly:

    $$\begin{aligned} p_1&=\frac{\lambda_0}{\mu_1}p_0,\\ p_2&=\frac{\lambda_1}{\mu_2}p_1=\frac{\lambda_0\lambda_1}{\mu_1\mu_2}p_0,\\ &\ \,\vdots\\ p_n&=\frac{\lambda_0\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_{n-1}}{\mu_1\mu_2\cdots\mu_n}\,p_0 . \end{aligned}$$
    Birth–death steady-state solution $$p_n=p_0\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\lambda_k}{\mu_{k+1}},\qquad p_0=\Bigg[1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\lambda_k}{\mu_{k+1}}\Bigg]^{-1}$$

    (the second from $\sum_np_n=1$). A steady state exists only if the sum in the bracket is finite.

    Medium Discouraged arrivals

    Customers arrive at rate λ, but a customer who sees $n$ people already present joins only with probability $\frac1{n+1}$, so $\lambda_n=\frac{\lambda}{n+1}$. Service is at rate μ ($\mu_n=\mu$, $n\ge1$). Find the steady-state distribution and $L$, the mean number present.

    Solution

    With $\rho=\lambda/\mu$, the product is

    $$\begin{aligned} \prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\lambda_k}{\mu_{k+1}}&=\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\cdot\frac{\lambda/2}{\mu}\cdot\frac{\lambda/3}{\mu}\cdots\frac{\lambda/n}{\mu}\\ &=\frac{\rho^n}{n!}. \end{aligned}$$

    Normalise: $\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{\rho^n}{n!}=e^{\rho}$, so $p_0=e^{-\rho}$ and

    $$p_n=\frac{\rho^n}{n!}e^{-\rho}:\quad\text{Poisson}(\rho),\qquad L=\rho .$$

    A steady state exists for every ρ (the sum always converges): discouragement keeps the queue in check even if λ > μ.

    11Queueing systems: notation, quantities, Little's law Beyond class notes

    Customers arrive at random at average rate λ. If the server is free they are served at once; otherwise they wait in the queue. After service they leave. We want the average number of customers present and the average time they spend. Three things specify a queue: (1) the arrival process, (2) the service mechanism (number of servers, service-time distribution), (3) the queue discipline (we assume first-come first-served).

    Kendall notation A/B/s/K
    • A = arrival process: M (Markov) means Poisson arrivals, i.e. iid exponential inter-arrival times;
    • B = service-time distribution: M means exponential service times; D = deterministic; G = general;
    • s = number of servers;
    • K = capacity of the system (maximum number present, including the one in service). Omitted ⇒ unlimited.

    So M/M/1 = Poisson arrivals, exponential service, one server, unlimited room. M/M/1/N (also written M/M/1/K or M/M/1/M) = the same with room for at most N customers; arrivals that find the system full are turned away (lost). Examples of limited capacity: a telephone exchange with a fixed number of trunks, a hospital emergency room with limited beds, a parking lot, a router buffer.

    Performance measures
    $L$mean number of customers in the system (waiting + in service)
    $L_q$mean number waiting in the queue
    $W$mean time a customer spends in the system (waiting + service)
    $W_q$mean time a customer spends waiting in the queue
    $\rho=\lambda/\mu$traffic intensity (utilisation) = mean service time ÷ mean inter-arrival time

    Always: $W=W_q+\frac1\mu$ (time in system = wait + own service, mean service time $1/\mu$).

    Little's law $$L=\lambda_a\,W,\qquad L_q=\lambda_a\,W_q,\qquad L_s=\lambda_a\,W_s$$

    where $\lambda_a$ is the average rate at which customers actually enter the system ($\lambda_a=\lambda$ unless arrivals are turned away) and $L_s$, $W_s=\frac1\mu$ refer to the server alone.

    Why it's true (Schaum's "fee" argument). Suppose each customer pays 1 rupee per minute while in the system. The system collects money at an average rate of $L$ rupees per minute (that's how many are paying at any moment). On the other hand, customers enter at rate $\lambda_a$ per minute and each pays $W$ rupees on average. Two ways of counting the same income: $L=\lambda_aW$. Charging only while in the queue gives $L_q=\lambda_aW_q$.

    Why it's useful: it holds for almost any queue (any arrival process, any service distribution, any number of servers). Find $L$ from the state probabilities, then $W=L/\lambda_a$ for free.

    12The M/M/1 queue Beyond class notes

    Model

    Arrivals form a Poisson process with rate λ (mean arrival rate). Service times are iid exponential with parameter μ (mean service rate; mean service time $1/\mu$). One server, unlimited waiting room, first-come first-served. Let $N(t)$ = number of customers in the system at time $t$.

    Step 1: $N(t)$ is a birth–death process. Arrivals happen at rate λ whatever the state (Poisson: probability λΔt in Δt). If $n\ge1$ the server is busy and, by the memoryless property, the customer in service finishes in the next Δt with probability μΔt, however long they have been in service. So

    $$\lambda_n=\lambda\ \ (n\ge0),\qquad \mu_n=\mu\ \ (n\ge1).$$

    Step 2: steady-state probabilities. Put the rates into the birth–death solution:

    $$\begin{aligned} p_n&=p_0\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\lambda}{\mu}=p_0\Big(\frac\lambda\mu\Big)^n=p_0\,\rho^n . \end{aligned}$$

    Normalise using the geometric series (W4), which converges only if $\rho\lt1$:

    $$\begin{aligned} 1=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_n&=p_0\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\rho^n=\frac{p_0}{1-\rho}\\ \Rightarrow\ p_0&=1-\rho . \end{aligned}$$
    M/M/1 steady state ($\rho=\lambda/\mu\lt1$) $$p_n=P[N=n]=(1-\rho)\,\rho^n,\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots\quad(\text{geometric})$$
    • $p_0=1-\rho$ = probability the server is idle; $1-p_0=\rho$ = probability it is busy (utilisation). An arriving customer has to wait with probability ρ.
    • Tail: $P[N\ge k]=\sum_{n\ge k}(1-\rho)\rho^n=(1-\rho)\frac{\rho^k}{1-\rho}=\rho^k$.
    • If $\rho\ge1$ (customers arrive at least as fast as they can be served) there is no steady state: the queue grows without bound.

    Step 3: the mean number in the system, $L$.

    $$\begin{aligned} L=E[N]&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\,p_n\\ &=(1-\rho)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\rho^n\\ &=(1-\rho)\cdot\frac{\rho}{(1-\rho)^2} &&\why{derivative trick, W4}\\ &=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}=\frac{\lambda}{\mu-\lambda}. \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4: the mean number in the queue, $L_q$. With $n\ge1$ customers present, one is in service and $n-1$ wait; with $n=0$ nobody waits:

    $$\begin{aligned} L_q&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(n-1)\,p_n\\ &=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\,p_n-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_n\\ &=L-(1-p_0) &&\why{the second sum is $P[N\ge1]$}\\ &=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}-\rho\\ &=\frac{\rho-\rho(1-\rho)}{1-\rho}=\frac{\rho^2}{1-\rho}=\frac{\lambda^2}{\mu(\mu-\lambda)}. \end{aligned}$$

    ($L-L_q=\rho$ is the mean number in service, which is the probability the server is busy.)

    Step 5: mean times, by Little's law ($\lambda_a=\lambda$, nobody is turned away):

    $$\begin{aligned} W&=\frac{L}{\lambda}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{\lambda}{\mu-\lambda}=\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda},\\ W_q&=\frac{L_q}{\lambda}=\frac{\lambda}{\mu(\mu-\lambda)}=\frac{\rho}{\mu-\lambda}=W-\frac1\mu . \end{aligned}$$
    M/M/1 formula card $$\rho=\frac\lambda\mu,\quad L=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}=\frac{\lambda}{\mu-\lambda},\quad L_q=\frac{\rho^2}{1-\rho},\quad W=\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda},\quad W_q=\frac{\rho}{\mu-\lambda},\quad P[N\ge k]=\rho^k$$

    Extra The time a customer spends in the system is itself exponential with rate $\mu-\lambda$ (Schaum 9.6): $P[T\gt t]=e^{-(\mu-\lambda)t}$. Also $\Var(N)=\frac{\rho}{(1-\rho)^2}$.

    The most important lesson of queueing theory: $L$ and $W$ blow up as ρ → 1. A server that is busy 90% of the time has $L=9$; at 80% it has $L=4$. Running a system close to full capacity produces enormous queues, even though on average there is enough capacity.

    Recipe: M/M/1 numericals
    1. Get both rates in the same time unit. "One customer every 10 minutes" ⇒ λ = 1/10 per min. "Mean service time 8 minutes" ⇒ μ = 1/8 per min (rate = 1/mean time!).
    2. Compute $\rho=\lambda/\mu$ and check $\rho\lt1$.
    3. Use the formula card. Give times in sensible units.
    4. Sanity checks: $W-W_q=1/\mu$; $L=\lambda W$; $L-L_q=\rho$.

    13The M/M/1/N queue (finite capacity) Beyond class notes

    Model

    As M/M/1, but the system can hold at most $N$ customers. An arrival that finds $N$ present is turned away (blocked, lost). The number in the system is a birth–death process on $\{0,1,\dots,N\}$ with

    $$\lambda_n=\begin{cases}\lambda,&0\le n\lt N\\0,&n\ge N\end{cases}\qquad\qquad\mu_n=\mu,\quad1\le n\le N .$$

    Steady state. Exactly as for M/M/1, $p_n=\rho^np_0$, but now only for $n=0,1,\dots,N$. Normalise with the finite geometric sum (W4):

    $$\begin{aligned} 1=\sum_{n=0}^{N}p_n&=p_0\sum_{n=0}^{N}\rho^n=p_0\,\frac{1-\rho^{N+1}}{1-\rho}\qquad(\rho\ne1)\\ \Rightarrow\ p_0&=\frac{1-\rho}{1-\rho^{N+1}} . \end{aligned}$$

    If ρ = 1 every term is 1, the sum is $N+1$, and $p_0=\frac1{N+1}$.

    M/M/1/N steady state $$p_n=\frac{(1-\rho)\,\rho^n}{1-\rho^{N+1}}\ \ (\rho\ne1),\qquad p_n=\frac1{N+1}\ \ (\rho=1),\qquad n=0,1,\dots,N.$$
    • No condition ρ < 1 is needed: the sum is finite, so a steady state always exists. Excess customers are simply turned away.
    • Blocking probability (fraction of arrivals lost): $P_B=p_N$ (Poisson arrivals see the time-average state).
    • Effective arrival rate (customers who actually enter): $\lambda_a=\lambda(1-p_N)$. Equivalently, in steady state "rate in = rate out": $\lambda(1-p_N)=\mu(1-p_0)$, since the server completes jobs at rate μ whenever it is busy.

    Mean number in the system. Let $S(\rho)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\rho^n=\frac{1-\rho^{N+1}}{1-\rho}$. Then $\sum_{n=0}^{N}n\rho^n=\rho\,S'(\rho)$ (derivative trick), and $L=p_0\,\rho\,S'(\rho)=\rho\,S'(\rho)/S(\rho)$:

    $$\begin{aligned} S'(\rho)&=\frac{-(N+1)\rho^N(1-\rho)+(1-\rho^{N+1})}{(1-\rho)^2} &&\why{quotient rule}\\ L=\frac{\rho\,S'(\rho)}{S(\rho)}&=\frac{\rho\big[(1-\rho^{N+1})-(N+1)\rho^N(1-\rho)\big]}{(1-\rho)^2}\cdot\frac{1-\rho}{1-\rho^{N+1}}\\ &=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}-\frac{(N+1)\rho^{N+1}}{1-\rho^{N+1}}\qquad(\rho\ne1). \end{aligned}$$

    For ρ = 1: $L=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{n}{N+1}=\frac N2$. Note the first term is the M/M/1 value; the second is the reduction caused by turning customers away. As $N\to\infty$ with ρ < 1 the second term vanishes and we recover M/M/1.

    M/M/1/N formula card $$L=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}-\frac{(N+1)\rho^{N+1}}{1-\rho^{N+1}},\qquad L_q=L-(1-p_0),\qquad \lambda_a=\lambda(1-p_N),\qquad W=\frac{L}{\lambda_a},\qquad W_q=\frac{L_q}{\lambda_a}.$$

    Use $\lambda_a$, not λ, in Little's law: blocked customers never enter, so they contribute nothing to $L$.

    15Mistakes to avoid & self-check

    The classic mistakes
    1. Rows vs columns. In $P$ the rows sum to 1. In $\pi P=\pi$ the equation for $\pi_j$ uses column $j$. Writing $\pi_j=\sum_kp_{jk}\pi_k$ (row $j$) is wrong.
    2. Multiplying on the wrong side. $\mathbf p(n)=\mathbf p(0)P^n$ with $\mathbf p$ a row vector on the left.
    3. Reading a path backwards. $P(X_3=1,X_2=2,X_1=3)$ is the path 3 → 2 → 1 in time.
    4. Squaring entries instead of the matrix. $p_{ij}(2)\ne(p_{ij})^2$; it is a sum over intermediate states.
    5. Using all the balance equations and forgetting $\sum\pi=1$. The balance equations alone are dependent and only give ratios.
    6. Rate vs mean time. "Mean service time 8 min" means μ = 1/8 per min, not 8.
    7. Mixing time units (λ per hour with μ per minute).
    8. Forgetting ρ < 1 for M/M/1; or wrongly demanding it for M/M/1/N.
    9. Using λ instead of $\lambda_a$ in Little's law for M/M/1/N.
    10. Confusing $L$ with $L_q$ (and $W$ with $W_q$): "waiting for service" means in the queue; "spends in the shop" means in the system.

    A. The Markov property

    ES 2022 Q5b (5 marks) Define and explain Markov process. Consider the moving average of a Bernoulli sequence: $Y_n=\frac13(X_n+X_{n-1}+X_{n-2})$, where the $X_i$ are independent Bernoulli random variables with $p=\frac12$. Is $Y_n$ a Markov process?
    Solution

    Part 1: definition (model answer). A random process $X_n$ is a Markov process if, given its present value, its future is independent of its past:

    $$P[X_{n+1}=x_{n+1}\mid X_n=x_n,\ X_{n-1}=x_{n-1},\dots,X_0=x_0]=P[X_{n+1}=x_{n+1}\mid X_n=x_n]$$

    for all $n$ and all values (in continuous time: $P[X(t_{k+1})\le x\mid X(t_k)=x_k,\dots,X(t_1)=x_1]=P[X(t_{k+1})\le x\mid X(t_k)=x_k]$ for any $t_1\lt\dots\lt t_{k+1}$). The present state summarises all of the past that is relevant for predicting the future ("memoryless" property). If the state space is discrete it is a Markov chain. Consequence: the joint PMF factorises as $P[X_0=k_0,\dots,X_n=k_n]=P[X_0=k_0]\prod_{m=1}^np_{k_{m-1}k_m}$, so the process is fully described by its initial PMF and one-step transition probabilities. Examples: random walk, binomial counting process, Poisson process (all have independent increments). A Markov process need not be independent or stationary.

    Part 2: what to notice. $Y_n$ and $Y_{n-1}$ share two of the $X$'s ($X_{n-1}$ and $X_{n-2}$). The present value $Y_{n-1}$ tells us only the sum $X_{n-1}+X_{n-2}+X_{n-3}$, not which of them were 1. But which ones were 1 matters for $Y_n$, because $X_{n-3}$ drops out of the window and $X_{n-1},X_{n-2}$ stay. An older value $Y_{n-2}$ can reveal that hidden information. So we expect "not Markov", and we prove it with one counterexample (recipe in §1).

    Step 1: possible values and PMF of $Y_n$. The sum $X_n+X_{n-1}+X_{n-2}$ is Binomial(3, ½), so

    $$Y_n\in\Big\{0,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac23,\ 1\Big\}\quad\text{with probabilities}\quad\tfrac18,\ \tfrac38,\ \tfrac38,\ \tfrac18 .$$

    Step 2: condition on the present only. Compute $P\big[Y_n=1\mid Y_{n-1}=\tfrac23\big]$.

    • $Y_n=1$ ⇔ $X_n=X_{n-1}=X_{n-2}=1$.
    • Given that, $Y_{n-1}=\frac13(X_{n-1}+X_{n-2}+X_{n-3})=\frac13(2+X_{n-3})$, which equals $\frac23$ ⇔ $X_{n-3}=0$.
    $$\begin{aligned} P\big[Y_n=1,\ Y_{n-1}=\tfrac23\big]&=P[X_n=1,\ X_{n-1}=1,\ X_{n-2}=1,\ X_{n-3}=0]\\ &=\big(\tfrac12\big)^4=\tfrac1{16} &&\why{four independent X's}\\[4pt] P\big[Y_n=1\mid Y_{n-1}=\tfrac23\big]&=\frac{P[Y_n=1,\ Y_{n-1}=\frac23]}{P[Y_{n-1}=\frac23]}\\ &=\frac{1/16}{3/8}\\ &=\frac16 . \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3: add one past value. Now also condition on $Y_{n-2}=1$.

    • $Y_{n-2}=1$ ⇔ $X_{n-2}=X_{n-3}=X_{n-4}=1$.
    • Then $Y_{n-1}=\frac13(X_{n-1}+1+1)=\frac23$ ⇔ $X_{n-1}=0$.
    • So $Y_n=\frac13(X_n+X_{n-1}+X_{n-2})=\frac13(X_n+0+1)\le\frac23$. The value 1 is impossible.
    $$P\big[Y_n=1\mid Y_{n-1}=\tfrac23,\ Y_{n-2}=1\big]=0 .$$

    (The conditioning event has positive probability: $P[Y_{n-1}=\frac23,Y_{n-2}=1]=P[X_{n-1}=0,X_{n-2}=X_{n-3}=X_{n-4}=1]=\frac1{16}\gt0$, so this conditional probability is well defined.)

    Step 4: conclusion.

    $$P\big[Y_n=1\mid Y_{n-1}=\tfrac23,\ Y_{n-2}=1\big]=0\ \ne\ \tfrac16=P\big[Y_n=1\mid Y_{n-1}=\tfrac23\big].$$

    Knowing the extra past value $Y_{n-2}$ changes the conditional distribution of $Y_n$, so the Markov property fails: $Y_n$ is not a Markov process.

    Interpretation. "$Y_{n-1}=\frac23$" means two of the three bits in the window are 1. Alone, it leaves open which bit is 0. "$Y_{n-2}=1$" tells us the old bits $X_{n-2},X_{n-3}$ were 1, so the 0 must be the newest bit $X_{n-1}$, which stays in the window and blocks $Y_n=1$. This is the three-term version of the lecturer's two-term example (§1). A moving average of iid variables is never Markov for this reason. (The triple $(X_n,X_{n-1},X_{n-2})$, an 8-state chain, is Markov.)

    ES July 2024 Q5b (5 marks) Let $X_n$ be an iid random process. Show that $X_n$ is a Markov process and give its one-step transition probability matrix.
    Solution

    (The printed paper says "id-valued"; it means iid, independent and identically distributed.)

    What to notice. Independence is a much stronger property than the Markov property. If the next value is independent of everything before it, then in particular conditioning on the whole past or on the present alone gives the same answer: both equal the unconditional probability.

    Setting. Let $X_n$ take values in $\{0,1,2,\dots\}$ with common PMF $P[X_n=j]=a_j$ for every $n$ (identically distributed), and let $X_0,X_1,\dots$ be independent.

    Step 1: condition on the whole past.

    $$\begin{aligned} P[X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i,\ X_{n-1}=i_{n-1},\dots,X_0=i_0]&=\frac{P[X_{n+1}=j,\ X_n=i,\dots,X_0=i_0]}{P[X_n=i,\dots,X_0=i_0]}\\ &=\frac{P[X_{n+1}=j]\ P[X_n=i]\cdots P[X_0=i_0]}{P[X_n=i]\cdots P[X_0=i_0]} &&\why{independence: joint PMF = product}\\ &=P[X_{n+1}=j]=a_j . \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2: condition on the present only.

    $$\begin{aligned} P[X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i]&=\frac{P[X_{n+1}=j]\,P[X_n=i]}{P[X_n=i]} &&\why{independence}\\ &=a_j . \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3: compare. Both equal $a_j$, so

    $$P[X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i,\ X_{n-1},\dots,X_0]=P[X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i]$$

    and $X_n$ is a Markov process (in fact a homogeneous Markov chain, since $a_j$ doesn't depend on $n$). ∎

    Step 4: transition matrix. $p_{ij}=a_j$ for every $i$: the entry depends only on the column, so all rows are identical and equal to the PMF:

    $$P=\begin{bmatrix}a_0&a_1&a_2&\cdots\\a_0&a_1&a_2&\cdots\\a_0&a_1&a_2&\cdots\\\vdots&&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Example: iid Bernoulli($p$) gives $P=\begin{bmatrix}1-p&p\\1-p&p\end{bmatrix}$ (diagram in §3). Each row sums to $\sum_ja_j=1$ ✓.

    Remarks worth adding. (i) Since all rows equal $\mathbf a=[a_0,a_1,\dots]$, $P^n=P$ for every $n\ge1$ (row $i$ of $P^2$ is $\sum_ka_k\,\mathbf a=\mathbf a$). (ii) $\mathbf aP=\mathbf a$, so the PMF itself is the stationary distribution, and the chain is stationary from the start. (iii) The converse is false: Markov does not imply independent (e.g. a random walk).

    B. Discrete-time Markov chain numericals

    ES 2022 Q5c (5 marks) Consider the DTMC with 3 states $\{1,2,3\}$ and transition matrix $P=\begin{bmatrix}1/2&1/4&1/4\\1/3&0&2/3\\1/2&1/2&0\end{bmatrix}$. (i) Compute $P(X_3=1,X_2=2,X_1=3)$ if $P(X_1=1)=P(X_1=2)=\frac14$. (ii) Draw the state-transition diagram and comment on the states.
    Solution

    What to notice. (i) is a path probability. In time order the path is $X_1=3\to X_2=2\to X_3=1$. The chain starts (for this question) at time 1, so we need $P(X_1=3)$, which we get from the fact that the initial PMF sums to 1. Then multiply one transition probability per step (§2). (ii) is classification (§7).

    (i) Step 1: the missing initial probability.

    $$P(X_1=3)=1-P(X_1=1)-P(X_1=2)=1-\tfrac14-\tfrac14=\tfrac12 .$$

    Step 2: factorise using the Markov property.

    $$\begin{aligned} P(X_1=3,X_2=2,X_3=1)&=P(X_1=3)\cdot P(X_2=2\mid X_1=3)\cdot P(X_3=1\mid X_2=2,X_1=3)\\ &=P(X_1=3)\cdot P(X_2=2\mid X_1=3)\cdot P(X_3=1\mid X_2=2) &&\why{Markov property}\\ &=P(X_1=3)\cdot p_{32}\cdot p_{21}. \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3: read the entries. $p_{32}$ = row 3, column 2 = $\frac12$. $p_{21}$ = row 2, column 1 = $\frac13$.

    $$P(X_3=1,X_2=2,X_1=3)=\frac12\cdot\frac12\cdot\frac13=\frac1{12}\approx\mathbf{0.0833}.$$

    Check you didn't reverse it. The reversed path 1 → 2 → 3 would give $\frac14\cdot p_{12}\cdot p_{23}=\frac14\cdot\frac14\cdot\frac23=\frac1{24}$, a different number. Always order by time.

    (ii) Diagram. Arrows for every non-zero entry: 1→1 (½), 1→2 (¼), 1→3 (¼); 2→1 (⅓), 2→3 (⅔); 3→1 (½), 3→2 (½). There are no self-loops at 2 and 3 because $p_{22}=p_{33}=0$.

    Comments on the states.

    • Communication: 1→2 and 2→1 directly; 1→3 and 3→1 directly; so every pair communicates. One class $\{1,2,3\}$: the chain is irreducible.
    • Recurrence: a finite irreducible chain is closed, so all states are positive recurrent (no state is transient; none is absorbing, since no $p_{ii}=1$).
    • Period: state 1 has a self-loop ($p_{11}=\frac12\gt0$), so it is aperiodic; period is a class property, so all states are aperiodic. (Directly for state 2: 2→1→2 has length 2, 2→3→2 length 2, 2→1→1→2 length 3; gcd = 1.)
    • Hence every state is ergodic, the chain is ergodic, and a unique limiting distribution exists, independent of the start.

    Bonus (strengthens the "comment"): the long-run distribution. $\pi=\pi P$ column by column:

    $$\begin{aligned} \pi_1&=\tfrac12\pi_1+\tfrac13\pi_2+\tfrac12\pi_3\\ \pi_2&=\tfrac14\pi_1+\tfrac12\pi_3 &&(b)\\ \pi_3&=\tfrac14\pi_1+\tfrac23\pi_2 &&(c) \end{aligned}$$

    $$\begin{aligned} 12\pi_3&=3(4\pi_2-2\pi_3)+8\pi_2\\ 18\pi_3&=20\pi_2\ \Rightarrow\ \pi_3=\tfrac{10}{9}\pi_2\\ \pi_1&=4\pi_2-\tfrac{20}{9}\pi_2=\tfrac{16}{9}\pi_2\\ \pi_2\big(\tfrac{16}{9}+1+\tfrac{10}{9}\big)&=1\ \Rightarrow\ \pi_2=\tfrac{9}{35} \end{aligned}$$ $$\pi=\Big[\tfrac{16}{35},\ \tfrac{9}{35},\ \tfrac{10}{35}\Big]\approx[0.457,\ 0.257,\ 0.286].$$

    Check the first equation: $\frac12\cdot\frac{16}{35}+\frac13\cdot\frac9{35}+\frac12\cdot\frac{10}{35}=\frac{8+3+5}{35}=\frac{16}{35}$ ✓. In the long run the chain spends about 46% of its time in state 1.

    ES 2025 Q5c (5 marks) A 2-state homogeneous DTMC $X_n$ has transition matrix $P=\begin{bmatrix}1-p&p\\q&1-q\end{bmatrix}$. (i) Find the PMF of the DTMC at time 3 if the initial PMF vector is $[p_0\ \ p_1]$. (ii) Given that the DTMC is in state 0 at time 5, what is the probability that it will be in state 1 at time 8?
    Solution

    What to notice. This is the general two-state chain of §6 with $a=p$, $b=q$. (i) asks for $\mathbf p(3)=\mathbf p(0)P^3$; (ii) asks for a 3-step transition probability $p_{01}(3)$ (from time 5 to time 8, homogeneous ⇒ only the gap 3 matters). Rather than cubing a symbolic matrix blindly, derive the recursion; it gives a clean answer. To avoid a clash of letters, write the initial PMF as $[p_0,\ p_1]$ with $p_0+p_1=1$, and put $r=1-p-q$.

    (i) Step 1: one-step recursion for $x_n=P[X_n=1]$ (total probability over the previous state):

    $$\begin{aligned} x_{n+1}&=P[X_n=0]\,p_{01}+P[X_n=1]\,p_{11}\\ &=(1-x_n)\,p+x_n(1-q)\\ &=p+(1-p-q)\,x_n=p+r\,x_n . \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2: solve (W6). The fixed point is $x^*=\frac{p}{1-r}=\frac{p}{p+q}$, and

    $$x_n=\frac{p}{p+q}+r^n\Big(x_0-\frac{p}{p+q}\Big).$$

    Step 3: put $n=3$, $x_0=p_1$.

    $$\begin{aligned} P[X_3=1]&=\frac{p}{p+q}+(1-p-q)^3\Big(p_1-\frac{p}{p+q}\Big)\\ &=\frac{p+(1-p-q)^3\,(q\,p_1-p\,p_0)}{p+q} &&\why{$p_1(p+q)-p=qp_1-p(1-p_1)=qp_1-pp_0$}\\[4pt] P[X_3=0]&=1-P[X_3=1]=\frac{q-(1-p-q)^3\,(q\,p_1-p\,p_0)}{p+q}. \end{aligned}$$
    Answer (i) $$\mathbf p(3)=\Bigg[\ \frac{q+(1-p-q)^3(p\,p_0-q\,p_1)}{p+q}\ ,\ \ \frac{p-(1-p-q)^3(p\,p_0-q\,p_1)}{p+q}\ \Bigg]\qquad(p+q\gt0).$$

    Same thing as $\mathbf p(0)P^3$, if your examiner wants to see the matrix. From §6 with $a=p$, $b=q$:

    $$P^3=\frac1{p+q}\begin{bmatrix}q+p\,r^3&\ p-p\,r^3\\q-q\,r^3&\ p+q\,r^3\end{bmatrix},\qquad r=1-p-q,$$

    and

    $$\begin{aligned} \mathbf p(3)&=[p_0\ \ p_1]\,P^3\\ &=\frac1{p+q}\Big[\,p_0(q+pr^3)+p_1(q-qr^3)\ ,\ \ p_0(p-pr^3)+p_1(p+qr^3)\,\Big]\\ &=\frac1{p+q}\Big[\,q+r^3(pp_0-qp_1)\ ,\ \ p-r^3(pp_0-qp_1)\,\Big] &&\why{use $p_0+p_1=1$} \end{aligned}$$

    which matches. Numerical check with $p=0.2$, $q=0.3$ ($r=0.5$), starting in 0 ($p_0=1$): $P[X_3=1]=\frac{0.2-0.125(0.2)}{0.5}=0.35$. Direct: $\mathbf p(1)=[0.8,0.2]$, $\mathbf p(2)=[0.8(0.8)+0.2(0.3),\ 0.8(0.2)+0.2(0.7)]=[0.70,0.30]$, $\mathbf p(3)=[0.7(0.8)+0.3(0.3),\ 0.7(0.2)+0.3(0.7)]=[0.65,0.35]$ ✓.

    (ii) Homogeneity: $P(X_8=1\mid X_5=0)=P(X_3=1\mid X_0=0)=p_{01}(3)$. That is $P[X_3=1]$ from part (i) with $p_0=1$, $p_1=0$:

    $$\begin{aligned} P(X_8=1\mid X_5=0)&=\frac{p-(1-p-q)^3\,p}{p+q}\\ &=\frac{p}{p+q}\Big[1-(1-p-q)^3\Big]. \end{aligned}$$

    A division-free form: since $1-r^3=(1-r)(1+r+r^2)=(p+q)(1+r+r^2)$,

    $$P(X_8=1\mid X_5=0)=p\,\big[1+(1-p-q)+(1-p-q)^2\big].$$

    Check by paths ($n=3$, from 0 to 1): the expanded form says "leave 0 at some step and end in 1". With $p=0.2$, $q=0.3$: $0.2(1+0.5+0.25)=0.35$ ✓ (same as above).

    Interpretation. As the gap grows, $(1-p-q)^n\to0$ (if $0\lt p+q\lt2$) and $p_{01}(n)\to\frac{p}{p+q}=\pi_1$: three steps after time 5 the chain has already partly forgotten that it was in state 0.

    ES June 2026 Q5a (5 marks) A man's smoking habits are as follows. If he smokes filter cigarettes one week, he switches to nonfilter cigarettes the next week with probability 0.2.     Solution

    What to notice. The state is "what he smokes this week": F (filter) or N (nonfilter). Each row of $P$ is given by one number; the other entry is the complement. "In the long run, how often" = the stationary probability $\pi_F$ (by ergodicity it is both the long-run probability and the long-run fraction of weeks, §8).

    Step 1: transition matrix (order F, N):

    • From F: switch to N with 0.2, so stay with F with $1-0.2=0.8$.
    • From N: stay with N with 0.7, so switch to F with $1-0.7=0.3$.
    $$P=\begin{array}{c|cc}&F&N\\\hline F&0.8&0.2\\N&0.3&0.7\end{array}$$

    Step 2: the chain is regular (all entries positive), so a unique stationary distribution exists and is the long-run limit.

    Step 3: solve $\pi P=\pi$, $\pi_F+\pi_N=1$. Column F gives

    $$\begin{aligned} \pi_F&=0.8\,\pi_F+0.3\,\pi_N\\ 0.2\,\pi_F&=0.3\,\pi_N &&\why{flow F→N = flow N→F}\\ \pi_F&=1.5\,\pi_N . \end{aligned}$$

    C. Birth–death processes

    ES Nov–Dec 2024 Q4b(ii) Define Birth–Death process with state transition diagram and equilibrium equations.
    Solution (model answer)

    Definition. A birth–death process is a continuous-time Markov chain $N(t)$ on the states $\{0,1,2,\dots\}$ (e.g. the number of customers in a system) such that:

    1. from state $n$ the only possible transitions are to $n+1$ (a birth/arrival) or to $n-1$ (a death/departure); from 0 only to 1;
    2. $P[\text{transition }n\to n+1\text{ in }(t,t+\Delta t)]=\lambda_n\Delta t+o(\Delta t)$, where $\lambda_n$ is the birth rate in state $n$;
    3. $P[\text{transition }n\to n-1\text{ in }(t,t+\Delta t)]=\mu_n\Delta t+o(\Delta t)$, where $\mu_n$ is the death rate ($\mu_0=0$);
    4. the probability of more than one transition in Δt is $o(\Delta t)$.

    State-transition diagram:

    Time-dependent equations. With $p_n(t)=P[N(t)=n]$, considering the three ways to be in $n$ at $t+\Delta t$ (stay; birth from $n-1$; death from $n+1$) and letting Δt → 0:

    $$\begin{aligned} p_n'(t)&=-(\lambda_n+\mu_n)p_n(t)+\lambda_{n-1}p_{n-1}(t)+\mu_{n+1}p_{n+1}(t),\quad n\ge1\\ p_0'(t)&=-\lambda_0p_0(t)+\mu_1p_1(t). \end{aligned}$$

    Equilibrium (steady-state) equations. In steady state $p_n'(t)=0$ and $p_n(t)=p_n$:

    $$\boxed{\ (\lambda_n+\mu_n)\,p_n=\lambda_{n-1}\,p_{n-1}+\mu_{n+1}\,p_{n+1}\ \ (n\ge1),\qquad \lambda_0\,p_0=\mu_1\,p_1\ }$$

    Interpretation: for every state, rate of leaving = rate of entering.

    Solution. These reduce (by induction from the $n=0$ equation) to the cut equations $\lambda_np_n=\mu_{n+1}p_{n+1}$, so

    $$p_n=\frac{\lambda_0\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_{n-1}}{\mu_1\mu_2\cdots\mu_n}\,p_0,\qquad p_0=\Big[1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda_0\cdots\lambda_{n-1}}{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n}\Big]^{-1},$$

    provided the series converges. Example: M/M/1 has $\lambda_n=\lambda$, $\mu_n=\mu$, giving $p_n=(1-\rho)\rho^n$ with $\rho=\lambda/\mu\lt1$. The Poisson process is the pure-birth case ($\lambda_n=\lambda$, $\mu_n=0$).

    (Full derivation of the differential equations: §10.)

    D. Queueing theory: explain and derive

    ES 2021 Q5a (5 marks) In the M/M/1 queueing system, the arrival process is the Poisson process with rate λ (mean arrival rate) and the service time is exponentially distributed with parameter μ (mean service time). Derive the expression for (a) the average number of customers in the system and (b) the average number of customers waiting in the queue.
    Solution

    (The paper calls μ the "mean service time"; strictly, μ is the mean service rate and the mean service time is $1/\mu$. Say this in one line in your answer.)

    What to notice. The derivation has three stages: (1) model the number in the system as a birth–death process; (2) solve its equilibrium equations to get the geometric distribution $p_n=(1-\rho)\rho^n$; (3) compute the means with the geometric-series derivative trick. Present all three: the examiner wants to see where $p_n$ comes from.

    Step 1: the model. Let $N(t)$ be the number of customers in the system. Arrivals are Poisson(λ): in any short interval Δt an arrival occurs with probability $\lambda\Delta t+o(\Delta t)$, regardless of the state. When $N\ge1$ the server is busy; since service times are exponential (memoryless), the current service ends in Δt with probability $\mu\Delta t+o(\Delta t)$. So $N(t)$ is a birth–death process with

    $$\lambda_n=\lambda\ \ (n\ge0),\qquad\mu_n=\mu\ \ (n\ge1).$$

    Step 2: steady-state probabilities. The equilibrium equations are

    $$\begin{aligned} \lambda\,p_0&=\mu\,p_1,\\ (\lambda+\mu)\,p_n&=\lambda\,p_{n-1}+\mu\,p_{n+1},\quad n\ge1 . \end{aligned}$$

    From the first, $p_1=\rho\,p_0$ with $\rho=\lambda/\mu$. Subtracting the cut equation $\lambda p_{n-1}=\mu p_n$ (true for $n=1$) from the $n$th equation gives $\lambda p_n=\mu p_{n+1}$, so by induction

    $$p_{n+1}=\rho\,p_n\quad\Rightarrow\quad p_n=\rho^n\,p_0 .$$

    Normalising (needs $\rho\lt1$ for convergence):

    $$\begin{aligned} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\rho^np_0&=1\\ \frac{p_0}{1-\rho}&=1\ \Rightarrow\ p_0=1-\rho,\\ p_n&=(1-\rho)\rho^n,\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots \end{aligned}$$

    (a) Average number in the system.

    $$\begin{aligned} L&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\,p_n=(1-\rho)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\rho^n . \end{aligned}$$

    To evaluate $\sum n\rho^n$: differentiate $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\rho^n=\frac1{1-\rho}$ with respect to ρ to get $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\rho^{n-1}=\frac{1}{(1-\rho)^2}$, then multiply by ρ:

    $$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\rho^n=\frac{\rho}{(1-\rho)^2}.$$

    Therefore

    $$\begin{aligned} L&=(1-\rho)\cdot\frac{\rho}{(1-\rho)^2}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}=\frac{\lambda}{\mu-\lambda}} \end{aligned}$$

    (b) Average number waiting in the queue. If $n\ge1$ customers are present, one is being served and $n-1$ are waiting:

    $$\begin{aligned} L_q&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(n-1)\,p_n\\ &=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\,p_n-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_n\\ &=L-(1-p_0)\\ &=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}-\rho\\ &=\frac{\rho-\rho+\rho^2}{1-\rho}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{\rho^2}{1-\rho}=\frac{\lambda^2}{\mu(\mu-\lambda)}} \end{aligned}$$

    Add for completeness (by Little's law $L=\lambda W$, $L_q=\lambda W_q$): $W=\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda}$, $W_q=\frac{\lambda}{\mu(\mu-\lambda)}$, and $W=W_q+\frac1\mu$.

    Interpretation. $L-L_q=\rho$ is the average number in service (= probability the server is busy). Both $L$ and $L_q$ grow without bound as $\rho\to1$; the formulas hold only for $\rho\lt1$.

    ES 2022 Q5a (5 marks) Explain the following queueing models: (a) M/M/1 queueing system (b) M/M/1/N queueing system.
    Solution (model answer)

    Notation. In Kendall's notation A/B/s/K, A is the arrival process, B the service-time distribution, s the number of servers and K the system capacity (omitted = unlimited). "M" stands for Markov (memoryless): Poisson arrivals / exponential service times.

    (a) M/M/1.

    • Assumptions: Poisson arrivals with rate λ (inter-arrival times iid Exp(λ)); service times iid Exp(μ), mean $1/\mu$; one server; unlimited waiting room; first-come first-served.
    • Markov model: the number in the system $N(t)$ is a birth–death process with $\lambda_n=\lambda$, $\mu_n=\mu$. [Draw the diagram: states 0, 1, 2, … with λ arrows right and μ arrows left.]
    • Stability: traffic intensity $\rho=\lambda/\mu$ must be < 1 (the server must on average work faster than customers arrive), otherwise the queue grows without bound.
    • Steady state: from the balance equations $\lambda p_n=\mu p_{n+1}$: $p_n=(1-\rho)\rho^n$, $n\ge0$ (geometric). $p_0=1-\rho$ = idle probability; ρ = utilisation; $P[N\ge k]=\rho^k$.
    • Measures: $$L=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho},\quad L_q=\frac{\rho^2}{1-\rho},\quad W=\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda},\quad W_q=\frac{\rho}{\mu-\lambda},$$ related by Little's law $L=\lambda W$, $L_q=\lambda W_q$ and $W=W_q+1/\mu$.
    • Examples: a single bank teller, a single-CPU job queue, a phone booth.

    (b) M/M/1/N.

    • Assumptions: as M/M/1, but at most $N$ customers can be in the system (1 in service + $N-1$ waiting). An arrival that finds $N$ customers present is blocked (lost).
    • Markov model: birth–death on $\{0,1,\dots,N\}$ with $\lambda_n=\lambda$ for $n\lt N$, $\lambda_N=0$, and $\mu_n=\mu$. [Diagram: as M/M/1 but ending at state N.]
    • Steady state: $p_n=\rho^np_0$, $n=0,\dots,N$, with $$p_0=\frac{1-\rho}{1-\rho^{N+1}}\ (\rho\ne1),\qquad p_0=\frac1{N+1}\ (\rho=1).$$ No requirement ρ < 1: the state space is finite, so a steady state always exists.
    • Blocking probability $P_B=p_N$; effective arrival rate $\lambda_a=\lambda(1-p_N)$.
    • Measures: $$L=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}-\frac{(N+1)\rho^{N+1}}{1-\rho^{N+1}}\ \ (\rho\ne1),\ \ L=\frac N2\ (\rho=1);\quad L_q=L-(1-p_0);\quad W=\frac{L}{\lambda_a};\quad W_q=\frac{L_q}{\lambda_a}.$$
    • Examples: a telephone exchange with limited lines, a router with a finite buffer, a barber shop with a few chairs, a parking lot.
    • Relation: as $N\to\infty$ (with ρ < 1), M/M/1/N → M/M/1.

    Solution

    What to notice. This is the same question as ES 2022 Q5a; here the capacity is called $M$ instead of $N$ (don't confuse this $M$, a number, with the letter M for "Markov" in the first two positions). Write the ES 2022 Q5a answer with $N$ replaced by $M$. Compact version:

    M/M/1: Poisson(λ) arrivals, Exp(μ) service, 1 server, infinite capacity. Birth–death with $\lambda_n=\lambda$, $\mu_n=\mu$. For $\rho=\lambda/\mu\lt1$: $p_n=(1-\rho)\rho^n$; $L=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}$; $L_q=\frac{\rho^2}{1-\rho}$; $W=\frac1{\mu-\lambda}$; $W_q=\frac{\rho}{\mu-\lambda}$.

    ES 2021 Q5b ES Nov–Dec 2024 Q5c (5 marks) Customers arrive at a watch repair shop according to a Poisson process at a rate of 1 per every 10 minutes, and the service time is an exponential random variable with mean 8 minutes. (i) Find the average number of customers L, the average time a customer spends in the shop W, and the average time a customer spends in waiting for service $W_q$. (ii) Suppose that the arrival rate of the customers increases 10 percent. Find the corresponding changes in L, W and $W_q$.
    Solution

    What to notice. Poisson arrivals + exponential service + one repairman = M/M/1. Convert both statements into rates per minute: "1 every 10 minutes" ⇒ λ = 1/10; "mean service 8 minutes" ⇒ μ = 1/8 (a rate is 1/mean time). "Time in the shop" = time in the system (W); "time waiting for service" = time in the queue ($W_q$).

    (i) Step 1: rates and traffic intensity.

    $$\begin{aligned} \lambda&=\tfrac1{10}=0.1\ \text{per min},\qquad \mu=\tfrac18=0.125\ \text{per min}\\ \rho&=\frac{\lambda}{\mu}=\frac{1/10}{1/8}=\frac{8}{10}=0.8\lt1\ ✓ \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2: the measures.

    $$\begin{aligned} L&=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}=\frac{0.8}{0.2}=\mathbf{4\ customers}\\ W&=\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda}=\frac{1}{0.125-0.1}=\frac{1}{0.025}=\mathbf{40\ min}\\ W_q&=W-\frac1\mu=40-8=\mathbf{32\ min} \end{aligned}$$

    Checks: Little's law $L=\lambda W=0.1\times40=4$ ✓. Also $W_q=\frac{\rho}{\mu-\lambda}=\frac{0.8}{0.025}=32$ ✓, and $L_q=\lambda W_q=3.2$ (= $\frac{\rho^2}{1-\rho}=\frac{0.64}{0.2}$ ✓).

    Meaning: a customer typically waits 32 minutes for an 8-minute repair; the repairman is busy 80% of the time.

    (ii) Step 1: new arrival rate. A 10% increase: λ′ = 1.1 × 0.1 = 0.11 per min, μ = 0.125 unchanged:

    $$\rho'=\frac{0.11}{0.125}=0.88 .$$

    Step 2: new measures.

    $$\begin{aligned} L'&=\frac{0.88}{0.12}=7.33\ \text{customers}\\ W'&=\frac{1}{0.125-0.11}=\frac{1}{0.015}=66.67\ \text{min}\\ W_q'&=66.67-8=58.67\ \text{min} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3: the changes.

    beforeafter (+10% λ)change
    ρ0.800.88+10%
    L47.33+3.33 (+83%)
    W40 min66.67 min+26.67 min (+67%)
    $W_q$32 min58.67 min+26.67 min (+83%)

    Interpretation. A mere 10% rise in traffic nearly doubles the queue. This is the "explosion near ρ = 1" effect (§12 graph): $W=\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda}$ depends on the spare capacity $\mu-\lambda$, which fell from 0.025 to 0.015 per minute (−40%).

    ES Nov–Dec 2024 Q5b(i) A drive-in banking service is modelled as an M/M/1 queueing system with customer arrival rate of 2 per minute. It is desired to have fewer than 5 customers line up 99% of the time. How fast should the service rate be?
    Solution

    What to notice. "Fewer than 5 customers 99% of the time" ⇔ $P[N\ge5]\le0.01$. For M/M/1 the tail has the beautiful closed form $P[N\ge k]=\rho^k$, so the condition is one inequality in ρ, hence in μ.

    Step 1: the tail probability.

    $$\begin{aligned} P[N\ge5]&=\sum_{n=5}^{\infty}(1-\rho)\rho^n\\ &=(1-\rho)\,\rho^5\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\rho^m &&\why{put $n=5+m$}\\ &=(1-\rho)\,\rho^5\cdot\frac{1}{1-\rho}\\ &=\rho^5 . \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2: impose the requirement.

    $$\begin{aligned} \rho^5&\le0.01\\ \rho&\le(0.01)^{1/5}=10^{-0.4}=0.3981 . \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3: translate into μ (ρ = λ/μ with λ = 2 per min):

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{2}{\mu}&\le0.3981\\ \mu&\ge\frac{2}{0.3981}=\mathbf{5.024\ customers\ per\ minute}. \end{aligned}$$

    Meaning. The teller must serve at least about 5 customers a minute (mean service ≈ 12 s), i.e. be busy only ≈ 40% of the time. Keeping long queues rare needs a lot of spare capacity.

    If "line up" is read as "waiting in the queue" (not counting the one being served), "fewer than 5 waiting" ⇔ $N\le5$, so the condition becomes $P[N\ge6]=\rho^6\le0.01$, giving ρ ≤ 0.464 and μ ≥ 4.31 per min. Schaum (Prob. 9.8) uses the first reading (customers in the system), with answer 5.024.

    ES Nov–Dec 2024 Q5b(ii) A corporate computing centre has 2 computers of the same capacity. The jobs arriving at the centre are of two types, internal jobs and external jobs. These jobs have Poisson arrival times with rates 18 and 15 per hour respectively. The service time for a job is an exponential random variable with mean 3 minutes. Find the average waiting time per job when one computer is used exclusively for internal jobs and the other for external jobs.
    Solution

    What to notice. Because each computer serves only its own job type, the centre splits into two separate M/M/1 queues. "Waiting time" in queueing language means time in the queue, $W_q$ (we also give $W$). Work in minutes: mean service 3 min ⇒ μ = 1/3 per min for both computers.

    Internal jobs (computer 1).

    $$\begin{aligned} \lambda_1&=\tfrac{18}{60}=0.3\ \text{per min},\qquad \rho_1=\frac{0.3}{1/3}=0.9\\ W_{q1}&=\frac{\rho_1}{\mu-\lambda_1}=\frac{0.9}{\frac13-0.3}=\frac{0.9}{1/30}=\mathbf{27\ min}\\ W_1&=W_{q1}+3=30\ \text{min}\qquad(=\tfrac{1}{\mu-\lambda_1}=\tfrac{1}{1/30}\ ✓) \end{aligned}$$

    External jobs (computer 2).

    $$\begin{aligned} \lambda_2&=\tfrac{15}{60}=0.25\ \text{per min},\qquad \rho_2=\frac{0.25}{1/3}=0.75\\ W_{q2}&=\frac{0.75}{\frac13-\frac14}=\frac{0.75}{1/12}=\mathbf{9\ min}\\ W_2&=9+3=12\ \text{min}\qquad(=\tfrac{1}{1/12}\ ✓) \end{aligned}$$

    Average over all jobs. A randomly chosen job is internal with probability $\frac{18}{33}$ and external with probability $\frac{15}{33}$ (proportional to the arrival rates), so

    $$\begin{aligned} \overline{W_q}&=\tfrac{18}{33}(27)+\tfrac{15}{33}(9)=\frac{486+135}{33}=\frac{621}{33}\approx\mathbf{18.8\ min},\\ \overline{W}&=\tfrac{18}{33}(30)+\tfrac{15}{33}(12)=\frac{540+180}{33}\approx21.8\ \text{min}. \end{aligned}$$

    Answer: internal jobs wait 27 min, external jobs 9 min (Schaum 9.13(a) reports these two), about 18.8 min per job overall.

    Interpretation. The internal computer is 90% busy, so its jobs wait three times as long although its traffic is only 20% higher. Schaum's part (b) shows that if both computers serve both job types (one M/M/2 queue with λ = 33/hr) the average wait drops to about 6.4 min: pooling servers greatly reduces waiting. That is why banks use a single line for several tellers.
